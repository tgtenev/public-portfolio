## Abstract

This paper presents a non-expansionary mechanism for galactic redshift from a Young Earth Creationist (YEC) perspective. This working hypothesis is developed through three progressively complex conceptual models that have mathematical backing: the Small Young Luminous Shell (SYLS), the Large Young Luminous Shell (LYLS), and the Large Young Luminous Ball (LYLB). All models are based on a recently created static universe and incorporate the finite speed of gravity. The core idea is that in a newly created, static universe of finite size (not expanding from a singularity), the gravitational field has not yet reached its stable, long-term state. This temporary imbalance in the gravitational field causes a redshift that mimics accelerated expansion. We derive expressions for the redshift associated with each of the three models considered herein, and show that the LYLS and LYLB redshift-vs-distance relationships bear similarity with observations. In the process, we also derive a formula for the gravitational acceleration due to a spherical shell cap along its central axis.

## Background

Hubble [1] observed that distant astronomical objects show a redshift, where light spectra are shifted towards the red end, roughly proportional to their distance. He interpreted this as the Doppler effect of stellar recession with redshift $z$ given by $z \approx v/c$ where $v$ is galactic velocity and $c$ is the speed of light. This led to the Hubble relation,

$$v = H_0 x \quad \text{or} \quad z \approx \frac{H_0}{c}x = \frac{x}{R_H} \quad (1)$$

where $x$ is distance, $H_0$ is the Hubble constant, and $R_H$ is the Hubble radius (the Hubble constant interpreted as distance).

Modern mainstream cosmology interprets the observed redshift as the effect of the expansion of the universe, which lengthens light waves in transit [2]. However, recent observations of Type Ia supernovae [3][4] show that the relation in Equation (1) is not linear but has a slight downturn with distance, which is commonly understood to imply an accelerated expansion of the universe due to a hypothetical substance called dark energy. While the gravity of massive objects may also cause a nonlinear redshift, such effects cannot be used to explain Hubble's observation, because they do not apply on cosmological scales.

In this paper, we propose a novel cause of redshift that arises from the *ex-nihilo* creation of finite size matter (as opposed to a singularity) and the finite speed of gravity. Specifically, we show that in a recently created, inhomogeneous universe where the Distant Starlight Paradox (DSP) has a solution of a certain type, one would observe a redshift even without expansion. To this end, we build on the DSP solution presented by Tenev *et al.* [5] to show that redshift is a natural outcome of the setup described there. We offer this proposal as an additional tool for developing a YEC cosmology. While it does not necessarily contradict the concept of an expanding universe, it can be utilized alongside it to better explain astronomical observations without requiring the invocation of accelerated expansion or inflation.

Humphreys [6] recently proposed a non-expansionary redshift mechanism due to light's electromagnetic interaction with the luminiferous ether. However, the gravity-based mechanism presented here avoids the light scattering issue, a notable drawback in Humphreys' proposal.

## Method

Just like matter, we treat space as a created thing. We do this for convenience, because it lets us ascribe cosmological time $\tau = \tau(X)$ at every point $X$ in space representing the elapsed time measured by a stationary clock at $X$ since the creation event. Nevertheless, we expect that the ideas presented herein can also be formulated from a Machian perspective.

For simplicity, we assume an approximately flat space under the conditions of weak gravity. This simplification is largely justified by the Planck 2018 results [8] which, observing the acoustic peaks of the Cosmic Microwave Background, concluded that at very large scales the spatial curvature of the universe is roughly flat. This simplification allows us to remain within the domain of Special Relativity and make use of equations from Newtonian gravity.

We will consider several spherically symmetric setups of increasing complexity: Small Young Luminous Shell (SYLS), Large Young Luminous Shell (LYLS), and a Large Young Luminous Ball (LYLB). They all share one feature: center-bound light emitted from any point $X$ at time $\tau$ since the creation event at $X$ must reach the center point $O$ no later than time $\tau$ plus one day since the creation event at $O$. In other words, the Distant Starlight Paradox (DSP) has a solution within each setup, and such a solution is similar to the one described by Tenev *et al.* [5], or by Lisle [7].

The terms "shell" and "ball" refer to the shape of gravitating matter: for a shell, matter is distributed equidistantly from the center and has surface density $\sigma$, while for a ball, matter is distributed evenly within a sphere and has volume density $\rho$. The term "luminous" means that we consider light emitted from any point within the setup. This includes light emitted from the shell and from within its interior. We also construct the ball such that it is not opaque, but light from any distance can reach the center unobstructed. This is an idealization of a universe filled with point-like stars. Likewise, we allow for light to pass through the shell unobstructed.

## The Small Young Luminous Shell (SYLS)

Consider a static, massive, hollow shell of luminous matter, with radius $R$ equal to one light-day. This shell is "small" in the sense that it is much smaller than the size of the universe. We consider this setup first, because within it the DSP is trivially solved, and the redshift result we obtain is simplest.

We focus on a photon departing the shell at time $\tau$ since its creation. Let $\varepsilon = c\tau$. Because the photon's past causal region (or simply "causal region") expands at the same speed as the photon's, $\varepsilon$ remains constant, as illustrated in Figure 1. The causal region of some event $E$ is the region of space containing the worldlines of any objects that might have influenced $E$. Any intersection of $E$'s past light cone with a space-like hypersurface is a causal region of $E$. Here, of particular interest is the causal region that is the intersection of $E$'s past light cone with the creation hypersurface. Since $\delta\Omega$ is recently created, only a portion of it is included in the photon's causal region, which leads to the gravitational imbalance mentioned above.

We will calculate the energy loss $V_{\delta\Omega}$ of the photon as it arrives at the shell center in order to determine the resulting redshift $z_{\delta\Omega}$ per $z_{\delta\Omega} \approx V_{\delta\Omega}/c^2$. Let $g_{\delta\Omega}$ be the gravitational pull of the causal spherical cap at point $X$. Its value is given by Equation (A6) in Appendix A, and $V_{\delta\Omega}$ is obtained by integrating $g_{\delta\Omega}$ over the path of the photon from the shell's rim at $x = R$ to its central vicinity at $x = \varepsilon/2$. Once the photon is within $\varepsilon/2$ of the center, its causal region begins to include the entire shell and so per the Shell Theorem, the net gravity influence on the photon vanishes. Therefore,

$$
\begin{split}
z_{\delta\Omega} &\approx \frac{V_{\delta\Omega}}{c^2} = \frac{1}{c^2}\int_{\varepsilon/2}^R g_{\delta\Omega}\ dx \\
&= \frac{\pi \sigma G}{c^2}R\varepsilon\int_{\varepsilon/2}^R \frac{2x-\varepsilon}{x^2(R-x+\varepsilon)}\ dx \\

\end{split}
\tag{2}
$$

## The Large Young Luminous Shell (LYLS)

This arrangement ensures that a center-bound photon emitted at any point $X$ at creation time $\tau$ will arrive at $O$ at creation time $\approx \tau$. In fact, the photon arrives at each point $x_i$ along its trajectory at creation time $\approx \tau$ as measured at $x_i$. See Ref. [5] for more details on this setup.

Note that the hyperboloid shape of the creation surface does not represent spatial curvature. Rather, it corresponds to a peculiar creation-defined clock synchronization. Moreover, while light arrives at $O$ the same day local time as the day it left, gravity needs to propagate the full diameter of the shell before it stabilizes, resulting in redshift lasting for long ages.

## The Large Young Luminous Ball (LYLB)

We consider a LYLB $\Omega$ with radius $R = 3.38 R_H$ and derive expressions for the redshift-vs-distance relationship $z_\Omega(x)$. We treat the LYLB as consisting of concentric LYLS-s whose creation events are along the creation hypersurface depicted on Figure 3. To compute the LYLB redshift $z_\Omega = z_\Omega(x)$ for light coming from point $x$, we superimpose the contributions of the relevant LYLS-s of radii $R' \leq R$, which leads to the following expression:

$$
\begin{split}
z_\Omega(x) = \frac{\rho}{\sigma}\Bigg(&\int_{\varepsilon/2 \leq x \leq R'} z_{\delta\Omega}^{(1)}(x)\ dR' \\
&+ \int_{R' \leq x \leq R'+\varepsilon/2} z_{\delta\Omega}^{(2)}(x)\ dR' \\

\end{split}
\tag{5}
$$

## Discussion

We believe that the LYLS and LYLB models introduced here can be used as components of a larger Young Earth Creationist (YEC) cosmological model. The redshift plots for each model (Figure 4 and Figure 5) both show a monotonic increase of redshift with distance. Moreover, over the majority of the distance the plots appear near linear with a slight downward slope. This suggests that either model or some combination thereof might be useful in explaining the observed Hubble relation [1] without necessarily invoking recessionary motion or universal expansion, as mainstream cosmology does. For example, while LYLB can be used to model the matter within a young universe, the LYLS can be used to model the effects of the "waters above" (Psalm 148:4) as Humphreys [9] has speculated that these are literal waters forming a shell around the expanse of space. In this case, the Cosmic Microwave Background (CMB) may turn out to be the redshifted thermal radiation of such an outer shell.

We acknowledge that for a homogeneous, isotropic universe, Einstein's field equations forbid a simultaneously static, flat, and stable solution. However, a static, flat solution is possible with the appropriate cosmological constant. Although this solution is unstable over deep time (matter perturbations cause expansion or contraction) the universe can remain essentially static within the YEC timeframe. Our aim is not to reject universal expansion but to propose an additional, YEC-specific mechanism for the observed galactic redshift.

A possible objection to the inward-moving star-creation front model concerns the gravitational potential difference, which could be misinterpreted by a static analysis as causing a blueshift. However, as detailed in Appendix C, this argument is invalid because the model fundamentally depends on time-dependent gravity.

The vertical intercept $z_0$ of the LYLB redshift curve in Figure 5a (and similarly on the LYLS curve) indicates that LYLS and LYLB alone are likely insufficient to fully explain the observed Hubble relationship, and that one would need an additional model component. For example, one might speculate that Earth is located in a gravitational well, which slows timelapse at Earth compared to timelapse in the distant cosmos. All else being equal, an observer within such a gravitational well will see that distant stars are blueshifted, but in the context of a LYLB universe, such a blueshift will cancel out with the sharp redshift rise represented by the intercept $z_0$ in Figure 5a. The value of $z_0 = 1.5$ is equivalent to a time dilation factor of 2.5 times near Earth compared to the distant cosmos. The notion that time in the distant cosmos elapses several times faster than near Earth has also been suggested by Humphreys [10].

The applications of the LYLS and LYLB models along the lines described above might be developed in future work. Such work should involve the fitting of data from the Type Ia supernovae, which are used as standard candles to precisely measure redshift versus distance [3][4]. The parameters of the models that can be adjusted to fit the data would be: the age parameter $\varepsilon$, the radius of the universe $R$ (can be larger than that of the observable universe), and the densities $\rho$ or $\sigma$. For the model sketches above, we had chosen $\rho$ to be the density of the ISM, which might be too high as an average density of the universe. In future work, we might consider adjusting this density down while increasing the age parameter $\varepsilon$ to account for the near-Earth time dilation. Additionally, future work should consider incorporating manifold geometry from General Relativity for improved accuracy to account for additional effects of universal expansion and spatial curvature.

Model prediction: the dependence of redshift on the age parameter $\varepsilon$ is an interesting feature of the LYLS and LYLB models that is unique to the YEC presupposition. If these models were correct, we would expect that the Hubble parameter $H_0$ would increase over time by measurable amounts.

## Appendix A: Gravitational acceleration along the central axis of a shell cap of uniform mass density

For a spherical shell cap $\delta\Omega$ of mass $M$, and a point $X$ along the central axis at distance $x$ from the center of the sphere, find the gravitational acceleration $g_{\delta\Omega}$ at $X$ due to the shell cap.

The cap can be visualized as the revolution of the arc AA' around the central axis OB.](https://figures.tgtenev.com/tenev2026-non-expansionary-redshift/figA1-shell-cap-interior.png)

**Figure A1.** A spherical shell cap $\delta\Omega$ defined as the intersection of a spherical shell centered at point $O$ and a sphere of radius $a = R - x + \varepsilon$ such that $x = OX$ and where $\varepsilon = BE$ is the extension of the intersecting sphere beyond the rim of the shell. The cap can be visualized as the revolution of the arc $AA'$ around the central axis $OB$.

We will first consider the case when point $X$ is within the interior of the cap. Let the cap be defined as per Figure A1, and let $x = OX$, $a = XA = XA'$, $b = XB$. We observe that $a > b$ as long as $X$ is within the interior of the cap ($X$ is between $O$ and $B$). We divide the cap into infinitesimal concentric rings $d\delta\Omega$ of radii $r$, compute the gravitational acceleration at $X$ due to each ring, and then integrate over $l$ from $b$ to $a$, where $l$ is the distance from $X$ to the rim of each ring.

Let $h$ be the height from $X$ to the center of each ring $d\delta\Omega$ and let $dM$ be its mass. It is a well known result that the gravitational acceleration $dg_{\delta\Omega}$ due to such a ring is given by:

$$dg_{\delta\Omega} = G\frac{h}{\left(h^2+r^2\right)^{3/2}}dM \quad (A1)$$

where $G$ is the gravitational constant. From here on, we apply some basic geometry as follows:

$$
\begin{split}
h^2+r^2 &= l^2 \\
(x+h)^2+r^2 &= R^2 \\
&\Longrightarrow\quad h = \frac{1}{2x}\left(R^2 - x^2 - l^2\right)
\end{split}
\tag{A2}
$$

$$
\begin{split}
l^2 &= R^2+x^2-2xR\cos\theta \\
2l\ dl &= 2xR\sin\theta\ d\theta \\
&\Longrightarrow\quad R\sin\theta\ d\theta = \frac{l}{x}dl
\end{split}
\tag{A3}
$$

$$
\begin{split}
dM &= (2\pi r)\sigma R\ d\theta = 2\pi R^2\sin\theta\ d\theta \\
&\therefore\quad dM = 2\pi\sigma R\frac{l}{x}dl
\end{split}
\tag{A4}
$$

where $\sigma$ is the surface mass density of $\delta\Omega$. Combining Equations (A2) and (A4), we finally obtain:

$$
\begin{split}
dg_{\delta\Omega} &= \pi\sigma GR\frac{R^2-x^2-l^2}{l^2x^2}dl \\
&= \pi\sigma G\frac{R}{x^2}\left(\frac{R^2-x^2}{l^2}-1\right)dl \\
g_{\delta\Omega} &= \pi\sigma G\frac{R}{x^2}\int_{R-x}^{R-x+\varepsilon}\left(\frac{R^2-x^2}{l^2}-1\right)dl
\end{split}
\tag{A5}
$$

$$g_{\delta\Omega} = 2\pi\sigma G\frac{R\varepsilon}{x^2}\frac{x-\varepsilon/2}{R-x+\varepsilon}, \quad x \leq R \quad (A6)$$

Equation (A6) is the result we wanted. Let's check it against the well-known formula for the acceleration due to a semi-spherical shell at the center of the shell, and for an infinite slab at the surface of the slab.

When $x \rightarrow 0$ and $OA \perp OB$, we have the case of a semi-spherical shell which yields the expected result as follows:

$$
\begin{split}
x &\rightarrow 0 \\
OA &\perp OB \\
&\Longrightarrow\quad \varepsilon = \sqrt{x^2+R^2} - (R-x) \rightarrow x \\
&\therefore\quad g_{\delta\Omega} \rightarrow 2\pi\sigma G\frac{Rx}{x^2}\frac{x-x/2}{R-x+x} = \pi\sigma G
\end{split}
$$

In the case of an infinite slab:

$$
\begin{split}
x &= R \\
\varepsilon &\rightarrow 0 \\
&\Longrightarrow\quad g_{\delta\Omega} = 2\pi\sigma G\frac{1}{R}(R-\varepsilon/2) = 2\pi\sigma G
\end{split}
$$

In both cases, we obtain the correct well-known formulas, which confirms the result in Equation (A6).

Next, we will consider the case when point $X$ is in the exterior of the cap, as illustrated in Figure A2. As before, $x = OX$, $a = XA$, and $b = XB$. We can proceed along the same lines as Equations (A2)–(A6), except for a few sign changes. For example, in Equation (A2), $h$ figures with a negative sign, but when substituted back into Equation (A1), this sign is once again reverted to positive, in order to account for $dg_{\delta\Omega}$ pointing in the opposite direction from the interior case.

![A spherical shell cap δΩ defined as the intersection of a spherical shell centered at point O and a sphere of radius a = R − x + ε centered at point X, such that x = OX and ε = BE is the extension of the intersecting sphere beyond the rim of the shell.](https://figures.tgtenev.com/tenev2026-non-expansionary-redshift/figA2-shell-cap-exterior.png)

**Figure A2.** A spherical shell cap $\delta\Omega$ defined as the intersection of a spherical shell centered at point $O$ and a sphere of radius $a = R - x + \varepsilon$ centered at point $X$, such that $x = OX$ and $\varepsilon = BE$ is the extension of the intersecting sphere beyond the rim of the shell.

In the end, we arrive at the same integrand as Equation (A5), but with different limits, as follows:

$$g_{\delta\Omega} = -\pi\sigma G\frac{R}{x^2}\int_{-(R-x)}^{R-x+\varepsilon}\left(\frac{R^2-x^2}{l^2}-1\right)dl \quad (A7)$$

$$g_{\delta\Omega} = -2\pi\sigma G\frac{R(2R+\varepsilon)}{x^2}\frac{R-x+\varepsilon/2}{R-x+\varepsilon}, \quad R \leq x \leq R+\frac{\varepsilon}{2} \quad (A8)$$

Figure A3 plots $g_{\delta\Omega}$ for several values of $\varepsilon$ to more fully illustrate its behavior.

![The gravitational pull g as a function of the distance from the origin that is due to a spherical cap defined as per Figure A1. Several graphs are shown at different values of the extension ε.](https://figures.tgtenev.com/tenev2026-non-expansionary-redshift/figA3-shell-g.png)

**Figure A3.** The gravitational pull $g_{\delta\Omega} = g_{\delta\Omega}(x)$ as a function of the distance from the origin that is due to a spherical cap defined as per Figure A1. Several graphs are shown at different values of the extension $\varepsilon$.

As before, we can check the result in Equation (A8), to find out that when we set $x = R$ and $\varepsilon = 0$, Equation (A8) reduces to the equation for the gravity of an infinite slab, as one would expect. Likewise, when we set $x = R + \varepsilon/2$, we observe that $g_{\delta\Omega} = 0$ as expected, because that is when the intersecting sphere becomes tangential to the shell.

## Appendix B: Integrations and Closed Form Approximations for LYLB

We want to compute closed form solutions for the following piecewise integral sums:

$$
\begin{split}
S^{(1)}(x) &= \int_x^R z_{\delta\Omega}^{(1)}\ dR' + \int_{\varepsilon/2}^x z_{\delta\Omega}^{(2)}\ dR' \\
&= I_1^{(1)} + I_2^{(1)}, \quad \frac{\varepsilon}{2} \leq x \leq \varepsilon
\end{split}
\tag{B1a}
$$

$$
\begin{split}
S^{(2)}(x) &= \int_x^R z_{\delta\Omega}^{(1)}\ dR' + \int_{x-\varepsilon/2}^x z_{\delta\Omega}^{(2)}\ dR' + \int_{\varepsilon/2}^{x-\varepsilon/2} z_{\delta\Omega}^{(3)}\ dR' \\
&= I_1^{(2)} + I_2^{(2)} + I_3^{(2)}, \quad \varepsilon \leq x \leq R
\end{split}
\tag{B1b}
$$

where

$$
\begin{split}
I_1^{(2)} = I_1^{(1)} &\equiv 2\varepsilon \int_x^R \left[A(x) - \frac{R'(x-\varepsilon/2)}{x(R'+\varepsilon)}\right]dR' \\
I_2^{(1)} &\equiv 2\varepsilon \int_{\varepsilon/2}^x \left[A(x) - \frac{2R'-\varepsilon/2}{R'+\varepsilon} - \frac{2(R'+\varepsilon/2)^2(x-R')}{x\varepsilon(R'+\varepsilon)}\right]dR' \\
I_2^{(2)} &\equiv 2\varepsilon \int_{x-\varepsilon/2}^x \left[A(x) - \frac{2R'-\varepsilon/2}{R'+\varepsilon} - \frac{2(R'+\varepsilon/2)^2(x-R')}{x\varepsilon(R'+\varepsilon)}\right]dR' \\
I_3^{(2)} &\equiv 2\varepsilon \int_{\varepsilon/2}^{x-\varepsilon/2} \left[A\left(R'+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\right) - \frac{2R'}{R'+\varepsilon}\right]dr
\end{split}
\tag{B2}
$$

with $A(x) \equiv \dfrac{R'(R'+\varepsilon/2)}{(R'+\varepsilon)^2} \ln\left(\dfrac{x(2R'+\varepsilon)}{\varepsilon(|R'-x|+\varepsilon)}\right)$ in this Appendix, $R'$ being the running (integration) variable.

The closed form solution of the above integrals involves dilogarithms, which would make the result difficult to analyze. So, instead of computing the exact solutions, we compute closed form approximations by approximating the coefficient in front of the logarithmic term to eliminate the dependence on the integration variable $R'$. Specifically, we take:

$$\frac{R'\varepsilon(2R'+\varepsilon)}{(R'+\varepsilon)^2} \approx 2\varepsilon, \quad \varepsilon \ll R' \quad (B3)$$

Which yields very good approximations for the above integrals as follows:

$$
\begin{split}
I_1^{(2)} = I_1^{(1)} &\approx 2\varepsilon\left\lbrace(R-x)\left[\ln\left(\frac{x(2R+\varepsilon)}{\varepsilon(R-x+\varepsilon)}\right)-\frac{2x-\varepsilon}{2x}\right] + x\ln\left(\frac{2R+\varepsilon}{2x+\varepsilon}\right)\right\rbrace \\
&\quad + \varepsilon^2\ln\left[\frac{\varepsilon^2(2R+\varepsilon)}{(R-x+\varepsilon)^2(2x+\varepsilon)}\right] \\
&\approx 2\varepsilon x\left\lbrace\frac{R-x}{x}\left[\ln\left(\frac{x(2R+\varepsilon)}{\varepsilon(R-x+\varepsilon)}\right)-\frac{2x-\varepsilon}{2x} - \frac{\varepsilon}{2R-\varepsilon}\ln\left(\frac{2R+\varepsilon}{2\varepsilon}\right)\right] + \ln\left(\frac{2R+\varepsilon}{2x+\varepsilon}\right)\right\rbrace
\end{split}
\tag{B4}
$$

$$
\begin{split}
I_3^{(2)} &\approx -8\varepsilon x + 8\varepsilon^2 + 4\varepsilon^2\ln\left(\frac{2x+\varepsilon}{3\varepsilon}\right) + 2\varepsilon x\ln\left(\frac{4x^2}{3\varepsilon^2}\right) - 2\varepsilon^2\ln\left(\frac{4}{3}\right) \\
&\approx 2\varepsilon x\left(\ln\left(\frac{4x^2}{3\varepsilon^2}\right)-4\right) - 2\varepsilon^2\left(\ln\left(\frac{4}{3}\right)-4\right)
\end{split}
$$

We notice that since the limits of ${I_2}^{(1)}$ and ${I_2}^{(2)}$ are very narrow, those two integrals are approximately zero for all admissible values of $x$. Furthermore, it turns out that ${I_3}^{(2)}(x) \approx 0$ in the range of $\varepsilon/2 \leq x \leq \varepsilon$, which allows us to combine the two piecewise integral sums from Equation (B1) into a single piece as follows:

$$S^{(1)}(x) \approx S(x) \approx I_1^{(2)} + I_3^{(2)}, \quad \frac{\varepsilon}{2} \leq x \leq \varepsilon \quad (B5a)$$

$$S^{(2)}(x) \approx S(x) \approx I_1^{(2)} + I_3^{(2)}, \quad \varepsilon \leq x \leq R \quad (B5b)$$

Where we obtain finally:

$$
\begin{split}
S(x) = 2\varepsilon x\Bigg\lbrace&\left(\frac{R}{x}-1\right)\left[\ln\left(\frac{x(2R+\varepsilon)}{\varepsilon(R-x+\varepsilon)}\right)-\frac{2x-\varepsilon}{2x}\right] \\
&+ \ln\left(\frac{4x^2(2R+\varepsilon)}{3\varepsilon^2(2x+\varepsilon)}\right) - 4\Bigg\rbrace - \varepsilon^2\left[\ln\left(\frac{2R+\varepsilon}{6\varepsilon}\right)-4\right]
\end{split}
\tag{B6}
$$

In Equation (B6), which has the final result of the integral computation, we have added the last term to ensure that $S(\varepsilon/2) = 0$. We have verified by comparing with numerical computation for the integrals in Equation (B1) that Equation (B6) is indeed a very good approximation and that the two values converge as $\varepsilon/R \rightarrow 0$.

## Appendix C: Gravitational Potential Evolution and Photon Energy Loss

A potential objection to the inward-moving star-creation front model relates to the gravitational potential difference, which a static analysis might suggest causes a blueshift. This blueshift reasoning is based on treating gravitational potential as a state function: as mass accumulates, the potential at the center (reception) becomes much lower (more negative) than at the perimeter (emission). In a static field, a photon moving from higher to lower potential gains energy, implying it should arrive blueshifted.

The static conclusion described above is invalid because the gravitational potential in this model is time-dependent ($\partial\Phi/\partial t \neq 0$). The critical factor determining the frequency shift is not the scalar difference between endpoints, but the total work done by the photon against the field during its transit.

![A center-bound photon emitted at time T1 and position x1 climbs the inward-moving gravitational potential wave as a ball of matter is created ex nihilo from the outside in. The photon loses energy (is redshifted) by the time it reaches the center, despite the final potential at the center being lower than at x1.](https://figures.tgtenev.com/tenev2026-non-expansionary-redshift/figC1-potential-wave.png)

**Figure C1.** A center-bound photon (yellow dot) emitted at time $T_1$ and position $x_1$ climbs the inward-moving gravitational potential "wave" (green) as a ball of matter is created *ex nihilo* from the outside in (brown squares, following $T_1$ to $T_n$ in Einstein Synchrony Convention). The photon loses energy (is redshifted) by the time it reaches the center, despite the final potential at the center being lower than at $x_1$.

In a dynamic system, the energy change is derived by integrating the potential gradient along the photon's worldline. While the absolute potential at the center decreases as mass accumulates, the photon itself travels in tandem with the collapsing front (see Figure C1). Throughout its trajectory, the photon is located slightly "inside" the deepening potential well of the creation front. Consequently, the photon constantly encounters a positive potential gradient, effectively "climbing" out of the local depression created by the front, even as that depression moves inward. The integral of this gradient along the path yields a positive value, signifying that the photon expends energy to maintain its trajectory against the evolving geometry. This energy expenditure results in a redshift.

To intuitively visualize the distinction between the static and dynamic interpretations, consider the mechanics of a "stair climber" exercise machine (Figure C2). A user on such a machine may maintain a constant geometric elevation relative to the ground (analogous to the gravitational potential at the photon's position). However, because the steps are dynamically moving downward (analogous to the potential well moving inward), the user must constantly expend energy to climb.

This work done against the field manifests as a redshift, illustrated here by the athlete's exertion and the spectral color shift.](https://figures.tgtenev.com/tenev2026-non-expansionary-redshift/figC2-stairmaster-analogy.png)

**Figure C2.** The "Stairmaster" Analogy. The photon is shown climbing against the inward-moving potential front. Despite the deepening potential well, the dynamic geometry forces the photon to expend energy to maintain its trajectory. This work done against the field manifests as a redshift, illustrated here by the athlete's exertion and the spectral color shift.

In this model, the photon is the climber, and the center-bound front of the gravitational potential depression is the moving staircase. Although the "destination" (the floor beneath the machine) might be at a lower potential, the dynamic nature of the path requires the photon to climb a continuous gradient, resulting in a net loss of energy and a shift toward the red end of the spectrum.
