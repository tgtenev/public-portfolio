Introduction.

Among creationists, dark matter is often brought up in the context of rebutting the big bang model. But, in such a context, dark matter is actually tangential to the origins debate and needlessly controversial. The gravitational anomalies commonly attributed to dark matter, henceforth the dark-matter effect, have been readily observable for decades. Yet, in another context, the dark-matter effect is highly relevant. As shown in Tenev and Horstemeyer, the dark-matter effect is likely evidence for the macroscale structure of space, and therefore reveals design and purpose.

This is why I advocate for a creationeering perspective on dark matter. Horstemeyer coined the term to describe the combined application of engineering design principles and entrepreneurial purpose. Engineering creates functionality, and entrepreneurship creates value. God is therefore the greatest engineer and entrepreneur. Incorporating design and purpose into our scientific research will yield more fruit to society by which we glorify the Creator and demonstrate the authority of His Word.

Background.

Dark matter ideas date back to the 1920s and 1930s, but it wasn't until the 1970s and 1980s that dark matter became popular as it was used to explain anomalous rotational curves of galaxies and gravitational lensing. Only later, the big bang model co-opted dark matter to align big bang predictions with observations and provide an explanation for early structure formation. Therefore, as Faulkner admonishes, young-earth creationists should not view dark matter as merely a rescue device for the big bang. At the same time, all attempts to directly confirm the existence of dark matter have failed after decades of searching. Therefore, while we acknowledge the abundance of the observed dark-matter effect, we should look elsewhere for its explanation, not at dark matter.

In a recent paper advancing a non-dark-matter explanation for the dark-matter effect, Li argued that the dark-matter effect apparent in the Milky Way galaxy resulted from underestimating the gravitational effects of its non-spherical mass distribution. During the 1980s, Milgrom proposed an adjustment to Newton's second law of motion, also known as Modified Newtonian Dynamics, or MOND, to explain the larger-than-expected accelerations observed at galactic edges. A common challenge for these types of explanations is that they do not generalize well. Nevertheless, MOND may be a clue to a more satisfactory explanation as discussed below.

A clue from MOND: the dark-matter effect may be a geometric effect.

MOND postulates that in the regime of very small accelerations, where a is much less than a-naught, the force that caused a is no longer proportional to it, but to its square. The constant a-naught, which equals one point two times ten to the minus ten meters per second squared, also known as the MOND parameter, is determined from fitting the model to observations of galactic rotation curves. With this modification, MOND is able to explain the observed excess centripetal acceleration at the edges of galaxies. Not surprisingly, there are many outlier galaxies that do not fit MOND's predictions, but remarkably many do! A single-parameter model like MOND should not be able to fit the data so well unless the parameter has ubiquitous physical significance.

The MOND parameter a-naught behaves as a kind of a length-scale parameter, except that a-naught is acceleration and not length. However, there is a natural way to understand acceleration in geometric terms, see figure one, and show that a-naught is indeed a length-scale parameter in disguise.

A particle, the world line of which is illustrated, accelerates at point A with acceleration a equals c squared over r, where r is the radius of the tangent circle. Note that the radius must be calculated using a relativistic metric where time and space figure with opposite signs. For example, assuming nearly flat, or Minkowski, spacetime: r squared equals delta x squared minus c squared times delta t squared.

Thus, the length value r-naught corresponding to the MOND parameter is given by r-naught equals c squared over a-naught, which equals seven point four nine times ten to the twenty-six meters. This value is uncannily comparable to the Hubble radius r-sub-H, equal to one point three six times ten to the twenty-six meters, which is the distance to the edge of the observable universe, without adjusting for expansion. It appears that MOND's apparent success points to a cosmos-sized geometric effect, while MOND's misses may be due to second-order local, galaxy-sized geometric effects.

By way of analogy, consider the geometric effect of Earth's curvature on road construction planning: it only begins to matter at continent-sized projects. At the same time, local geometric effects due to the relief of the terrain contribute on a local scale and may enlarge or reduce the geometric effect of Earth's curvature. Could the dark-matter effect be likewise the result of superimposed local plus global geometric effects of physical space itself?

The dark-matter effect due to the inherent structure of space.

In Tenev and Horstemeyer, we demonstrated that a region of physical space with non-flat neutral curvature will amplify the gravity of the matter placed in it. We called this the inherent structure hypothesis explanation for the dark-matter effect. The inherent structure hypothesis is illustrated in figure two, where physical three-dimensional space is visualized as bending into an extra fourth spatial dimension, w. Per Newton's Law of Gravity, the gravitational acceleration at point P due to mass M-sub-B centred at point B should have been a-sub-P equals G times M-sub-B over l squared, where l equals the distance between B and P. However, we can show that the actual acceleration is greater, namely, equation one: a-sub-P equals G times M-sub-B over r squared, which is greater than G times M-sub-B over l squared, where r is the flat projection of l. In other words, in pre-curved space, gravity attenuates slower than the inverse square distance.

Here is an intuitive explanation for the modified inverse-square law in equation one that is simpler than the more rigorous derivation in reference one, by Tenev and Horstemeyer. Pretend that gravity is a force field, as per the Newtonian model, and imagine its flux lines emitting from a point source at B in figure two. The strength of the field at point P is proportional to the flux density at P, which, in turn, is inversely proportional to the surface of the sphere centred at B and passing through P. In figure two, which omits a spatial dimension, such a sphere can be visualized as a hoop through P that circles the dimple. In a flat space, the surface of said sphere would have been proportional to the square of the radius P B, which is how we get the familiar inverse-square law. But, in a positively curved space, as in figure two, the sphere will be smaller, and thus the flux density stronger. More precisely, the sphere surface through P is proportional to the flat projection of P B, that is r on the figure, which is how we get to equation one.

Taking a step back, here is the picture that emerges: there is a cosmos-sized curvature at the length-scale of the Hubble radius that amplifies tiny gravitational accelerations leading to a first-order dark-matter effect, which is what MOND captures. In addition, there are local, galaxy-sized spatial distortions that further amplify or reduce the cosmic-sized dark-matter effect. The superposition of the cosmic-sized and galaxy-sized spatial curvature form the inherent structure of space. The observed dark-matter effect is a manifestation of this inherent structure and not of some hidden mass.

What other evidence is there for space having structure? Figure three shows the large-scale structure of matter in the cosmos according to measurements from the Sloan Digital Sky Survey. One observes galactic matter shaped into filaments and sheets with huge voids between them. The size of these structures is too large for gravity alone to have influenced them. But folds and dimples comprising the inherent structure of space could in fact account for them. Likely, the large-scale structure is yet another manifestation of the inherent structure of space besides the dark-matter effect.

The creationeering perspective for dark matter and for all of cosmology.

Figure four, described in words. The figure is a photograph, taken from below against a pale, overcast sky, of a multistory steel building frame under construction. Columns, beams, and diagonal braces form an open lattice several floors high, with metal floor decking on the lower levels and no walls in place. We study conventional engineering artifacts through understanding of, first, the materials, second, the constitutive relations governing those materials, and third, the material structures. These three determine the behaviour of the artifact, which, in turn, points to its purpose. Hooke's Law is an example of a constitutive relationship between the stresses and deformation of a material that depends on the nature of the material. Furthermore, while the same constitutive law governs both a flat sheet of metal and a truss made of the same metal, these two differ in behaviour because their structures are different, see figure four.

In the same way, we should study the cosmos according to, first, its material-like elements, which are physical space and different types of matter; second, its constitutive relations, which are the laws of physics, like the equations of General Relativity; and third, its structures, which are discerned in the distribution of matter and curvature of space.

Why is this a reasonable perspective? It is evident that conventional matter can be shaped. We also know from Scripture, and more recently from General Relativity, that space, too, has measurable geometry that matter affects. While it is commonly presumed that the neutral shape of space, when no matter is present, is flat, this need not be the case. In fact, perfect flatness is a unique configuration for a material-like thing, and, as such, it is the exception, not the norm.

We need to see the cosmos as a work of engineering similar to, for example, how one looks at a building construction. This necessarily involves questions about purpose and design. What purpose is served by the inherent structure of space that is revealed in the large-scale structure and the dark-matter effect? Perhaps, just like in a house, structure is needed for stability, communications, transportation, and the support of life in general.
