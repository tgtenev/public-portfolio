Abstract.

In Consistent Young Earth Relativistic Cosmology, published in 2018, Phillip Dennis solves the distant-starlight problem entirely within general relativity, and in 2025 and 2026 he communicated substantial revisions of the model in private correspondence. This article presents the revised model in plain terms for a reader with an introductory background in relativity. The model has two ingredients. The first is the choice of solution: the created universe is a flat Friedmann-Lemaître-Robertson-Walker cosmology cut along a differently shaped initial surface, an asymptotically null hyperbola on which the entire observable universe was created within about 103,500 light years of Earth. Light from every depth then reaches Earth within a time b, equal to seven thousand years, of creation, the age of the earth. The second is the inflationary Dasha period, a miraculous episode of the creation week in which God advances cosmic time at different rates in different places, equivalently accelerates mechanical processes, so that the created galaxies are propelled outward through space to their present distances of billions of light years. Every observation since creation matches the standard cosmology exactly. The article incorporates Dennis's corrections through August 2026 and was reviewed by him.

Section 1. Introduction.

In Consistent Young Earth Relativistic Cosmology, Phillip Dennis, 2018, offers a solution to the distant-starlight problem built entirely inside general relativity. Since publication, Dennis has continued to develop the model, and in 2025 and 2026 he communicated some revisions privately which are captured here in plain terms. Everything herein is Dennis's and is intended to represent his views; only the pictures, a few connecting derivations detailed in Section 11, and the expository glue are new. The reader should know from the outset that this account goes beyond the published record.

The problem the model solves is distant starlight. The observable universe is roughly 92 billion light years across, and on the ordinary account light from the most distant galaxies we can see has been travelling for something like 13.8 billion years to reach us. If the universe is a few thousand years old, none of that light should have arrived, and nothing beyond a few thousand light years should be visible at all; yet we see galaxies at billions of light years. In the 2018 paper that was the only problem Dennis set out to solve. The 2025 materials broaden the scope, asking also how old the objects in the sky should appear, which is taken up in Section 8.

Earlier creationist attempts, he notes, either changed the speed of light, or supposed the light was created already in flight, or invoked strong gravitational time dilation. Each ran into trouble elsewhere; Faulkner, 2013, surveys the difficulties. Dennis's approach accepts the standard physics and asks instead what the initial conditions were and how the creation week ran. In its current form, he observes, the model has come to sit remarkably close to the standard picture: it now contains a clearly stated inflationary period, arrived at from the biblical account rather than from what he regards as the standard model's atheistic initial conditions.

The model as it now stands has two ingredients rather than one.

The first is the choice of solution. General relativity's equations admit many universes, and the created universe is taken to be one that begins on a differently shaped initial surface than the standard cosmology assumes. This ingredient adds no new physics: nothing about gravity changes, and nothing about the locally measured speed of light.

The second ingredient, which the 2018 paper stated in its abstract but left largely implicit in its equations, and which Dennis now names explicitly, is the inflationary Dasha period: a miraculous episode during the creation week in which God advances cosmic time at different rates in different places, spreading the created galaxies outward from a beginning that fit within about a hundred thousand light years of Earth. The advancement is expressed through quantities that general relativity itself leaves free, so it too breaks no equation of the theory. However, it is a specification of how the cosmic clocks were run during the week, and the model cannot be described without it. Section 7 below is devoted to it.

A reader who has had a semester of modern physics, or a good course in special relativity, should be able to follow all of it. The general-relativistic tools the argument uses, chief among them the freedom to cut solutions apart and stitch them together, are genuinely general relativity rather than anything a special-relativity course supplies, but they are explained as they arise; no prior course in the subject is needed.

Finally, a note on sources. The base of this article is the published 2018 paper. Everything dated 2025 and later comes from personal communication from Dennis: a quantitative note of September 2025, two explanatory slides, two rounds of annotated comments on earlier versions of this article, and a covering email. Section 11 catalogues these sources and states, item by item, which claims come from which and which connecting derivations are new here.

Section 2. Reading a spacetime diagram.

Two ideas from relativity carry the whole argument, and both are easiest to see in a diagram, shown in Figure 1.

One caution before the ideas. A spacetime diagram is a chart of events, a record of where and when, and in Dennis's model that is all it is. On his view the four-dimensional spacetime of the diagram is a myth, an abstraction: a bookkeeping device, not an existing thing. What exists is the present. This is the presentist metaphysics, and Section 9 takes it up; it changes nothing about how the diagrams are used, but it governs what they are taken to mean.

An event's light cone. Draw time going up the page and one direction of space across it, and pick the scale so that a light ray travels along a line at 45 degrees. Now take any event, any particular place at any particular moment, and draw the two 45-degree lines through it. They cut the diagram into three regions. Above are the events this one can influence, since a signal from it, travelling at or below the speed of light, can reach them. Below are the events that can influence it. To the left and right are events that can do neither, because a signal would have to outrun light in one direction or the other. Nothing material can ever cross a 45-degree line: an object's path through the diagram is always steeper than that.

What may count as now. We are used to thinking of the present moment as something that happens everywhere at once, and in a diagram that would be a horizontal line. Relativity says otherwise. Two observers moving relative to each other slice spacetime into instants differently, and neither is wrong. What the theory does require is that an instant not connect two events with a cause-and-effect relation between them, since one of those is genuinely earlier than the other and they cannot be simultaneous. In the diagram this means only one thing: a surface may serve as an instant provided it stays shallower than 45 degrees everywhere. Such a surface is called spacelike. Every spacelike surface is an admissible now: there are infinitely many of them through any event, and relativity provides no way to pick one out.

Figure 1, described in words. The figure has two panels. The first panel shows a single event, labeled E, with its future and past light cones drawn as two lines crossing at 45 degrees, dividing the diagram into the region E can affect, the region that can affect E, and the two side regions that are causally disconnected from E in both directions. The second panel shows the same event with three different spacelike surfaces passing through it, each one admissible as a slice of the present, alongside a fourth surface, drawn too steep, that is not admissible. The caption reads: how to read a spacetime diagram. Panel a. Time runs up the page and one direction of space across it, with the scale chosen so that light travels along the dashed 45-degree lines. Nothing material can cross those lines, so an object's own path through the diagram is always steeper than 45 degrees. The lines separate the events that E can affect, the events that can affect E, and the events on either side that are cut off from E in both directions. Panel b. A surface serving as a single instant for the whole universe must stay shallower than a light ray everywhere. Any such surface will do, infinitely many pass through any given event, and relativity cannot single one of them out. Two observers in relative motion already disagree about which is the real now.

That second point is the hinge of the model. Keep it in view: relativity does not single out one slicing of spacetime into instants. Dennis uses it to place the moment of creation, and then, in the model's finished form, to specify every now of the creation week and every now thereafter.

Section 3. The expanding universe the model starts from.

The standard cosmological model rests on a solution of Einstein's equations found in the 1920s and 1930s, named for Friedmann, Lemaître, Robertson and Walker, and commonly known by their initials as the Friedmann-Lemaître-Robertson-Walker cosmology. It describes a universe filled with matter that exerts no pressure, whose overall size changes with time. The changing size is carried by a single number, the scale factor a, which multiplies all distances. Galaxies are labelled by fixed coordinates that ride along with the expansion, so a galaxy keeps the same label forever while the physical distance to it grows. These are called comoving coordinates. The value of the comoving coordinate is the predicted distance of the object at the present time, called the epoch. From a differential-equation point of view it is the initial condition for an extrapolation into the past, and such an extrapolation ultimately ends at the theoretical Big Bang singularity.

The Friedmann-Lemaître-Robertson-Walker family has closed, flat and open members. The 2018 paper worked in the closed version, in which space is finite and curves back on itself; Dennis describes that as a slight prejudice of the time, and his current model is the flat version, the one observation currently favors: the universe is infinite, expands forever, and its geometry at any instant is Euclidean. The construction is indifferent to the choice, and this digest follows the current model throughout. With the cosmological constant set to zero and only pressureless matter present, the flat solution grows in size as a is proportional to t to the two-thirds power, with t the cosmic time since the solution's beginning and t-naught, equal to 13.8 billion years, its present value.

On homogeneity, Dennis's position, in 2018 and now, is that the perfect uniformity of the Friedmann-Lemaître-Robertson-Walker solution is a zeroth-order approximation: the mass density averaged over large patches, adequate for modelling the large-scale motion of the galaxies, and nothing more. The model universes he builds depart from exact homogeneity in their initial data while retaining it as the large-scale average, and Section 8 returns to what survives of it observationally. At the end of the inflationary Dasha period described in Section 7, the universe is homogeneous on that large-scale average.

Two features of the setup matter for what follows.

The singularity is an extrapolation. Running the solution backward, the scale factor shrinks to zero and the density becomes infinite. That moment is the Big Bang. It is not something the equations hand you as a fact about the world; it is where the solution runs out if you extend it as far back as it will go. In Dennis's September 2025 note the present epoch time t-naught is glossed the same way, quote, conceptually merely time to singularity, end quote. His whole move is to decline the extrapolation.

The instants are a choice. Pictures of the expanding universe are usually drawn as a series of slices, each one the whole of space at one moment. A complete family of such slices, one for every moment, is what Dennis calls a foliation of the spacetime. Those slices are surfaces of constant cosmic time, which incidentally equals earth time, and they are spacelike, so by Section 2 they are admissible. They are also natural, since the average density is uniform on them. But natural is not forced: they are one admissible family among many, and nothing in the equations says the universe began on one of them. That gap between what is natural and what is determined is where the model lives.

One more piece of bookkeeping. It is convenient to rescale the time coordinate so that light rays run at exactly 45 degrees even while the universe is expanding. The rescaled clock is called conformal time, written eta. In conformal coordinates the flat Friedmann-Lemaître-Robertson-Walker metric is given by Equation 1: d s squared equals a squared of eta, times the quantity, minus d eta squared, plus d r squared, plus r squared d omega squared.

Here r is the comoving distance from the origin and d omega squared collects the angular directions. For the flat matter-only solution, the scale factor and the clock conversion are given by Equations 2 and 3. Equation 2: a of eta equals k squared eta squared over nine, where k equals one over t-naught. Equation 3: eta equals three over k, times the quantity k t, raised to the one-third power.

So that today, eta-naught equals three t-naught, equal to 41.4 billion years, and a of eta-naught equals one. These are the forms Dennis's September 2025 note uses. One consequence is worth pausing on: since light travels at 45 degrees in these coordinates, the comoving distance light could have covered since the solution's beginning is eta-naught, 41.4 billion light years. That is the flat matter-only model's horizon, the stand-in for the radius of the observable universe throughout what follows, and the note uses the deepest galaxies now measured, those of the JADES survey, the James Webb Space Telescope's deep field, as its marker for objects at essentially that depth.

Everything below is drawn in conformal coordinates, which is why the diagrams look like ordinary flat-space diagrams even though the universe in them is expanding.

Section 4. When did it begin? A freedom hiding in the equations.

Einstein's equations are local. They relate the curvature at a point to the matter at that point, and they say nothing about the shape of the whole. Two consequences follow that Dennis calls the cut-and-stitch method.

First, if you have a solution and you cut a piece out of it, what remains is still a solution. The equations held at every point before, and the points you kept have not changed. Second, if you have two solutions occupying separate regions, you may join them along a shared boundary and get a third solution, provided the geometry matches smoothly across the seam. These are not tricks; they are standard, and they are how physicists build models of collapsing stars by joining an interior to an exterior.

Dennis puts this to work on a deliberately idealized model he calls the bar bell, shown in Figure 2: two regions of the closed Friedmann-Lemaître-Robertson-Walker solution with different densities, joined by an empty neck. Its purpose, he stresses, is to exhibit one specific thing: that general relativity permits the flow of time in different regions to run through radically different amounts of aging. The denser region is smaller and lives out its whole history in less total time. So the two regions cannot both begin together and end together. If you line up their moments of maximum size, their beginnings fall at different times. If you slide one so that the beginnings coincide, the endings separate. Both arrangements solve the same equations, and nothing in the theory says which is the real one.

Figure 2, described in words. The figure has two panels, each showing two lens-shaped regions of different size joined by a vacuum neck, drawn as a hatched wedge between them. In the first panel the two regions' moments of maximum expansion are lined up, so their beginnings fall at different times; in the second panel one region is slid so that the two beginnings coincide instead, and their endings fall apart. The caption reads: why the theory does not fix when things began. Each region expands, stops and falls back, and the outlines are drawn from the exact solution, so the denser region is both smaller and shorter-lived; the hatched wedge between them is the empty neck that joins them. Panel a. A denser region runs through the whole cycle faster, so matching the two clocks at maximum size unmatches their beginnings. Panel b. Slide the denser region until the two beginnings coincide, and the endings separate instead. Both are solutions of the same equations: general relativity does not say when each region begins, and the general solution carries a free function, the start time at each place, that it never fixes.

This is not a quirk of the bar bell. When the general solution for a spherically symmetric universe with varying density is written out, it contains an arbitrary function of position, conventionally called the time to the Big Bang at each place. The equations never determine it. It has to be put in by hand as part of the initial conditions, and every choice gives a valid universe.

Dennis is careful to point out that this is not a creationist invention. Cosmologists studying accelerated expansion had already noted it. He quotes Enqvist, 2008: quote, the universe could have an inhomogeneous big bang, where the universe came into being at different times at different points, end quote.

So the situation is this. General relativity tells you how a universe develops once you say what it was like on some initial spacelike surface. It does not tell you which surface, and it does not tell you when. Those are initial conditions, and they come from somewhere else.

Section 5. The construction.

Now the argument itself, shown in Figure 3.

A useful analogy first. Think of the standard cosmology as a long film, running from the Big Bang to the present. Every frame is one instant of the whole universe. Dennis throws away the opening reels, the ones containing the bang, and assembles a new first frame out of pieces of the remaining film: for parts of the universe near Earth he takes a piece from late in the film, and for parts far away he takes a piece from much earlier. That collaged frame is the moment of creation. Because he has only selected from footage that already existed, what he ends up with is still a legitimate solution of the equations, by the cutting rule of Section 4. The analogy is loose, but it gets the shape of the move right; the rest of this section makes it precise.

Choosing the surface. Put Earth at the origin of the comoving coordinates. Dennis takes as the initial surface, the beginning of Day One, the hyperbola of Equation 4: eta equals eta-naught, minus the square root of the quantity r squared plus b squared. Here b is a parameter with the dimensions of time. Near Earth the surface sits at eta equals eta-naught minus b: creation at Earth precedes the present by b, and in his current model b equals 7,000 years, so this is the age of the earth and, in a presentist cosmology, the age of the universe as well. Far from Earth the surface dips down toward the underlying solution's beginning, approaching the straight line eta equals eta-naught minus r, which is exactly the path of a light ray arriving at Earth today. Two properties matter. The surface is spacelike everywhere, its slope always shallower than 45 degrees, so by Section 2 it is a legitimate instant. And it is asymptotically null: far away it runs just below the 45-degree line rather than crossing it. Unlike its closed-model counterpart, the flat-model surface also has an outer edge, where it meets the underlying solution's beginning: at comoving radius 41.4 billion light years the square root in Equation 4 uses up all of eta-naught, and the surface runs into the epoch of infinite density. The created state there is the primordial plasma itself, and Section 8 returns to what that means for the microwave background.

Everything below this surface is thrown away. In Dennis's words, quote, the portion of the Friedmann-Lemaître-Robertson-Walker solution below the surface labeled Day One did not exist, end quote. What remains is a genuine solution of Einstein's equations that begins on a differently shaped surface than the standard one, and the initial data on that surface, matter density, motion, the quantum fields themselves, are whatever the underlying solution carried there. Because the surface passes through progressively earlier stages of the underlying solution as one moves outward, the created state is graded with distance: as Dennis puts it in his comments, the geometry is inhomogeneous, since the surface slices through regions of varying density.

The revised metric. The 2018 paper works the construction out in coordinates adapted to the surface, in its equations 17 through 20, for the closed model. His September 2025 note supplies the flat-model replacements, and the steps mirror the paper's exactly. Replace the comoving radius by a stretched coordinate rho, and define a time coordinate tau that is constant on each hyperbola of the family, as in Equation 5: r equals b times the hyperbolic sine of rho, and tau equals eta plus b times the hyperbolic cosine of rho.

The creation surface of Equation 4 is the member tau equals eta-naught, and the surfaces of constant tau above it are its successors, each one an admissible now of the model universe. Substituting Equation 5 into Equation 1 gives the metric in the adapted coordinates, Equation 6: d s squared equals a squared of the quantity tau minus b times the hyperbolic cosine of rho, times the quantity, minus d tau squared, plus two b times the hyperbolic sine of rho, times d tau times d rho, plus b squared d rho squared, plus b squared times the hyperbolic sine squared of rho, times d omega squared.

Completing the square in the bracket, the step the 2018 paper records as its equations 19 and 20, puts it in the form used below, Equation 7: d s squared equals a squared of the quantity tau minus b times the hyperbolic cosine of rho, times the quantity, minus the hyperbolic cosine squared of rho, times d tau squared, plus b squared times the quantity, d rho plus the hyperbolic sine of rho over b, times d tau, that whole quantity squared, plus b squared times the hyperbolic sine squared of rho, times d omega squared.

These are the 2018 paper's equations 18 and 20 with exactly two substitutions: the closed model's angular profile, f sub K of kai, equal to the sine squared of kai, becomes the flat model's r squared equals b squared times the hyperbolic sine squared of rho, and the closed model's cycloidal scale factor becomes Equation 2. Nothing else changes.

Why the light arrives. Light rays satisfy d s squared equals zero, and for radial rays Equation 7 integrates in closed form, exactly as in the 2018 paper's equation 21, giving Equation 8. For light coming toward Earth, tau minus tau-naught equals b times the quantity, e to the minus rho, minus e to the minus rho-naught. For light going out, tau minus tau-naught equals b times the quantity, e to the rho, minus e to the rho-naught.

Look at the incoming case. However large rho-naught is, that is, however far away the source, the elapsed time cannot exceed b, because e to the minus rho-naught is never negative. Light from any distance reaches Earth within a time b of setting out. The outgoing case is the mirror image: light leaving Earth crawls outward, taking longer and longer to cover each additional step.

Nothing here changes the local speed of light, which is still c everywhere in the model, as it must be in any solution of Einstein's equations. What has changed is the relation between the chosen time coordinate and the light cones. The surfaces of constant tau are tilted toward the incoming direction, so measured against them, incoming light crosses large comoving distances in little coordinate time, and outgoing light does not.

How the sky fills in. Putting the emission on the creation surface, tau-naught equals eta-naught, and writing tau-prime for the time elapsed since creation, the incoming law solved for the farthest visible source gives the model's particle horizon, Equation 9: rho-naught equals minus the logarithm of the quantity one minus tau-prime over b, which grows without bound as tau-prime approaches b: after a time b has passed, the entire created universe is in view. Inverting the same law gives the arrival time of the first light from a source at comoving distance r, Equation 10: T of r equals b times the quantity one minus e to the minus rho, where rho equals the inverse hyperbolic sine of r over b.

With b equal to 7,000 years the schedule is concrete, and Dennis states it plainly in his comments: light from the very distant galaxies took seven thousand years or so to reach Earth, and much closer objects still took hundreds to thousands of years. From a star 1,000 light years out, the first light arrived after about 930 years, barely ahead of the naive travel time; from 10,000 light years, after about 4,800 years; from everything deeper than a few million light years, in a compressed rush spread across the years approaching the seven-thousandth, detailed in Table 1 of Section 6. The sky filled from near to far across the whole of history since creation, and it is completing about now: the deep-field light now arriving at the James Webb telescope is the first light from the outermost created regions. That is his stated design condition for the current value of b: the initial creation present is chosen so that all the light now arriving, from the James Webb telescope's deep fields say, gets here within about 7,000 years. In the 2018 paper the one quantitative figure, its Figure 10, used a value of about 1,000 years, which Dennis now describes as chosen for, quote, chartmanship reasons, end quote, to fit the effect within the graph's range; and its schematic of the creation week, its Figure 9, was notional and drawn freehand. That figure's caption remarked that, as drawn, the entire cosmos is visible at Earth by Day Seven, but Dennis notes that he specified no distances for the objects visible by then, and his current schedule, seven thousand years for the deepest light, supersedes the drawing.

Section 6. How big was the universe when it was created?

The question sounds odd for an infinite flat universe, but it has a precise and finite answer for the part of it we can see, and Dennis's September 2025 note computes it. The answer explains where the inflationary Dasha period of Section 7 starts from, so it is worth walking through slowly.

What size means here. The creation surface is an instant, all of it at once. Its size is therefore measured with rulers laid along the surface itself: proper distance, the sum of local ruler lengths, at the instant of creation. This is a different quantity from today's comoving distance. A galaxy whose light now reaches us from billions of light years away sat, on the creation surface, at some definite proper distance from Earth; the calculation finds that distance.

Distance along the surface. Move outward along the creation surface by a small step d rho. By Equation 5 the comoving radius changes by d r equals b times the hyperbolic cosine of rho, times d rho, and conformal time changes by d eta equals minus b times the hyperbolic sine of rho, times d rho: the step goes outward and downward at once, because the surface dips toward the past. The interval of Equation 1 for this step is given by Equation 11: d s squared equals a squared of eta, times the quantity, d r squared minus d eta squared, which equals a squared of eta, times b squared, times the quantity, the hyperbolic cosine squared of rho minus the hyperbolic sine squared of rho, times d rho squared, which equals a squared of eta, times b squared, times d rho squared.

The near-cancellation is the whole story. The surface runs just inside the light cone, so its space-leg and time-leg almost cancel, and the identity that the hyperbolic cosine squared of rho, minus the hyperbolic sine squared of rho, equals one, leaves a small positive remainder: each step of d rho costs a proper length of just a times b times d rho, however large the comoving stride, b times the hyperbolic cosine of rho, times d rho, has become. This is the same margin by which the surface stays spacelike; the geometry that makes it an admissible instant is the geometry that makes it small.

The scale factor along the surface. The factor a in Equation 11 is evaluated on the surface, where eta equals eta-naught minus b times the hyperbolic cosine of rho, and it shrinks as one moves out, because the surface passes through ever-earlier epochs of the underlying solution. Near Earth a is approximately one; at the outer edge, where the surface meets the epoch of infinite density, a reaches zero. Distant matter is created in an early, dense, compact state, and that is precisely why it takes up so little room.

The integral. The proper radius out to comoving distance r is then, by Equation 12: D of r equals the integral of d s, which equals k squared b over nine, times the integral, from zero up to rho, of the quantity, eta-naught minus b times the hyperbolic cosine of rho-prime, that whole quantity squared, with respect to rho-prime, using Equation 2 for a.

Expanding the square and integrating term by term, the note's closed form, Equation 13, is: D of r equals k squared b over nine, times the quantity, eta-naught squared times rho, minus two b eta-naught times the hyperbolic sine of rho, plus b squared over two, times the quantity, rho plus the hyperbolic sine of rho times the hyperbolic cosine of rho, where rho equals the inverse hyperbolic sine of r over b.

The first term dominates over almost the whole range, and since k squared eta-naught squared over nine equals one, it contributes just b times rho: proper distance at creation grows only logarithmically with comoving distance, because rho is approximately the logarithm of two r over b, once r exceeds b. Each factor of ten in today's distance added roughly the same 16,000 light years at creation. Near the outer edge the correction terms grow comparable to the leading term and the total settles at D approximately equal to b times the quantity, rho-max minus three-halves, with rho-max equal to the inverse hyperbolic cosine of eta-naught over b, approximately 16.29.

The result. Taking the note's marker for the outermost visible matter, the JADES deep field at comoving distance 41.4 billion light years, which is the flat model's whole horizon, Equation 13 gives Equation 14: D sub JADES equals 103,500 light years.

In the note's words, that is the distance at which JADES was created, quote, before being supernaturally accelerated to its current distance, end quote. The entire observable universe, everything the James Webb telescope now photographs across 41 billion light years, was created incrementally within a ball about a hundred thousand light years in radius: roughly the size of our own galaxy. Dennis's comments spell out the sequence: after the JADES shell of galaxies was formed and accelerated out of the way, the next closest shell was created at roughly the same hundred-thousand-light-year distance, and this stretching-out inflationary Dasha period repeated until the heavens were completed. Why b cannot be small. The initial size scales almost linearly with b, and Dennis turns this into a physical argument for a large b. A creation week in which distant starlight arrives within ordinary days would need b of order one light day; Equation 13 then puts the entire universe, every star and galaxy, inside about one light month at creation. In his comments he is blunt about the consequence: at that distance the radiation from all the stars would turn the earth into a molten blob. No proposal, his own included, can deliver all the light to Earth within 24 hours without running into such difficulties. At b equal to 7,000 years the created universe fits within a hundred thousand light years of Earth and no such problem arises. The value of b is not derived from physics, Section 10 returns to what does fix it, but physics bounds it from below.

Section 7. The inflationary Dasha period.

Everything so far describes which universe was created: a solution of Einstein's equations beginning on the hyperbolic surface, a hundred thousand light years of created cosmos, light converging on Earth. What it does not yet describe is the creation week itself: how the universe got from that beginning to the state whose light we now receive. This is the part of the model Dennis regards as its most important ingredient, the part an earlier version of this digest omitted, and the part he has now named: the inflationary Dasha period.

The idea was present in the 2018 paper, stated in its abstract as its actual conclusion: the models solve the distant light problem, quote, from a theological point of view, in which God advances the, cosmic, time of the spacelike hypersurfaces at a non-uniform rate during the miraculous creation week, end quote. The paper's Figure 9 drew it as an unfolding sheet: the surfaces of the week peeling upward from the Day One hyperbola, with the remote regions advanced through far more cosmic time per day of the week than the region near Earth. In the years after publication Dennis stepped back from the idea, in informal discussions on the creationist online forum CRSnet, because he could not see direct observable consequences of the advancement. He now calls that retreat a, quote, momentary lapse of insanity, end quote, and the advancement essential: later consideration showed him that direct observable effects are not needed, since the implication for accelerated mechanical processes is sufficient. In the current form of the model it is stated as a definite episode with a definite mechanism.

What advances. During the week, God advances cosmic time at different rates in different places: slowly near Earth, where the week passes as an ordinary week, and at miraculous rates in the remote universe, where billions of years of cosmic evolution run their course. The advancement is equivalent, in Dennis's words, to accelerated mechanical processes: distant galaxies genuinely form, burn and evolve, at rates ordinary physics would not produce on its own. The histories written in the deep sky are real histories, run fast, not painted appearances. This is also how deep-sky structures whose formation appears to require vast stretches of time, evolved stars, wound spiral arms, relaxed clusters of galaxies, can be present in a universe seven thousand years old: their formative processes genuinely ran to completion during the week, at the accelerated rates of the advancement.

What spreads. The same advancement, seen in proper distance, is a spreading of the created matter outward. As the remote regions race through their history, the scale factor there climbs from nearly zero toward its present value, and the created galaxies move outward, from the hundred-thousand-light-year ball of Section 6 to their present distances of millions and billions of light years. Dennis is emphatic about the ontology: space does not grow, and he repudiates the expansion-of-space interpretation altogether. On his Machian view, set out in Dennis, 2022, the galaxies themselves were propelled through space; the scale factor is a mathematical device describing their motion, not a property of an expanding space. In comoving labels nothing changes, because the labels ride with the matter. In proper distance the recession of the remote galaxies is faster than light, and this is not prohibited: the speed limit c only ever applies locally, saying that no object outruns a light ray passing beside it, and it does not apply to the relative velocities of matter at great distances. The superluminal recession of distant galaxies in the standard cosmology is permitted on the same ground. Dennis describes the process as the galaxies being created at about a hundred thousand light years and then propelled, or shot, outward, and identifies it with, quote, the stretching out of the heavens, end quote, his phrase in both the 2018 paper's Figure 9 caption and the covering email; per that email, the stretching belongs before or at the beginning of Day One. His own illustration of it is reproduced as Figure 5: successive snapshots in which each new galaxy appears near the hundred-thousand-light-year mark while those created before it stand ever farther out.

Why this breaks no equation. General relativity determines how geometry and matter evolve, but it famously does not determine how a spacetime is to be sliced into moments, and it leaves two functions entirely free: the lapse, which says how much proper time elapses at each place between one slice and the next, and the shift, which says how the spatial coordinates slide from slice to slice. In the three-plus-one formulation these are written N and N-superscript-i, essentially the metric components g-sub-zero-zero and g-sub-zero-i; the 2018 paper's postscript sets the model in this language, and for the foliation of Equation 7 at fixed b they read N equals a times the hyperbolic cosine of rho, and N-superscript-rho equals the hyperbolic sine of rho, divided by b. The theory's own equations never fix these functions; every choice describes the same physics sliced differently, and a choice of how the nows advance is exactly a choice of lapse. Dennis's account of the inflationary Dasha period is that he has merely deduced the lapse and shift required to produce a young-earth cosmology; the specification is consistent with the theory because the theory leaves that choice open. What is miraculous in the week is the schedule, the rates at which the Creator advances the world's time from place to place, not any violation of the equations that govern what happens as it advances. The interpretation of the advancement is, Dennis notes, ambiguous between two equivalent descriptions: a rapid advancement of cosmic time is the same thing as an acceleration of the mechanical processes running in it. He now prefers the accelerated-processes description, since it is precisely the nod to Faulkner's Dasha conjecture.

One function, not one number. This is also where the parameter b takes its final form. A single number b describes one hyperbolic surface; the week's foliation is a family of surfaces, and Dennis states in his comments that the model technically contains one inflationary function, b of t, governing how the surfaces' shape develops during the inflationary Dasha period, anchored at b equal to 7,000 years on the Day One surface. Its present value is b equals infinity: the current now-surface is flat, its hyperbolic radius having grown without bound. The full functional form has not yet been published; Dennis indicates that the complete explication appears in a forthcoming book on the model. What the digest can already say is what the function does: it interpolates the foliation from the Day One hyperbola through the unfolding of the week, flattening the surfaces as it advances them, after which time advances uniformly on the flat surfaces of constant cosmic time and ordinary providence replaces the miraculous schedule. All the arrival-time results of Section 5 are unaffected by the shape of b of t, because light rays and their arrival events are facts about the spacetime itself, not about how it is sliced.

The name. Dasha is the Hebrew verb of Genesis chapter one, verse eleven, quote, let the earth bring forth grass, end quote, from which Faulkner, 2013, named his own proposal for the starlight problem: as the plants sprang up mature within a day, so God brought the light of the heavens to Earth miraculously fast. Dennis's inflationary Dasha period incorporates Faulkner's shooting concept with the direction reversed: not light shot inward from distant galaxies, but galaxies created close and shot outward, their light thereafter travelling normally. And he draws the comparison with the standard cosmology explicitly: with the inflationary Dasha period the model now contains an early inflationary epoch of superluminal spreading, closely aligned with the standard model's own inflation, arrived at from the biblical account rather than from atheistic initial conditions. The secularists, he remarks in the comments, have vindicated the model.

What was created when. On the question of the days, Dennis is specific, and it revises a common young-earth reading: the stars were not created on Day Four. They were created in verse one, as a necessary constituent of the heavens, since the heavens that, quote, declare the glory of God, end quote, cannot be invisible realms or empty space. He argues further that the Hebrew word for heaven would have been understood by the original audience as including the starry heavens, and he adduces Job chapter thirty-eight, in his description the earliest and Gentilic creation account, spoken directly by the Logos out of the whirlwind. The luminaries' role on Day Four is a separate matter belonging to the biblical text rather than to the model's geometry; what the model requires is only that the stellar heavens exist from the beginning, which verse one supplies.

Section 8. What stays the same, and what we see tonight.

Because the model is a piece cut out of the standard Friedmann-Lemaître-Robertson-Walker solution, carrying the same geometry on the piece that was kept, every equation and every measurable quantity inside it is the standard cosmology's. Dennis states the consequence plainly: quote, the predictions of red-shifts are identical to those of the Friedmann-Lemaître-Robertson-Walker cosmology, end quote. The Hubble relation between distance and redshift, the stretching-out of distant supernova light curves, the dimming of distant surfaces, the cosmic microwave background, the characteristic scale imprinted in the clustering of galaxies: all of these behave exactly as in the standard model, because in every respect except where it starts and how its early time was run, this is the standard model, inflationary period included. A natural worry illustrates the point: should galaxies created only about a hundred thousand light years away not appear enormously large on the sky? They do not, because the light now arriving left each galaxy at an event of the underlying solution and crossed the same geometry as in the standard model, so apparent angular sizes follow the standard angular-diameter distance exactly as redshifts follow the standard Hubble relation.

That is the construction's chief economy. There is no observation that fits the ordinary cosmology and fails here. It is also worth stating the other side plainly: since the two agree on everything measurable, no observation can distinguish them either, and the model makes no new predictions of its own.

What survives of the cosmological principle. The created state is graded with depth, so the exact geometry is not homogeneous; but nothing about that is observable as a departure from the standard picture. In Dennis's summary, the zeroth-order approximation gives the same visual field and the same density as a function of redshift as the standard model, and the symmetry the exact model retains is rough global isotropy about us, as in the Lemaître-Tolman class of solutions the 2018 paper develops. Homogeneity, which for Dennis was always a large-patch average rather than an exact symmetry, as noted in Section 3, holds in the model in exactly that averaged sense.

The ages of the things we see. Dennis's apparent-ages slide, redrawn from the model's equations in Figure 6, superimposes the creation surface on the standard cosmic history and reads off what we should see, adding to the model what the 2018 construction left unaddressed: apparent ages. We observe along Earth's past light cone. An object at depth is seen at the moment that cone crosses its worldline, so its seen age is the time from its creation, on the hyperbola, to that crossing. Near Earth the two surfaces are far apart: a nearby star is seen thousands of years after its creation. Far away the hyperbola hugs the light cone, and the seen age shrinks toward zero: the deep field is seen essentially as it was created, in the young, compact, still-forming state the surface inherited from the early epochs of the underlying solution. The slide also notes the grading of the created states themselves: matter nearer Earth was created in a later, more developed stage of the underlying history, so in principle the created age grows toward Earth, the elapsed time since creation adds to it, and both the creation model and the standard model therefore predict the same thing: closer objects should appear more mature. The James Webb telescope's youthful-looking deep-field galaxies sit in this picture exactly where the model puts its just-created outer regions.

The microwave background. The creation surface's outer edge is created as primordial plasma, as described in Section 5, and Earth's past light cone meets the surface at that edge: the light now arriving from the deepest visible shell left the recombining plasma there. On the slide Dennis marks the recombination of electrons and protons into the first hydrogen atoms with the note, quote, all by the hand of God, end quote, and labels the standard history's Dark Ages, the stretch between last scattering and the first galaxies, as not part of the creation model: at the depths where that era would be seen, the created state is already on either side of it. The background thus arrives with the standard spectrum and the standard redshift, inherited whole from the underlying solution.

Section 9. Why a third of the paper is about the nature of time.

Readers sometimes skip the 2018 paper's long philosophical section. It is not decoration; the construction leans on it.

There are two views about what time is. On eternalism, past, present and future all equally exist, spacetime is a fixed four-dimensional block, and the sense that time passes is an illusion. Einstein held this, and put it memorably, in a remark Dennis, 2018, quotes: quote, for us believing physicists, the distinction between past, present and future is only a stubbornly persistent illusion, end quote. On presentism, the present moment is real, it genuinely passes, the past is gone and the future is not yet. Dennis's model is presentist through and through: this is the sense in which spacetime, for him, is a myth, an abstraction. The block of the diagrams is a chart of history, not a thing that exists; what exists is the present surface, and the week of Section 7 is a real succession of presents, advanced at the rates God chose.

Dennis argues for presentism several ways. It is what the biblical account requires, since a creation week and a history of redemption have to be things that happen rather than things that eternally obtain. It is more coherent than the alternative, which removes the flow of time from the cosmos only to reintroduce an infinite number of time flows, one along each observer's worldline. He presses this to a theological conclusion in his comments: on the eternalist picture those observers merely traverse a block of foreordained events created whole, so that every event in it, sin included, was created directly by the hand of God; a view he judges heretical, since it makes God directly the author of sin. And, most importantly for the physics, relativity does not compel eternalism. He traces this through Eddington and Reichenbach to Whitrow, 1980, whose reading is that the theory imposes a causal structure on events and is simply silent on whether they happen.

The argument then turns back on itself in a way that favors the model. Einstein's reason for denying a real present was that no measurement can determine which spacelike surface is the current one. But that cuts both ways. If no measurement can find the present surface, then no measurement can rule out the surface Dennis has chosen for creation either. As he puts it, quote, relativity forbids that man can operationally determine what is the current now surface, end quote. The choice of creation surface is not the sort of thing observation can refute, and anyone who invokes the operational argument against a real present has already conceded the point. What settles the question, on Dennis's view, is not empirical observation at all but special revelation: Scripture discloses the true present and the true history of its advancement, precisely the things measurement cannot reach.

Section 10. Conclusion.

What Dennis has shown is an existence proof, and a clean one. Within ordinary general relativity, with no new fields, no varying constants and no light created in flight, there is a universe in which every place is the same age as the earth, about seven thousand years on the creation clock; in which the light from every visible distance has arrived or is arriving, the deepest of it about now; and in which every observation since creation matches the standard cosmology. The one miracle is confined to the creation week: the schedule by which God advanced the world's time, written into functions the theory itself leaves free. Before this, it was reasonable to suspect that the distant-starlight problem was a contradiction. It is not. It is a question about initial conditions and about how the first week was run.

What the model does not do is derive its own initial condition. The surface of Equation 4 and the function b of t are not forced by the equations; the equations merely permit them. Dennis's comments put the division of labor directly: the 2018 paper was technical, and its purpose was to expose the limits of general relativity, what the theory does and does not determine. Where the theory falls silent, the guide is the Bible. The choice of b of t, the placement of the creation present, the reality of the week: these come from special revelation, not from empirical evidence, which by the operational argument of Section 9 could never reach them anyway. In the 2018 conclusion's words: quote, General Relativity can say nothing about which initial condition is to be accepted. The decision to accept one over another is motivated by philosophical and theological presuppositions alone, end quote. That is the point, Dennis says: the Bible is the guide.

He would say this is exactly right and not a shortcoming, since initial conditions have to come from somewhere and physics has never supplied its own. A reader may take that as sufficient or may want more. Either way the logical situation is clear, and it is the clarity, more than any particular value of b, that the work contributes.
