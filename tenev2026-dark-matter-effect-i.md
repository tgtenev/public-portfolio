## Abstract

In the first of three papers, we derive the galactic law of the dark-matter effect from the steady state of renewal in the Cosmic Fabric Model and test its algebraic form on rotation curves.
In galaxies the effect follows the baryons with one acceleration scale, near $`cH_0/2\pi`$, which modified Newtonian dynamics (MOND) introduces empirically alongside a chosen interpolation function.
While earlier accounts invoke the vacuum, an elastic medium or a superfluid, we propose a kinetic mechanism.
In the model's open-system form, space is an elastic solid that grows by a two-way exchange with a precursor, the renewal, whose net is the expansion.
Under one new postulate, modeled on athermal transformations in solids, a renewal event re-lays a layer in alignment when the strain contrast across the catchment length, its range of sensing, exceeds a threshold drawn afresh from an exponential law.
Other events trap a free volume.
The law also rests on three inherited postulates, requirements on the fabric's parts and nine identifications with laboratory behaviors of ordinary materials.
Because the share of aligning events is proportional to the field in weak fields, the law has the deep-MOND exponent of $`-1/2`$, and its one-parameter interpolation function reduces to MOND's simple function at zero free volume.
Both potentials of the metric take the same boost, so lensing matches the dynamics at linear order.
Given two identifications, the scale is the de Sitter acceleration, $`cH_{\rm dS}`$, times the logarithm of one plus the free volume, independent of the exchange's set point.
With this scale relation imposed, the law has one fitted constant, like MOND's simple function.
The set point instead determines the catchment length, estimated from the data at about 30 to 200 pc.
A smaller system keeps its own field Newtonian, so the model predicts a null result in wide binaries.
The law's algebraic form leads MOND's simple function on the rotation curves of the SPARC database by $`27 \pm 17`$ in $`\chi^2`$, or 1.6 standard deviations.
The fitted scale of $`0.97\times10^{-10}\ \mathrm{m\ s^{-2}}`$, about $`cH_0/7`$, implies a free volume of about 0.19, which the curves do not measure independently.
Solved in full in Paper II, the field equation fits worse than the algebraic form, by 1.7 to 2.0 standard deviations, and on the model's own Newtonian field worse than MOND's simple function solved likewise, by 2.7.
At catalog distances, the empirical function of the radial-acceleration relation fits better than the law's algebraic form, by up to 3.0 standard deviations.
The galactic acceleration scale becomes a property of a material comparable in free volume with glasses.

## 1. Introduction

In the outskirts of disk galaxies, stars and gas orbit faster than the gravity of the visible matter allows, so their orbits imply up to ten times the visible mass.
Four decades of rotation-curve work, culminating in the Spitzer Photometry and Accurate Rotation Curves (SPARC) database [1], show that this dark-matter effect (DMe) is a near-deterministic function of the visible baryons.
Thousands of measurements fall on one curve, the radial-acceleration relation [2], with an intrinsic scatter below 0.057 dex [3].
In its deep limb, the relation is the baryonic Tully–Fisher relation, with a logarithmic slope of 4 [4].
Fits that let the scale vary from galaxy to galaxy find no second parameter in the relation [3], as in the central-density relation [5].
A single acceleration scale, $`a_0 \approx 1.2\times10^{-10}\ \mathrm{m\ s^{-2}}`$, organizes the relation and lies within a factor of order one of $`cH_0`$, the acceleration that the present expansion rate $`H_0`$ sets [6].
Among these findings, the single scale and its nearness to $`cH_0`$ most demand an explanation.

Modified Newtonian dynamics (MOND), proposed by Milgrom [7], describes this regularity without explaining it: its interpolation function is chosen from a menu and its scale fitted.
Four accounts propose an origin for it [8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13], whereas the field theories of the regularity [14, 15] and MOND's relativistic completions [16, 17] take the function and the scale as inputs.

We propose that within bodies the DMe is a gravitational boost arising from the constitutive properties of space, which also set the floor of its global expansion and the range over which it responds to a body's field.
Building on the Cosmic Fabric Model, which treats space as a thin elastic solid, the fabric [18], we adopt its open-system form [19].
In this form, cosmic expansion is the growth of the fabric, the small net of a much faster two-way exchange with a bulk precursor phase.
We call this exchange the fabric's renewal.
Two constitutive scales come from the open-system model: the de Sitter rate, $`H_{\rm dS}`$, at which the fabric grows without matter, and the configurational memory time, $`\tau_c`$.
Given that rate, the memory time is equivalent to the set point, the residual strain that the exchange maintains without matter.
We identify the length that a signal at the speed of light covers in one memory time, $`c\tau_c`$, with the catchment length, the range over which a renewal event senses the field.
This identification, like each of the paper's nine, makes an element of the model correspond to a behavior of ordinary materials that the laboratory measures, here the finite memory of an anelastic solid.

When the field's strain contrast across the catchment length exceeds a threshold drawn at the event, the event re-lays its layer in alignment; otherwise it traps a free volume.
Around a body, the renewal holds a balance that maintains a radial stretch of the material, which orbits interpret as extra mass.
Here a body is any bound concentration of mass below the mass threshold of §2.5, from a planetary system or a star cluster to a galaxy or a small group of galaxies.
From that balance we derive the renewal growth boost law and its field equation.
Its interpolation function is MOND's simple function with an exponential hardening.
A second identification takes the mean of the thresholds to be a third of the set point, and the two together relate the law's acceleration scale to the de Sitter rate.
Systems smaller than the catchment length keep their own fields Newtonian.

Beyond galaxies, the DMe behaves as a gravitating content in the pattern of cosmic structure rather than as a boost set by the local field.
In this series we therefore treat the effect as having two sources: the boost that the renewal gives a body's field, and a gravitating content beyond bodies.
The series asks whether the same material, through its constitutive properties and its structure, can supply the second source as well.
In groups and clusters above a mass threshold, the effect has a further component whose nature the series leaves open.
Figure 1 maps where the law acts and where it is not applied.

This is the first of three papers on the DMe in the Cosmic Fabric Model.
It builds on the model of Tenev and Horstemeyer [18], on their 2019 treatment of the dark-matter effect [20], and on the open-system model [19].
To these it adds one postulate, the Threshold Renewal postulate.
We treat the consequences of the balance: the law, its scale, its lensing, the Newtonian screen below the catchment length, and the tests on the rotation curves of galaxies, where the renewal is the only source of the effect.
Paper II [21] covers the kinetics by which a disturbed stretch returns to the balance and the field theory off spherical symmetry.
For an example microstructure, it also checks the free volume, the one material parameter that the law adds to the open-system model, against a simulated unit.
Paper III [22] treats the outer limit of the law and the content beyond bodies.

Section 2 reviews the background, Section 3 derives the law and the screen, and Section 4 tests the law on the SPARC rotation curves.
Section 5 discusses the place of the law among earlier accounts, its relativistic status, its inputs, its open problems, and its falsification criteria.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 The fabric as a growing elastic solid

The Cosmic Fabric Model treats three-dimensional space as a thin elastic solid, termed the fabric, that bends into a four-dimensional hyperspace [18].
In this framework, matter enters as inclusions whose strain encodes the spacetime metric.
Solid mechanics then recovers the scalar spatial curvature of General Relativity [18] and the linearized Schwarzschild metric of a single spherical body [23], from which follow the gravitational redshift, the deflection of light, and the Shapiro time delay.
The perihelion advance is not derived, because evaluating it requires the lapse function at second order in the gravitational potential.
Although a material medium has a rest frame, the material postulates of the fabric hide it kinematically from rods, clocks, and light, for which Tenev and Horstemeyer [24] derived the Lorentz transformations.
We retain only the lapse and geodesic equations and the covariant form of the inclusion postulate from the 2019 treatment of the dark-matter effect by Tenev and Horstemeyer [20].

Tenev et al. [19] extended the framework by treating the fabric as an open thermodynamic system, immersed in a precursor fluid that fills the four-dimensional space and from which it solidifies continuously.
In this picture, cosmic expansion is the net growth of a two-way exchange across the growth interface, the fabric's two faces along the fourth dimension.
Because the fabric is thin, the exchange across these faces reaches every point of its volume.
The open-system model [19, Sec. 2.1] inherits from Tenev and Horstemeyer [18] the condition that the fabric's thickness along the fourth dimension stay invisible at the continuum scale, which removes the longitudinal wave.
The P-wave modulus therefore vanishes, $`M_{\text{P}} = 0`$, corresponding to a negative bulk modulus, $`K_0 = -\tfrac43\mu_0`$, where $`\mu_0`$ is the shear modulus.
Rather than eliminating the instability of this state, open-system growth converts it into steady growth.
From the sign structure of the growth kinetics, departures of the residual strain are argued to self-correct, although a formal proof remains an open problem.
Grown volume relaxes at a derived rate of $`1/\tau_c`$, where $`\tau_c`$ is the configurational memory time [19, Sec. 6.2].

Whatever matter the fabric holds, the constitutive constants of the interface set the floor of the fractional volumetric growth rate at $`3H_{\rm dS} = \lvert K_0\rvert\ \varepsilon_e^\ast/\zeta`$.
Here $`\varepsilon_e^\ast`$ is the constitutive set point, the residual strain that the exchange maintains without matter, written $`\varepsilon_{\text{res}}^\ast`$ in [19], while $`\zeta`$, the effective bulk viscosity, emerges from an assumed linear two-way exchange law [19, Sec. 3.1].
The same relation sets the floor of the expansion rate, the de Sitter rate $`H_{\rm dS}`$.
At $`H_0`$ = 67.4 km/s/Mpc and the de Sitter rate of §3.5, the associated acceleration, $`cH_{\rm dS}`$, is $`5.4\times10^{-10}\ \mathrm{m\ s^{-2}}`$, against $`5.9\times10^{-10}\ \mathrm{m\ s^{-2}}`$ at 73 km/s/Mpc, both of the order of Milgrom's acceleration scale $`a_0`$.
The open-system model does not set a third scale, the gross exchange rate of which the growth is the small net, and that rate enters only the time domain that Paper II treats.
The precursor acts as the fabric's reservoir, and in the ideal reservoir limit this expansion floor obeys an exact equation of state, $`w = -1`$, because the growth environment is constant [19, Sec. 7.2].
Matter of mean density $`\bar\rho`$, including the content beyond the baryons that §2.5 describes, raises the expansion rate above this floor through the Friedmann constraint:

$$H^2 = H_{\rm dS}^2 + \frac{8\pi G}{3}\ \bar\rho \quad (1)$$

where the curvature term of the fabric, a closed shell in the working geometry of [19], is neglected, as the flat-space value of $`\Omega_\Lambda`$ that we adopt assumes.
The floor is derived from the growth balance, while its equality with the Friedmann constraint's constant term belongs to the postulate stated below, as does the constraint's energy of motion, its term in the square of the expansion rate [19, Secs. 3.3 and 5.2].

The Background Datum postulate of Tenev et al. [19] designates the fabric's homogeneous configuration in the embedding hyperspace as the dynamical datum of the theory, the reference state relative to which the deviations that gravitate are defined.
Here the term has its engineering and geodesy sense: a reference against which position and deviation are defined and which is not itself an object of measurement.
This datum has energy but no momentum density and experiences no resistance to motion along its normal.
The following clauses and corollaries of the postulate enter the present model, the first two under names of our own:

1. *The trajectory clause* denies inertia to the fabric's placement and bends, so matter cannot couple inertially to the fabric's bulk motion.
The planetary ephemerides [25] select this specification, because an inertial alternative predicts a perihelion precession 600 times their bound or more [19, Sec. 9.3].
2. *The athermal clause*, the postulate's corollary of Configurational Thermodynamics, asserts that the precursor, the interface, and the bulk motion of the fabric involve no temperature, heat, or bath.
Under this clause, the strain contrast that guides the renewal involves configurational quantities alone, as does the agitation behind the fabric's roughening, the renewal that lays material out of alignment.
3. *The Datum Energy corollary* establishes that the energy that growth lays in the datum produces no curvature.

Observations exclude the Inherent Structure Hypothesis of Tenev and Horstemeyer [20], which attributed the dark-matter effect to an inherent curvature of space that matter does not cause, as §5.2 shows.
The curvature of the closed shell itself gives only a fractional field excess of order $`\lvert\Omega_k\rvert\ (H_0 r/c)^2`$ at a distance $`r`$ from a mass, below $`10^{-7}`$ within 1 Mpc even for a curvature parameter $`\lvert\Omega_k\rvert = 1`$.
By the trajectory clause, the only local signature of cosmic expansion is then the tidal term of the de Sitter floor, $`g(r) = -Gm(r)/r^2 + H_{\rm dS}^2\ r`$, which cannot generate the radially growing excess that flat rotation curves require.
We therefore look for the origin of the dark-matter effect in the rate of the expansion, through the renewal, rather than in the geometry of the fabric.

### 2.2 Thresholds, layers and free volume in ordinary materials

Thresholds, layers, and trapped free volume recur where ordinary solids transform.
In the athermal martensitic transformation of steels, Koistinen and Marburger [26] gave the transformed fraction as $`1 - e^{-0.011\ \Delta T}`$ at an undercooling $`\Delta T`$ in kelvin below the start temperature, whatever the time held.
A layered host, such as intercalated graphite [27] or a swelling clay [28], takes up material between its layers and expands along the stacking axis alone.
Marginal solids such as glasses hold free volume [29].
Section 3 sets each of these behaviors beside the element of the model that it motivates, while the table of the paper's inputs in the discussion collects the further precedents.

### 2.3 MOND as an observation

Viewed as an empirical observation, MOND indicates that a single acceleration scale organizes galactic anomalies and relates to the cosmic expansion rate [30].
McGaugh, Lelli, and Schombert [2] analyzed 2,693 data points from 153 SPARC galaxies on a unified curve, fitting a transition scale of $`g_\dagger = (1.20 \pm 0.02_{\rm rand} \pm 0.24_{\rm sys})\times10^{-10}\ \mathrm{m\ s^{-2}}`$, which aligns numerically with MOND's conventional acceleration scale, $`a_0`$ [6].
As Famaey and McGaugh [6] note, this scale matches $`cH_0/2\pi`$ within tens of percent.
The intrinsic scatter of the baryonic Tully–Fisher relation, $`0.10 \pm 0.02`$ dex [4], is notably smaller than the minimum variation, about 0.15 dex, produced by halo-to-halo differences in a standard model treatment [31].
Weak lensing extends the radial-acceleration relation two orders of magnitude lower in acceleration than the rotation curves reach [32].

Our earlier review of MOND [30] asks any explanation to produce the scale and its coincidence with the expansion rate, the deep exponent, the tightness of the relation, and the suppression of the effect at high acceleration.
Such an explanation must also make at least one prediction that differs from those of both MOND and the standard model.

MOND remains contested regarding both the external-field effect and galaxy clusters, where Table 1 states its shortfall.
Because the aquadratic-Lagrangian theory (AQUAL) of Bekenstein and Milgrom [14] is nonlinear in the total field, a uniform external field alters a system's internal dynamics, which Newtonian gravity with dark matter does not allow.
Analyses of overlapping Gaia data for wide binary stars also yield conflicting results [33, 34].

Relativistic extensions of MOND face further constraints from the neutron-star merger GW170817, which tightly bounds the fractional difference between the speed of gravitational waves and the speed of light to within $`-3\times10^{-15}`$ and $`+7\times10^{-16}`$ [35].
This multimessenger constraint ruled out a large class of relativistic MOND theories in which photons and gravitons couple to distinct physical metrics [36].

### 2.4 The dark-matter effect across four classes of system

The effect takes a different form in each of four classes of system.
Table 1 compares these forms with the Newtonian field of the baryons and with a MOND-type algebraic law.
In an algebraic law, the boosted acceleration at a point follows from a single value there, the Newtonian acceleration of the baryons, $`g_N`$, as $`g = \nu(g_N/a_0)\ g_N`$ with an interpolation function $`\nu`$.
In a field law, it follows instead from a field equation, such as the modified Poisson equation of AQUAL, whose differential operator acts on the potential around the point, so it depends on how the mass is arranged.
In spherical symmetry, the two coincide.

### 2.5 The second source of the dark-matter effect, in brief

Given the features of Table 1, the three papers of this series apply the law to bodies only, and not above a conventionally inferred host halo mass of approximately $`10^{13}`$ solar masses.
Above this threshold, groups and clusters require an additional gravitating component whose nature is left open.
No physical mechanism for the threshold is known, and it conflicts in kind with a renewal that is blind to the provenance of a field, as §3.5 states.
Paper III [22] addresses the outer limit of the law, the mass threshold, and the content residing beyond individual bodies.

Whatever its composition, this content must remain uniform on galactic scales, since any part of it clustered within a galaxy would add a field that the baryons do not trace, which the tight scatter of the rotation curves constrains [3].
Together with the component of open nature above the mass threshold, it holds 0.27 of the critical density, the matter density of the standard fits less that of the baryons [51].
That density enters the Friedmann constraint of Eq. (1).
None of the conclusions presented in this paper depend on the specific nature of that non-baryonic content.
Throughout the paper the law's source is the baryons of bodies, the content enters only through its mean density, and its field around bodies is the subject of Paper III.

## 3. The renewal growth boost law

The open-system model supplies two constitutive scales.
One is the de Sitter rate, $`H_{\rm dS}`$, which sets the floor of the fabric's growth and thus of its global expansion.
Alongside that rate, the model provides the configurational memory time, $`\tau_c = \zeta/\lvert K_0\rvert`$, which Tenev et al. [19] denote $`\tau_\zeta`$ and call the relaxation time of the in-plane exchange.
It is the fabric's effective bulk viscosity, $`\zeta`$, divided by the magnitude of its bulk modulus, $`K_0`$.
Through the floor relation of §2.1, the memory time equals $`\varepsilon_e^\ast/(3H_{\rm dS})`$, so the memory time and the constitutive set point, $`\varepsilon_e^\ast`$, represent one degree of freedom.

The law follows from three statements about the renewal around a body, covering guidance, the store of excess, and its readout:

1. A body's field guides the renewal.
Each renewal event is guided with probability $`1 - e^{-x_s}`$, where $`x_s = g/(cH_{\rm dS})`$ is the total field in units of the de Sitter acceleration.
This form holds at the counting factor of one that §3.2 identifies.
2. A guided event smooths, re-laying a layer of the material in alignment and removing its excess, whereas an unguided event roughens, adding a free volume $`\delta_{\text{fv}}`$ to its layer.
Each layer therefore holds an excess of $`\delta_{\text{fv}}`$ times the number of roughening events since its last smoothing.
3. The excess lies along the field as folded material path that instruments do not measure.
It lowers the conductivity of the static field along the stack of layers, so a given mass holds a steeper gradient, and the boost equals one plus the mean excess.

Under statement 1, the field that guides the renewal is the total field, and under statement 3 the excess sets the total field through the boost.
The three statements therefore form a closed loop, running from the total field through the guided share and the excess back to the total field, which Figure 2 draws with the inputs and the output of the renewal.
The law, $`\nu = 1 + \delta_{\text{fv}}/(e^{x_s} - 1)`$, is the steady state of that loop, the boost at which the excess returns the same total field that sets the guided share.
At each baryonic field, the loop has a unique steady state, as §3.5 shows.

These statements rest on requirements on the fabric's parts, which §3.1 states.
Statements 1 and 2 make up one new postulate, the Threshold Renewal postulate, which §§3.2 and 3.3 state in full, while statement 3 rests on inherited postulates and identifications.
Nine identifications enter as well, each making an element of the fabric correspond to a named behavior or property of ordinary materials that laboratory measurements establish.
That behavior or property, the identification's counterpart, gives the element its physical meaning and provides our ground for adopting it.
In §3.3, for instance, a layer of the fabric holds its excess along its normal, just as graphite takes up a guest species and swells along its stacking axis alone [27].
No identification is derived, since the laboratory measures the counterpart rather than the fabric, and §5.1 tabulates the counterparts, which match their elements only in part.
Each identification names its counterpart where it enters, and two of them relate the law's acceleration scale to the de Sitter rate.

Three inherited postulates support the law: the Background Datum postulate of Tenev et al. [19] alongside the inclusion and lapse postulates of Tenev and Horstemeyer [18].
Tenev et al. [19] count a third postulate in the original formulation, that space is a thin elastic solid, which this series takes as the model itself rather than as a postulate of the law.
By an identification in §3.4, the inclusion postulate is taken as a local law of the material.
Because the athermal clause denies the interface a temperature, a heat, and a bath, each concept borrowed from materials science is defined here in the fabric's own terms.
As with the statics of loaded structures, we evaluate the steady state maintained by renewal without detailing how a disturbed region returns to it, which Paper II derives.
Consequently, time enters this static treatment solely through material constants such as the memory time.
Inputs that come from data or from a convention are marked as such where they enter, and Appendix A summarizes the notation.

### 3.1 The requirements on the fabric's microstructure

The derivation rests on a few properties of the fabric's material, which we state here as requirements, while a theory of the microstructure that derives them remains future work.
The fabric must be one material with the following parts, called sectors:

1. *A shear-bearing sector* that bears the whole shear load.
Its shear modulus, $`\mu_0`$, equals its rest energy density, $`u_0`$, at the datum, as in [19], and in every state differing from it only in the second sector's arrangement, so its transverse waves travel at $`c`$.
No configuration of it is denser than its uniform saturated state.
2. *A sector of discrete units* whose densest state is the template, the aligned arrangement in which neighboring units share one orientation.
Misaligned units hold a larger natural volume, and rulers and clocks do not measure its excess, the free volume.
The packing of the units has no stiffness against strain or shear.
With the boundaries between misaligned units included, its configurational energy is datum energy.
3. *A sector that the renewal does not re-lay*, on which light and gravitational waves travel and whose charged faults are ordinary matter.
Its spacing exceeds the template's units by a factor of several, so its links span the folds that roughening lays into the template rather than following them.
The uniform growth adds to it by a process separate from the exchange that lays material, so the exchange's deposit around a static field adds nothing to it.

The density requirements of the first two sectors form the premises for the loose packing of roughening in §3.3.
Because the configurational energy of the second sector is datum energy, the standing pattern of free volume maintained by renewal produces no field of its own, in accordance with the Datum Energy corollary of the open-system model.

Two results of Paper II [21] support building the law on these requirements.
By an argument just short of a theorem, a fabric that saturates the causal bound, as the first requirement does, cannot bear its shear load on discrete elements in void and must instead be a composite.
Folds laid into the lattice on which light travels would also turn rays through angles of order one, so light needs a sector that the renewal does not re-lay, as the third requirement states.

The separate-lattice architecture, examined in Paper II, provides an example satisfying these requirements.
Its three components are the shear continuum, the orientational sector, and the lattice of cells.
Analogous to the matrix of a composite material [52], the shear continuum acts as a structureless condensate whose uniform saturated state is its densest.
In the orientational sector, misaligned units hold free volume, just as the boundaries between misoriented grains in a polycrystal do [53].
Within the lattice of cells, light behaves as a field on the links, comparable to the artificial photon in a frustrated magnet [54].
Each sector of this example thus has a counterpart in ordinary materials, though no ordinary material combines all three and no theory yet derives the requirements.
From here on we name the three sectors by their forms in this example, though the results below need only the stated requirements.

### 3.2 The first statement: guidance of the renewal by a drawn threshold

The Threshold Renewal postulate has two parts: statement 1 on guidance and statement 2 on the two modes of an event.
Under statement 1, each renewal event compares the strain contrast across one catchment length with a threshold and is guided when the contrast exceeds it.
Each event draws its threshold afresh from an exponential law.
We call a guided event a smoothing and an unguided one a roughening, and §3.3 states what each does under statement 2.

A renewal event senses the actual strain, $`\varepsilon`$, the strain that rulers and clocks measure.
The neutral strain, $`\varepsilon_n`$, is the strain at which the fabric would hold no stress.
It belongs to the natural configuration of Tenev et al. [19], the configuration in which the exchange lays new material.
Their difference, the elastic strain $`\varepsilon_e = \varepsilon - \varepsilon_n`$, drives the exchange.
Around a static field, the actual strain stays at the value that the mass prescribes [19, Sec. 6.1].
The exchange lays more material where the actual volume exceeds the natural volume and less where it falls short, so the neutral strain follows the actual strain.
The elastic strain that the field imposes therefore decays at the rate $`1/\tau_c`$, leaving only the uniform residual strain that steady growth sustains.
The fabric thus relaxes its stress at a fixed shape, like a bolt held at a fixed length, rather than creeping under the load.
The actual strain keeps its gradient, so the total field, which the lapse potential of §3.4 gives as $`g = c^2\lvert\nabla\varepsilon\rvert`$, sets the contrast that a renewal event senses.

We identify the catchment length with $`\ell_c = c\ \tau_c`$, the distance that a signal at speed $`c`$ covers within the memory time, a time that matter leaves unchanged.
Its counterpart is anelastic relaxation, in which a solid's memory of a strain decays on a relaxation time that the laboratory measures through internal friction [55].
Fed at speed $`c`$, a detector with such a memory integrates the signals arriving within one memory time of travel, and we call that travel distance, $`\ell_c`$, a telegraph length.
The signal's mediator remains unnamed, an open problem of the microscopic model of a renewal event, and we take its speed as $`c`$, that of transverse waves in the shear continuum.

The contrast is, by convention, the change in $`\varepsilon`$ across a window one catchment length long, centered on the event and laid along the gradient, $`\Delta\varepsilon_{\ell_c} = \lvert\nabla\varepsilon\rvert\ \ell_c`$, with $`\lvert\nabla\varepsilon\rvert`$ averaged along that length.
At a distance $`r`$ from a body, that average departs from the local gradient only by a fraction $`(\ell_c/r)^2/4`$ in an inverse-square field.
For the catchment length of at most 200 pc stated in §3.7, the local field therefore sets the contrast to within one percent beyond 1 kpc from a galaxy's center.

We measure the contrast in units of the per-axis share of the constitutive set point, $`\varepsilon_e^\ast/3`$, the residual strain that the exchange maintains without matter, divided equally among the three axes.
In that unit we call the contrast the drive, the dimensionless measure of the field on which the share of guided events depends,

$$x_s = \frac{\Delta\varepsilon_{\ell_c}}{\varepsilon_e^\ast/3} = \frac{\lvert\nabla\varepsilon\rvert\ c\ \varepsilon_e^\ast/(3H_{\rm dS})}{\varepsilon_e^\ast/3} = \frac{c\ \lvert\nabla\varepsilon\rvert}{H_{\rm dS}} \quad (2)$$

Every constant of the growth interface cancels through the set point, leaving only the rate that defines it, $`H_{\rm dS}`$.
The drive is thus proportional to the total field, $`x_s = g/(cH_{\rm dS})`$, so any field that it singles out, such as the scale of the law, equals $`cH_{\rm dS}`$ times a pure number.

Under statement 1, the threshold $`\theta`$ of an event is a strain drawn from the exponential law

$$p(\theta) = \frac{1}{\bar\theta}\ e^{-\theta/\bar\theta}, \qquad \bar\theta = \frac{\varepsilon_e^\ast}{3\kappa} \quad (3)$$

where $`\bar\theta`$ is the mean threshold and the counting factor, $`\kappa`$, is the per-axis share of the set point divided by the mean threshold.

We adopt as an identification that the mean threshold equals the per-axis share of the constitutive set point, so that $`\kappa = 1`$.
The mean threshold derives from the strain scale of the background renewal, which the homogeneous fabric undergoes without a field.
We identify that scale with the set point, $`\varepsilon_e^\ast`$, rather than with the larger residual strain, $`\varepsilon_e^\ast H/H_{\rm dS}`$, that the exchange holds when matter raises the expansion rate $`H`$ above its floor.
The athermal transformation of steels supplies the counterpart, a constant that Koistinen and Marburger measured as a property of the steel that sets the fraction transformed at a given undercooling [26].
In the drive of Eq. (2), the mean threshold also takes the part of the thermal energy in the supersaturation of crystal growth, $`\Delta\mu/k_BT`$, and the contrast that of $`\Delta\mu`$.

By the floor relation, the per-axis share of the set point, $`\varepsilon_e^\ast/3 = H_{\rm dS}\tau_c`$, is the strain that the floor's growth adds along one axis in one memory time.
The contrast is the strain that the field's gradient spans along one axis over the distance that a signal covers in one memory time.
With $`\kappa = 1`$, an event therefore compares one axis with one axis over one memory time, and Eq. (2) reduces the comparison to one between two rates, $`c\lvert\nabla\varepsilon\rvert`$ and $`H_{\rm dS}`$.
The strain $`\varepsilon`$ is volumetric, the strain on which the lapse depends, and around a body it lies on one axis.
Outside a spherical body the fabric's solution puts the whole volumetric strain on the radius, in the bend [23].
A mean threshold with an independent strain scale would add a fitted constant to the law and break the cancellation of the set point, causing the scale to lose its relation to the de Sitter rate.
We therefore restrict the counting factor to the few discrete values that the geometry of the comparison allows, rather than fitting it to the data.
A mean threshold equal to the whole set point would weigh the strain of the field's one axis against a dilation spread over all three, and it gives $`\kappa = 1/3`$.
A window reaching one catchment length to each side of the event spans two catchment lengths and doubles the contrast, which acts as an effective counting factor of 2 or 2/3.
From the resulting menu of $`\kappa = 1/3`$, 2/3, 1, and 2, we identify $`\kappa = 1`$, which matches the axis and the memory time of the contrast to those of the threshold, while §4.2 fits the law at each value.

The guided share, $`\phi`$, is the probability that the threshold of an event lies below the contrast, so that the event is guided,

$$\phi = \int_0^{\Delta\varepsilon_{\ell_c}} p(\theta)\ d\theta = 1 - e^{-\kappa x_s} \quad (4)$$

Because every event draws its threshold independently, $`\phi`$ is also the share of renewal events in a region, at one instant, that smooth.
No rate and no elapsed time enter it.
Without a field, the drive vanishes, and every event roughens at the rate set by the reservoir, as in the background renewal, whose excess is uniform and defines no axis.
Equation (4) is the result of statement 1, giving the guided share as a function of the drive alone.

Ordinary materials offer precedents for each element of statement 1, motivating its form without deriving it.
The transformed fraction of a steel in its athermal martensitic transformation depends on undercooling alone and saturates exponentially in it [26], just as the guided share of Eq. (4) does in the drive.
Drawing the threshold afresh at each event in Eq. (3) has precedents in the yield thresholds that some elastoplastic models redraw after each event [56] and in the soft glassy rheology model of Sollich, Lequeux, Hébraud, and Cates [57].
There, an element that yields takes a new yield energy, independent of the old one, from a distribution with an exponential tail.
The exponential form of Eq. (3) also has a precedent in the distribution of forces at the wall of a bead pack [58].
Moreover, it is the only continuous law without memory, so a contrast already exceeded says nothing about the margin that remains.
Among laws for a positive threshold of given mean, it is the least informative.

Unlike a crystal, the fabric has no heat bath, so its rates are driven athermally by the agitation that each loosely laid event delivers to its neighbors.
Paper II states the self-noise closure, the requirement that this agitation sustain the exchange.
The origin of the threshold law and of its mean remains open and belongs to the microscopic model of a renewal event.

### 3.3 The second statement: the excess that roughening lays on a layer

We call the region that one renewal event re-lays its renewal domain, or domain.
Under statement 2, covering the two modes, a guided event smooths, meaning it re-lays its domain in alignment with the template and removes all of the domain's excess.
An unguided event roughens, meaning it lays the domain without alignment and adds to the domain's excess the free volume per roughening event, $`\delta_{\text{fv}}`$, measured as a fraction of a unit's natural volume.
We call $`\delta_{\text{fv}}`$ the free volume for short.

By the requirements of §3.1, roughening packs a domain loosely, adding natural volume, since neither the shear continuum nor the template has a state denser than its ordered one.
For the example microstructure, this sign follows from its unit, which leaves sites empty when laid at random, as Paper II shows.
For microstructures in general, the sign is the content of those requirements and is not derived.
Paper II treats how far annealing reduces the error.

Alongside the postulate, we adopt sector selectivity as an identification: a renewal event, guided or not, re-lays only the orientational sector.
This goes beyond the third requirement of §3.1, which exempts the lattice of cells, by exempting the shear continuum as well.
Damage in an ordinary solid provides the counterpart, degrading some of the solid's responses while leaving others intact, as the damage mechanics of Lemaitre [59] describes and measures.
Confined to the orientational sector, the excess therefore enters neither the fabric's shear nor the lattice of cells, which holds matter, light, and gravitational waves.
We also adopt as an identification that a renewal domain is a layer across the field, renewing as a whole and holding its excess along its normal.
Its counterparts are a martensite plate formed in one rapid event [60], the plates of a nickel superalloy lying across the stress axis [61], and a layered crystal swelling along its stacking axis alone [27, 28].
A smoothing lays the layer across the local field, and where the transmitted flux of §3.4 and the gradient differ in direction, Paper II identifies the layer as laid across the flux.
The excess is therefore uniform across a layer.

We call length measured along the material, folds included, the material path.
A roughening event adds $`\delta_{\text{fv}}`$ to its layer along its normal, so a layer holds one plus its excess of material path for each unit of its thickness.
This excess accumulates across re-layings, as the large boosts of galaxy outskirts require.
Over a volume one catchment length across, the material path per unit distance along the field equals one plus the mean excess of the layers there, and we call it the radial eigenstretch, $`\bar\ell'`$, or simply the stretch.
Section 3.4 shows how the stretch acts on the field.
The reason for averaging it over that volume is given in §3.7.

In the steady state, which the rule of the two modes leaves unchanged, the number $`k`$ of roughening events since a layer's last smoothing follows a geometric distribution.
Among the events in a region at one instant, the share $`1 - \phi`$ adds $`\delta_{\text{fv}}`$ each while the share $`\phi`$ removes the mean excess, and the two cancel:

$$\begin{gathered} P(k) = \phi\ (1 - \phi)^k, \qquad \langle k\rangle = \frac{1 - \phi}{\phi} \\
(1 - \phi)\ \delta_{\text{fv}} = \phi\ (\bar\ell' - 1) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \bar\ell' - 1 = \delta_{\text{fv}}\ \frac{\mathcal{U}}{\mathcal{G}}, \qquad \frac{\mathcal{G}}{\mathcal{U}} = \frac{\phi}{1 - \phi} = e^{\kappa x_s} - 1 \end{gathered} \quad (5)$$

Here $`\mathcal{G}`$ and $`\mathcal{U}`$ are the numbers of smoothing and roughening events per domain per unit time.
Only their ratio enters, $`\mathcal{G}/\mathcal{U} = \phi/(1 - \phi)`$, the ratio of guided events to unguided ones, and Eq. (4) turns it into the final expression of Eq. (5).
Set by the reservoir, the total rate of events, $`\Gamma = \mathcal{G} + \mathcal{U}`$, cancels out of Eq. (5), so the steady state requires no clock.

This balance assumes that a smoothing has laid every domain as a layer, as holds in the steady state wherever the field guides the renewal.
As the drive vanishes, the mean excess of Eq. (5) grows without bound, and Paper III [22] treats that outer limit of the law.
The excess in Eq. (5) is natural volume of the orientational sector, to which the net flux of the exchange does not respond, so the relaxation of grown volume at the rate $`1/\tau_c`$ [19] does not affect it.
The excess therefore changes only at renewal events, as the balance assumes.
Because the steady state is a property of the rule rather than of elapsed time, a region that holds it keeps it.
At a given drive, the balance is linear in the excess and thus has a single solution.

### 3.4 The third statement: the excess as a conductivity of the lapse

The incoherence premise holds that a roughening event misaligns each unit of its layer at random, independently of its neighbors, so the bends of the units add up to no coherent displacement across the layer.
We adopt this as an identification because during roughening no contrast above the threshold aligns the units.
An untextured polycrystal serves as its counterpart, since its grains take orientations independent of their neighbors and their anisotropic expansions sum to an isotropic expansion of the solid [52, 53].
Consequently, roughening events cannot buckle the cells, because random bends leave a net strain quadratic in their slope.
The excess is folded within the orientational sector below the cell spacing, and we call that folded material path the crimp.
The crimp neither displaces nor stresses the material; it is an eigenstrain of the material path in the sense of Mura [63], a strain caused by no applied stress.

Rulers and clocks, made of matter whose charged faults belong to the lattice of cells rather than the folded orientational sector, measure distances between points rather than lengths along the folded path.
The two differ only by the crimp, and only their ratio along the field, the stretch, enters the law as a coefficient of the conductivity derived below.
No metric of the material path enters beyond this ratio, and the distances in the equations are those measured by rulers.
Around a body, the crimp lies radially, leaving transverse lengths unaffected, so a sphere of radius $`r`$, as rulers measure it, keeps its area $`4\pi r^2`$.
A radial distance $`\Delta r`$ contains $`\bar\ell'\Delta r`$ of material path.

The inclusion postulate of Tenev and Horstemeyer [18] states that matter of density $`\rho`$ prescribes the strain $`\varepsilon`$.
Their lapse postulate ties local clock rates to the local strain, $`d\tau/dt = (1 + \varepsilon)^{-1}`$.
Without a stretch, the solution of its source equation, $`\nabla^2\varepsilon = -4\pi G\rho/c^2`$, is the classical strain dimple of matter, with the Newtonian potential $`\Phi_N = -c^2\varepsilon`$.
In the open-system model, the static field of a single body is the bend of the fabric alone, and the lapse depends on the bend's own volumetric strain [19, Sec. 3.2].
Clocks respond to that field through the lapse potential $`\Phi = -c^2\varepsilon`$, and light responds as well through the potential $`\Psi`$ of the spatial curvature, defined by the spatial metric $`(1 - 2\Psi/c^2)\ \delta_{ij}`$.
At linear order, the two potentials obey Gauss laws of identical form, the inclusion postulate, $`\nabla^2\Phi = 4\pi G\rho`$, and the Hamiltonian constraint, $`\nabla^2\Psi = 4\pi G\rho`$, with the same source.
This pair is unchanged when $`\Phi`$ and $`\Psi`$ are exchanged and has a unique solution vanishing far from the body, so that solution satisfies $`\Psi = \Phi`$, and lensing and orbits therefore infer the same mass.
We take the static field, by identification, to be transmitted through one local law of the material for both potentials, relating the field at each element of material to that at adjacent elements.
In an ordinary solid, the counterpart is an inclusion whose misfit strain the matrix passes from element to element, as the micromechanics of Mura [63] describes.
That local law keeps its flat form where the material is unfolded, so the material transmits the field with unit conductivity along its path.

Counting paths then gives the conductivity of the crimped material along and across the normal of the layers, a unit axis $`\hat n`$.
Along $`\hat n`$, the flux crosses $`\bar\ell'`$ of material path per unit distance in series, making the conductivity there $`1/\bar\ell'`$.
Across $`\hat n`$, the paths keep their length while the material in a unit cross-section is $`\bar\ell'`$, conducting in parallel, making the conductivity there $`\bar\ell'`$.
Paper II derives this count for a slab of crimped material.
The count is taken over a volume one catchment length across, the scale on which §3.7 identifies the stretch as acting on the field.
The layers within such a volume enter through their mean excess alone.
The flux of each potential therefore obeys the uniaxial conductivity equation,

$$\partial_i\big(C^{ij}\ \partial_j\Phi\big) = \partial_i\big(C^{ij}\ \partial_j\Psi\big) = 4\pi G\hat\rho, \qquad C^{ij} = \bar\ell'^{-1}\ \hat n^i\hat n^j + \bar\ell'\ \big(\delta^{ij} - \hat n^i\hat n^j\big) \quad (6)$$

where $`\hat\rho`$ is the mass per unit volume as rulers measure it, the density measured by photometry.
The stretch thus reweights the differential operator while leaving the matter source unchanged.
We call $`C^{ij}`$ the conductivity because Eq. (6) takes the form of steady conduction, even though nothing in the fabric conducts heat or charge.

Because this path count depends only on the material path rather than on the potential whose flux crosses it, Eq. (6) preserves the pair's symmetry under the exchange of $`\Phi`$ and $`\Psi`$.
Its two equations differ by $`\partial_i[C^{ij}\partial_j(\Psi - \Phi)] = 0`$, and because $`C^{ij}`$ is positive definite, the only solution vanishing far from the body is $`\Psi = \Phi`$.
The two potentials are therefore equal for any conductivity and in any static geometry, and the stretch enters the spatial metric at order $`\Phi/c^2`$ through the flux, just as the bend of a mass does.

The excess varies from layer to layer, since each layer holds the roughenings accumulated since its last smoothing.
These differences are large: at the Sun's position in the Galaxy, three layers in ten hold no excess while the mean excess is 0.44.
Even so, the field exhibits no jump between neighboring layers.
Under the scale-selective coupling of §3.7, the stretch acts on the field only through its mean over a volume one catchment length across, and Paper II estimates that such a volume holds at least 6,050 domains.
A single layer therefore enters the field as one share among thousands, and its departure from the mean shifts the field by about 0.006 percent rms at the Sun and 0.016 percent at a boost of 30.
The shares within one volume are independent and add in quadrature.
The field then differs from one such volume to the next by at most 0.5 percent at the Sun and 1.3 percent at a boost of 30.
The layers impart no larger variation to the field on any scale, because below a catchment length the field does not follow the stretch, while larger regions average further.
A system smaller than a catchment length encounters this variation as an almost uniform field, changing across the $`10^4`$ astronomical units of a wide binary by less than $`10^{-3}`$ of the pair's internal acceleration.
These values hold at a free volume of 0.19 and for the largest domains allowed by Paper II's estimate.
By Paper II, each layer also renews 1 to 3.4 times per memory time, and a catchment length of at most 200 pc places the memory time at about 650 yr or less.
Over an orbit lasting some $`10^8`$ yr, the pattern of layers is therefore redrawn many times, averaging the departures still further.
This variation thus adds less than 0.006 dex to the scatter of the radial-acceleration relation, whose intrinsic scatter lies below 0.057 dex [3].

Written on a metric stretched by $`\bar\ell'`$ along $`\hat n`$, the covariant inclusion postulate of Tenev and Horstemeyer [20] becomes the law for $`\Phi`$ in Eq. (6) once multiplied by the square root of the metric's determinant.
That factor also turns its density into $`\hat\rho`$.
The covariant field equation and the conductivity equation are therefore the exact same equation, with nothing linearized or dropped.
The open-system model derives the source equation beneath Eq. (6) only outside a single spherical body [19, Sec. 3.2].
For combined sources, such as a galaxy's stars and gas, the equation rests on the postulate alone.

In spherical symmetry, where the axis is radial, Eq. (6) reduces to a local relation between the stretch at a radius and the boost there, which we call the readout.
Applying Gauss's law to Eq. (6) over a spherical shell of radius $`r`$ sets the transmitted flux by the enclosed mass $`M`$:

$$\begin{gathered} 4\pi r^2\ \bar\ell'^{-1}\ \Phi' = 4\pi G M \\
\therefore\quad \Phi' = \frac{G M\ \bar\ell'}{r^2} = \bar\ell'\ g_N, \qquad v^2 = r\ \Phi' = \frac{G M\ \bar\ell'}{r} \end{gathered} \quad (7)$$

which is exact apart from the weak-field approximation.
Because the mass sets the flux rather than the gradient of the lapse, the stretch equals the boost, $`\nu = g/g_N`$.
Here the boost is the ratio of the total acceleration $`g = \Phi'`$ to the baryons' Newtonian acceleration, $`g_N`$.
The boost's inverse, $`\mu = 1/\nu`$, is MOND's interpolation function, written $`\mu(X)`$ with an argument, whereas $`\mu_0`$ denotes the shear modulus.

For the metric $`ds^2 = -(1 + 2\Phi/c^2)\ c^2dt^2 + B\ dr^2 + r^2 d\Omega^2`$, the radial component of the geodesic equation [20], applied to a circular orbit, balances the clock term against the centripetal term.
Both terms contain the common factor $`1/B`$, which cancels, so the Doppler tangential speed satisfies $`v^2 = r\ \Phi'`$, the final expression in Eq. (7), up to lapse corrections of order $`\Phi/c^2`$, whatever the value of $`B`$.
Under the conductivity interpretation, in which the stretch enters $`C^{ij}`$ rather than the measuring instruments, the stretch acts on test particles as additional enclosed mass.
A Newtonian observer consequently infers an unseen mass component equal to $`(\bar\ell' - 1)\ M`$.

Observations of light exclude the alternative to the conductivity interpretation, namely a physical stretch of the radial metric that rulers would measure.
For a radial stretch $`\bar\ell'`$ of a few, the size of the boosts in galaxy outskirts, such a metric stretch would deflect a passing light ray through a radian or more.
That exceeds the arcsecond deflection produced by a galaxy by roughly five orders of magnitude.
Moreover, such a stretch would affect slow orbits only at order $`(v/c)^2`$, so light would show a large boost that stars would not feel.
Around isolated galaxies, weak lensing instead extends to larger radii the same radial-acceleration relation traced by rotation curves [32, 64], showing that light and stars experience boosts of the same size.

Excluding a ruler stretch leaves the conductivity interpretation, which remains an identification because the exclusion removes one alternative rather than every conceivable one.
Its counterpart is a laminate whose layers alter its conductivity while leaving its dimensions unchanged.
A laminate conducts at the harmonic mean of its layers along its stacking axis and at the arithmetic mean across it, matching the two coefficients of Eq. (6) and the two Wiener bounds reviewed by Milton [52].
Light responds to the spatial part of the weak-field metric just as strongly as to the lapse, whereas slow orbits respond to the lapse alone.
Because Eq. (6) yields $`\Psi = \Phi`$, lensing exhibits the same boost, and the masses inferred by lensing and by orbits are equal, the optical equality, at linear order in static fields.
Like the conductivity interpretation, taking the static field as a local law of the material is an identification supported through elimination by this exclusion.

Under the conductivity interpretation, neither the distances to the center of a gravitating body nor the transverse directions are physically stretched.
The abscissa of a rotation curve, an angular offset converted into a transverse distance, therefore tracks the true radius, while the boost resides solely in the Doppler velocity through $`v^2 = r\ \Phi'`$.

### 3.5 The law and its acceleration scale

The boost law is the relation between the total field and the baryons' field set jointly by the balance of Eq. (5) and the readout of Eq. (7).
In MOND's units, the drive enters as $`X = g/a_0`$, proportional to $`x_s`$.
The scale $`a_0`$ is the crossover field between the weak-field and Newtonian regimes, defined, as in MOND's simple function, by a steady excess of one and a boost of 2.
Because the excess equals one at $`X = 1`$, the exponent of Eq. (5) is $`\kappa\ x_s = \lambda X`$, where $`\lambda = \ln(1+\delta_{\text{fv}})`$.
We call the ratio $`\mathcal{G}/\mathcal{U} = e^{\lambda X} - 1`$ the guidance law, with $`\lambda`$ as its constant, because the drive enters it only through the guided share.
Linear in the drive where the guided share is small, it hardens exponentially as the share saturates.
The guided share at the crossover is $`\delta_{\text{fv}}/(1 + \delta_{\text{fv}})`$, or 0.16 at a free volume of 0.19, the value at which we evaluate derived quantities.

The law sets the value of the stretch at each point by the local drive, $`\bar\ell' = \nu(X)`$, involving neither spatial gradients of the stretch nor the history by which a region reached the steady state.
Combined with the guidance law, Eq. (5) gives the boost as an algebraic function of the drive,

$$\nu(X) = 1 + \frac{\delta_{\text{fv}}}{e^{\lambda X} - 1}, \qquad \mu(X) = \frac{e^{\lambda X} - 1}{e^{\lambda X} - 1 + \delta_{\text{fv}}} \quad (8)$$

forming the simple interpolation family in total-field form, which reduces to MOND's simple function at vanishing free volume.
Because $`X = Y\nu`$, where $`Y = g_N/a_0`$ is the baryons' field in the same units, the boost at a given baryonic field follows from solving $`Y = X\mu(X)`$ for $`X`$.
That equation has a unique solution, since $`X\mu(X)`$ rises monotonically with $`X`$.

We derive the deep-MOND exponent from the threshold law, whose probability density is finite at zero contrast and thus makes the guided share proportional to the drive in weak fields.
As a corollary of the postulate, renewal is blind to the provenance of a field: each event compares the strain contrast physically present against its threshold, whatever produced it.
By a second corollary, renewal couples to the total field rather than to a counterfactual field of the baryons alone, so the steady state closes on $`X`$ itself.
Where the guided share is small, the guidance law is linear, $`\mathcal{G}/\mathcal{U} \approx \lambda X`$, giving $`\nu - 1 = 1/(sX)`$ with the dilute slope $`s = \lambda/\delta_{\text{fv}}`$.
This represents an exponent of $`-1`$ in the mechanism's own argument.
Yet the radial-acceleration relation is plotted against the baryonic field, $`Y = X/\nu`$.
Deep in the weak-field regime, where $`\nu \gg 1`$, substituting $`X = Y\nu`$ into $`\nu \approx 1/(sX)`$ yields $`\nu^2 = 1/(sY)`$ and

$$\nu = (sY)^{-1/2} \quad (Y \ll 1) \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad g = \sqrt{a_0\ g_N/s} \quad (9)$$

which recovers the deep limb with exponent $`-1/2`$ exactly and, with it, the baryonic Tully–Fisher relation $`v^4 = (a_0/s)\ GM`$.
The deep normalization is therefore $`a_0/s`$, whereas the crossover defines $`a_0`$.
A threshold density vanishing at zero contrast as a power law would give $`\mathcal{G}/\mathcal{U} \propto X^p`$ in weak fields and produce a deep exponent of $`-p/(1+p)`$.
The measured exponent $`-1/2`$ therefore directly encodes $`p = 1`$, the finite density of the threshold law at zero contrast.

While the balance admits any threshold distribution, the exponential form of the postulate selects the guidance law.
Given that form, the interpolation function of the law becomes a one-parameter family.
Near the crossover, the steady state lies close to MOND's simple function, $`\nu(X) = 1 + 1/X`$, equivalently $`\mu(X) = X/(1+X)`$, and equals it at $`X = 1`$.
At small $`X`$, it has the dilute slope $`s`$, equal to 0.92 at a free volume of 0.19.
At large $`X`$, it hardens toward Newtonian dynamics, with an onset $`X_{\text{onset}} = \xi_\ast/\lambda`$ set by the same free volume.
We call the range of fields below this hardening, where the law departs from Newtonian dynamics, the live regime.
By the convention adopted for the onset, the constant $`\xi_\ast \approx 0.657`$ is the value of $`\lambda X`$ at which the guidance law reaches $`\sqrt2`$ times its linear extrapolation.
It is the root of $`e^{\xi_\ast} - 1 = \sqrt2\ \xi_\ast`$ rather than a fitted number.
At the inferred free volume of about 0.19, this onset lies at $`X = 3.8`$.

The curve's shape depends only weakly on the functional form of the threshold law.
With a half-normal threshold law of the same mean and the scale evaluated at its own crossover field, the total field at any baryonic field departs from Eq. (8) by at most 0.017 dex.
Over baryonic fields from $`10^{-3}`$ to 10 times the scale, a uniform threshold law departs by at most 0.035 dex.
At a free volume of 0.19, however, the crossover falls at a drive $`x_s`$ of 0.174, 0.25, and 0.32 for the exponential, half-normal, and uniform laws respectively.
Only the exponential law therefore relates the scale to the free volume in the clean form of Eq. (10).

The acceleration scale is the crossover field, placed by the guidance law at $`X = 1`$.
In physical units, it equals the floor's acceleration divided by a divisor, $`\mathcal{D}`$,

$$a_0 = \frac{cH_{\rm dS}}{\mathcal{D}}, \qquad \mathcal{D} = \frac{\kappa}{\ln(1+\delta_{\text{fv}})} \quad (10)$$

containing no free constant beyond the free volume once the counting factor $`\kappa`$ is set.
The counting factor of Eq. (3) is a pure number, the ratio of two strains.
In §3.2, identifying the catchment length as a telegraph length makes the drive $`x_s = g/(cH_{\rm dS})`$ via Eq. (2), and identifying the mean threshold with the per-axis share of the set point gives $`\kappa = 1`$.
Both steps are identifications, and $`\kappa = 1`$ also rests on the convention that the contrast spans one catchment length, selecting 1 rather than 2 from the menu of §3.2.
With $`\kappa = 1`$, the divisor is $`\mathcal{D} = 1/\ln(1+\delta_{\text{fv}})`$.
The scale then becomes $`a_0 = cH_{\rm dS}\ln(1+\delta_{\text{fv}})`$, containing a single material constant, the free volume, which sets how far below the floor's acceleration, $`cH_{\rm dS}`$, the scale lies.
Setting the dilute slope and the hardening onset as well, the free volume is the one material parameter that the law adds to the open-system model.

The scale thus rests on two identifications that tie it to the de Sitter rate in a relation from which the unknown set point cancels.
The set point itself is determined by the interface constants that set the floor of the expansion rate.
Consequently, the acceleration scale tracks the de Sitter rate and remains frozen in cosmic time.
The empirical coincidence of $`a_0`$ with the cosmic expansion rate thus becomes two expressions of one underlying material rate.
Through Eq. (10), any numerical scale corresponds to some value of the free volume, so this relation explains the coincidence only insofar as the required free volume is physically plausible for a material.
Galaxy rotation curves imply a free volume near 0.19, though they do not measure it independently of the scale, as §4.2 shows.
Compared against the standard Friedmann equation, Eq. (1) equates the expansion floor to the cosmological-constant rate, $`H_{\rm dS} = c\sqrt{\Lambda/3}`$.
The numerical coincidence between $`a_0`$ and $`\Lambda`$ highlighted by Famaey and McGaugh [6] is therefore structural as well.
Had the mean threshold instead tracked the present residual strain, the scale would evolve as $`cH(z)/\mathcal{D}`$, lying 21 percent higher today than the frozen scale.
The inferred free volume would absorb that difference, so only the evolution is testable, through high-redshift rotation curves.

We evaluate the divisor and the product $`\mathcal{D}\ln(1+\delta_{\text{fv}})`$ using the de Sitter rate $`H_{\rm dS} = \sqrt{\Omega_\Lambda}\ H_0`$, where $`H_0`$ is the present Hubble constant.
Adopting $`\Omega_\Lambda = 0.6847`$ from the Planck 2018 fit [51] gives $`\sqrt{\Omega_\Lambda} = 0.8275`$ and $`H_0 = 1.2085\ H_{\rm dS}`$.
The measured value of $`H_0`$ enters as our cosmological input through $`H_{\rm dS}`$, spanning from 67.4 km/s/Mpc in the Planck fit to 73 km/s/Mpc on the local distance ladder [65].

In our primary fits, we impose the scale relation, the link given by Eq. (10) between the acceleration scale and the free volume.
The law then has a single fitted constant, the scale, which sets the free volume.
For $`\kappa = 1`$, this scale relation ties the cosmic expansion floor directly to the free volume inferred in galaxies:

$$\mathcal{D}\ \ln(1+\delta_{\text{fv}}) = 1, \qquad \mathcal{D} = cH_{\rm dS}/a_0 \quad (11)$$

This relation applies specifically to the scale of the guidance law, whose crossover, $`\mathcal{G} = \delta_{\text{fv}}\ \mathcal{U}`$, defines $`a_0`$.
It therefore holds for a scale fitted using Eq. (8), rather than one fitted with a different interpolation function.

### 3.6 The field equation of the steady state

In the steady state, the boost law closes the conductivity equation.
Setting the stretch to its steady-state value at the local total field turns Eq. (6) into the field equation of the steady state,

$$\partial_i\big(C^{ij}\ \partial_j\Phi\big) = 4\pi G\hat\rho, \qquad \bar\ell' = \nu(\lvert\nabla\Phi\rvert/a_0) \quad (12)$$

This closure uses the total field because a decomposition by source is not a local physical quantity that an interface could sense.
The stretch amplifies the field of the matter present while exerting no force of its own.
Equation (12) holds on scales above the catchment length, where the field averaged over one catchment length matches the local field, as estimated in §3.2.
In spherical symmetry, Eq. (7) yields the algebraic form of the law, $`g = \bar\ell'\ g_N`$ with $`\bar\ell' = \nu(g/a_0)`$, exact for a spherical body, which we term the algebraic relation.
Our rotation-curve fits apply this algebraic relation to galactic disks as an approximation to the full field equation, an approximation we adopt as an input.
Section 4 thus tests the algebraic relation, whereas Paper II tests the field equation solved in full.

Off spherical symmetry, wherever the layer normal aligns with the field, the flux runs purely along that normal and only the axial conductivity coefficient acts.
Because $`1/\bar\ell' = \mu`$, Eq. (12) then reduces to exact AQUAL [14] with our model's interpolation function,

$$\nabla\cdot\big[\mu(\lvert\nabla\Phi\rvert/a_0)\ \nabla\Phi\big] = 4\pi G\hat\rho \quad (13)$$

a reduction that follows once layer alignment, an identification, is adopted.
Rafting in nickel superalloys provides its counterpart: Tien and Copley [61] showed that precipitates in superalloy crystals annealed under tension coarsen into plates lying perpendicular to the stress axis.
Paper II demonstrates that this aligned state is a fixed point of the renewal, stable wherever the axis is uniform, and builds the non-spherical field theory upon it.

### 3.7 The Newtonian screen below the catchment length

Because the drive is a strain contrast across one catchment length, the law responds to the field averaged over that length rather than to fine structure on smaller scales.
A system smaller than a catchment length cannot elevate the guided share around itself, because its own field gradients vary on scales below $`\ell_c`$ and are diluted by the catchment-length average.
By the definition in §3.2, the contrast is an average along a line segment one catchment length long.
Along such a line extending from the edge of a body of size $`R \ll \ell_c`$, the body's own field averages down to a fraction of order $`R/\ell_c`$ of its edge value.
Averaged over a three-dimensional volume one catchment length across, as required by the scale-selective coupling below, Gauss's law reduces that same field to at most a fraction of order $`(R/\ell_c)^2`$ of its edge value.
Renewal guidance around such a compact system is thus governed entirely by the ambient external field, so the small system develops no local stretch pattern of its own.

We further identify the field coupling as scale-selective: the stretch acts on the field averaged over a volume one catchment length across, via its own mean over that volume, while field structure on finer scales couples to nothing.
Two laboratory phenomena provide counterparts, one for each spatial average.
First, in a nanocrystalline ferromagnet, grains finer than the magnetic exchange length act on the magnetization only through their mean anisotropy across an exchange volume.
With randomly oriented grains, that mean equals a single grain's anisotropy divided by the square root of the number of grains in the volume, causing the measured coercivity to scale as the sixth power of the grain size [66].
The field variation estimated in §3.4 is a residual of this exact type.
Second, in a pure metal at low temperature, the conduction current responds to the electric field averaged over an electron's mean free path [67].
A high-frequency field confined to a surface skin much thinner than that path drives a current approaching a limit independent of the path, the anomalous skin effect measured by Chambers [68].
Expressed as a split of the stored gravitational energy, scale-selective coupling places a soft AQUAL term on the volume-averaged field while keeping bare Newtonian stiffness on the small-scale remainder.
Because the spatial average uses the same kernel at every point, this split is translationally invariant, as conservation of momentum requires, while its nonlocal field equation, and so its conservation laws, are not written out in this series.

Under scale-selective coupling, a system smaller than the catchment length moves in the ambient external field plus its own unboosted Newtonian field, which the ambient stretch does not rescale.
The alternative, an ambient-stretch coupling, would let the anisotropic conductivity tensor $`C^{ij}`$ of Eq. (6) act directly on the compact system's internal field as well.
Because the layers around the Sun align with the Milky Way's gravitational field, the Sun's own field along the axis toward the Galactic center would then be reduced to $`1/\bar\ell'`$ of its transverse field.
At the Sun's Galactocentric radius, where the mean excess is 0.44, that axial-to-transverse ratio would be 0.69, an anisotropy ruled out by planetary ephemerides.
If the layer axis instead rotated to follow the compact system's internal field, the ambient stretch would rescale its internal dynamics uniformly.
Globular clusters test this isotropic form of the coupling, and Paper II shows that their velocities favor scale-selective coupling over it.
This Newtonian screen endows the field equation with a short-distance cutoff absent from standard AQUAL.
Whereas unmodified AQUAL alters gravity down to arbitrarily small scales, our model behaves as AQUAL for systems larger than the catchment length and reduces to Newtonian gravity embedded in a uniform external field for smaller ones.

Observational bounds bracket the catchment length, $`\ell_c = c\ \varepsilon_e^\ast/(3H_{\rm dS})`$, because the open-system model does not yet derive the set point $`\varepsilon_e^\ast`$.
From above, classical dwarf spheroidal galaxies, with half-light radii of 0.2 to 1 kpc, set an upper bound: because their internal kinematics follow the boost law [6], their radii must exceed the catchment length, requiring $`\ell_c \lesssim 200`$ pc.
From below, the catchment length must exceed every compact stellar system that remains Newtonian despite internal accelerations low enough to trigger a boost.
The largest such systems are the globular clusters, whose kinematics are traced out to tens of parsecs and show none of the 1.02 to 2.13 boosts predicted by MOND (Table 1).
Together, these bounds leave an empirical window of roughly 30 to 200 pc.
Both boundaries are estimates, and the relaxation kinetics of Paper II place a floor of 58 pc inside this window for the shortest equilibration time adopted there.

The Sun's crossover shell, where solar gravity drops to the fitted scale $`a_0`$, has a radius near 0.038 pc, about 0.076 pc across, while Solar neighborhood wide binaries span 0.01 to 0.15 pc.
Across the entire 30 to 200 pc window, both systems lie below the catchment length by factors of roughly 200 to 20,000.
Even at a 30 pc catchment length, a wide binary $`10^4`$ astronomical units across retains only about $`2\times10^{-3}`$ of its internal field in a line average from its edge, and at most $`3\times10^{-6}`$ in a volume average.
Consequently, the Milky Way's ambient field at the Sun, roughly $`2\times10^{-10}\ \mathrm{m\ s^{-2}}`$, governs renewal throughout the Solar neighborhood.
Wherever the catchment length sits within its empirical window, the screen and scale-selective coupling keep both Solar System and wide-binary internal fields Newtonian.
The model therefore predicts a null boost in wide binaries, where current Gaia analyses conflict [33, 34].

The ultra-faint dwarf galaxies Segue 1 and Reticulum II have circularized half-light radii of about 21 and 32 pc [69, 70], below and at the lower edge of the window.
Yet both exhibit velocity dispersions an order of magnitude above Newtonian expectations [37, 38].
Lowering the catchment length enough to leave Segue 1 unscreened would simultaneously unscreen globular clusters of comparable size, which appear Newtonian, while unscreening Reticulum II would need a catchment length below Paper II's adopted floor of 58 pc.
The screen therefore predicts Newtonian internal dynamics for both ultra-faint dwarfs, leaving their observed velocity dispersions as an unexplained surplus.
If future observations confirm that those dispersions reflect equilibrium dynamics free of binary contamination or tidal disruption, they would falsify the screen or the uniformity that §2.5 requires of the content beyond bodies.

## 4. The law in galaxies: the rotation curves

### 4.1 The sample and the fit methodology

Our primary galaxy test uses the SPARC database of Lelli, McGaugh, and Schombert [1], comprising 175 disk galaxies with homogeneous 3.6 μm surface photometry.
Applying the standard quality cuts, which remove the lowest quality flag, $`Q = 3`$, and face-on inclinations below $`30^\circ`$, leaves 153 galaxies containing 3,168 velocity points.
Our fits use the 3,165 points having positive net baryonic acceleration.
Throughout this section, "the law" refers to the algebraic relation, $`g = \nu(g_N/a_0)\ g_N`$, evaluated on SPARC's published mass models.
For thin disks, this algebraic relation approximates the AQUAL field equation rather than solving it exactly, as Brada and Milgrom [71] demonstrated, and it similarly approximates Eq. (13).

At each radial point $`R`$, the predicted circular speed is $`V_{\text{pred}} = \sqrt{\nu(g_{\text{bar}}/a_0)\ g_{\text{bar}}\ R}`$, using the published baryonic decomposition $`g_{\text{bar}} = g_{\text{gas}} + \Upsilon_d\ g_{\text{disk}} + \Upsilon_b\ g_{\text{bulge}}`$.
For a total-field law such as Eq. (8), $`\nu(g_{\text{bar}}/a_0)`$ is evaluated at the drive $`X`$ solving $`Y = X\mu(X)`$.
Each velocity point is weighted by $`1/\sigma_{\text{pt}}^2`$, where $`\sigma_{\text{pt}}^2 = \sigma_V^2 + (0.05\ V_{\text{obs}})^2`$ combines the reported velocity uncertainty $`\sigma_V`$ in quadrature with a five-percent velocity error floor.
By the estimate in §3.2, the drive averaged over one catchment length matches the local field to within one percent beyond 1 kpc from a galactic center.
Inside 1 kpc lie 309 of the 3,168 points across 111 galaxies, with 124 points inside 0.5 kpc where the departure exceeds 4 percent for a 200 pc catchment length.
For simplicity, we evaluate the drive using the local field at those inner points as well.

Each galaxy enters the global objective through an individual profile likelihood.
Its stellar mass-to-light ratios $`\Upsilon_\star`$, comprising the disk ratio $`\Upsilon_d`$ and, for the 31 bulged galaxies, the bulge ratio $`\Upsilon_b`$, are profiled under lognormal priors of width 0.10 dex [3], centered at 0.5 and 0.7 $`M_\odot/L_\odot`$ at 3.6 μm respectively.
Following Li, Lelli, McGaugh, and Schombert [3], we treat the galaxy distance factor, $`f = D/D_{\text{cat}}`$, and disk inclination, $`i`$, as nuisance parameters constrained by Gaussian priors around their catalog values.
Rescaling distance scales luminosity as $`D^2`$ and radius as $`D`$, leaving $`g_{\text{bar}}`$ invariant point by point while scaling $`V_{\text{pred}}`$ as $`\sqrt f`$; rescaling inclination scales $`V_{\text{obs}}`$, $`\sigma_V`$, and the five-percent floor alike by $`\sin i_{\text{cat}}/\sin i`$.
Both geometric nuisances thus enter the velocity residual through a single combined factor $`q`$:

$$\chi^2_{\text{pt}} = \big(V_{\text{obs}} - q\ V_{\text{pred}}\big)^2/\sigma_{\text{pt}}^2, \qquad q = \sqrt{f}\ \frac{\sin i}{\sin i_{\text{cat}}} \quad (14)$$

reducing each galaxy to a profile optimization over $`\Upsilon_d`$ and $`q`$, plus $`\Upsilon_b`$ when present.
At each trial value of $`q`$, the galaxy's $`\chi^2`$ includes the minimum prior penalty achievable by splitting $`q`$ into $`f`$ and $`i`$:

$$\chi^2_{\text{prior}}(q) = \min_{i}\Big[\Big(\frac{f - 1}{\sigma_f}\Big)^2 + \Big(\frac{i - i_{\text{cat}}}{\sigma_i}\Big)^2\Big], \qquad f = \Big(\frac{q\ \sin i_{\text{cat}}}{\sin i}\Big)^2 \quad (15)$$

where $`\sigma_f`$ and $`\sigma_i`$ are the catalog uncertainties on distance and inclination.
A golden-section search bounded within four catalog standard deviations locates this minimum.
Across the sample, the median fitted shifts of the interpolation functions of record, $`f = 0.985`$ to 1.002 and $`\Delta i = -0.15^\circ`$ to $`-0.10^\circ`$, sit comfortably inside their priors.

Optimization for each galaxy begins with a coarse grid search, refines every local minimum, and iterates until successive passes improve the objective by less than $`10^{-7}`$.
Once the summed galaxy profiles converge across trial values of the acceleration scale, fitting a parabola in $`\log a_0`$ yields the best-fit scale $`a_0`$ for each competing interpolation function.
Our constrained fit steps along the scale relation $`a_0 = cH_{\rm dS}\ln(1+\delta_{\text{fv}})`$ across free volumes from 0.05 to 0.45 in increments of 0.01, refitting every galaxy's nuisance parameters at each step and interpolating the minimum via a three-point parabola.
In the unconstrained two-constant fit testing Eq. (11) in §4.2, $`a_0`$ is instead optimized freely at each grid value of $`\delta_{\text{fv}}`$.
Imposing Eq. (11) leaves the boost law with a single global parameter, $`a_0`$, matching the single fitted scale and nuisance freedoms of each competing function.
Every $`\Delta\chi^2`$ between the interpolation functions below therefore compares models with identical numbers of free parameters, while the square-root form and the two-constant fit each add one constant.
Each $`\Delta\chi^2`$ is the competing function's $`\chi^2`$ minus that of Eq. (8), so positive values favor our law.

Purely statistical uncertainties correspond to the parabolic half-width at $`\Delta\chi^2 = 1`$.
To account for correlated residuals along individual rotation curves, we compute galaxy-clustered uncertainties from the dispersion of $`\Delta\chi^2`$ and parameter estimates across 2,000 bootstrap resamplings of the 153 galaxies, refitting each model along its stored galaxy profiles.
Because points within a single galaxy correlate strongly, these clustered errors are two to three times as wide as naive $`\Delta\chi^2 = 1`$ intervals.
An analytic sandwich estimator agrees with the bootstrap widths to within 5 percent on primary margins and 20 percent elsewhere.
Unless noted otherwise, all significances quoted below use galaxy-clustered uncertainties.

To check systematic robustness, we evaluate sixteen analysis variants at both $`H_0 = 67.4`$ and $`73`$ km/s/Mpc, combining four treatments of stellar mass-to-light ratios with two choices each for geometric nuisances and inclination cuts:

1. Three mass-to-light treatments either retain the standard 0.10 dex priors, lock $`\Upsilon_b = 1.4\ \Upsilon_d`$, or widen both priors to 0.25 dex; the fourth pins both ratios within 0.01 dex to the fixed stellar-population values of McGaugh, Lelli, and Schombert [2].
2. Distances and inclinations are either profiled under their priors or held fixed at catalog values.
3. The galaxy sample is either the full 153-galaxy set or the subset inclined at $`45^\circ`$ or steeper.

Note that the best-fit value of $`a_0`$ shifts systematically with the choice of regression objective.
When we pin mass-to-light ratios and hold catalog distances and inclinations fixed to match McGaugh, Lelli, and Schombert [2], their empirical radial-acceleration function, $`\nu(Y) = 1/(1 - e^{-\sqrt{Y}})`$, fits at $`a_0 = 0.96\times10^{-10}\ \mathrm{m\ s^{-2}}`$ in our pipeline, compared with their published $`1.20\times10^{-10}\ \mathrm{m\ s^{-2}}`$.
Whereas we minimize velocity residuals, with a five-percent floor, across all 3,165 positive-acceleration points, they fitted the relation after cutting points with velocity errors exceeding ten percent.
On the same galaxies, with and without the published cut, switching between a velocity and a logarithmic objective shifts the fitted scale between $`0.96`$ and $`1.34\times10^{-10}\ \mathrm{m\ s^{-2}}`$, bracketing the published figure.
Via Eq. (10), $`a_0 = 1.20\times10^{-10}\ \mathrm{m\ s^{-2}}`$ corresponds to $`\delta_{\text{fv}} = 0.25`$ at $`H_0 = 67.4`$ km/s/Mpc.

### 4.2 The fits to the SPARC rotation curves

We first test the functional shape of the interpolation law.
Because Eq. (8) reduces to MOND's simple function, $`\nu(Y) = 1/2 + (1/4 + 1/Y)^{1/2}`$, as $`\delta_{\text{fv}} \to 0`$, comparing the two models with one fitted global constant each isolates the high-acceleration exponential hardening.
Our constrained law achieves $`\chi^2 = 4323.2`$, compared with 4350.3 for the simple function.
This improvement, $`\Delta\chi^2 = +27.1 \pm 17.2`$, or 1.6σ, is modest, meaning SPARC rotation curves alone do not distinguish the two shapes decisively.
Holding catalog distances and inclinations fixed increases the law's lead over the simple function to $`+63 \pm 26`$, or 2.4σ.
Compared against the one-parameter empirical function of the radial-acceleration relation (RAR), our law leads by an insignificant $`\Delta\chi^2 = +3.5 \pm 6.7`$, or 0.5σ, with the two functional curves agreeing to 0.009 dex rms across the data range.
In all eight robustness variants that hold catalog distances fixed, however, the empirical function outperforms our law by 8.9 to 58.4 in $`\chi^2`$, or 1.0σ to 3.0σ.
MOND's steeper standard function, $`\mu(X) = X/\sqrt{1+X^2}`$, trails our law by $`\Delta\chi^2 = +168 \pm 77`$, or 2.2σ, a secondary comparison, since rotation curves had already preferred the simple function over the standard one [6, 72, 73].
Overall residual scatter around our constrained law is 0.110 dex, matching the baseline observational uncertainties (Figure 4).

## 5. Discussion

### 5.1 The claims and their limits

We claim a physical derivation of the boost law.
Its deep-MOND exponent of $`-1/2`$ follows from the finite probability density of the renewal threshold distribution at zero strain contrast, while its functional shape across the live regime follows from the exponential form of that distribution.
Given two physical identifications, we also tie its acceleration scale directly to the cosmic de Sitter floor.
Our third primary claim is the short-distance Newtonian screen below the catchment length, bracketed empirically between roughly 30 and 200 pc.
Across galaxy rotation curves, renewal boost suffices without a second source.
Imposing the theoretical scale relation leaves the boost law, like MOND's simple function, with a single globally fitted constant, $`a_0`$.
Our constrained fits combine the cosmological floor rate $`H_{\rm dS}`$ with $`a_0`$ to infer the material free volume $`\delta_{\text{fv}}`$, whereas our unconstrained two-constant check, whose fit uses no cosmological input, tests that cosmological link.
All of these results describe the time-independent steady state maintained by renewal, which Paper II shows is regained rapidly after a disturbance.

Importantly, the analogs of the postulate and of the layer differ from the cosmic fabric both in how they are driven and in what they store.
Steels transform once upon cooling and glasses yield under applied shear stress, whereas the fabric renews continuously without temperature, comparing a spatial strain contrast across one catchment length against a random threshold at every event.
At the wall of a granular bead pack, the exponential distribution governs contact forces rather than yield thresholds.
Irradiated metals swell by collecting surplus vacancies into voids and intercalated hosts swell by absorbing guest species, whereas roughening lays the fabric's own units out of alignment.
Above all, no laboratory solid undergoes volumetric renewal through continuous exchange with an embedding higher-dimensional fluid.
These condensed-matter analogs therefore motivate the individual mechanisms separately, while the Threshold Renewal postulate that unites them in a single renewal event remains an explicit postulate.

Similarly, the experimental counterparts of our nine identifications correspond only partially to the fabric elements they represent.
The counterparts of the mean threshold are thermal or energetic scales of their respective media rather than dimensionless strains, meaning the per-axis factor of 1/3 rests purely on the geometry of the comparison.
Superalloy rafts and martensite plates form under applied stress or thermal undercooling rather than a nonlocal strain contrast.
Neither nanocrystalline ferromagnets nor pure metals combine both spatial averages of our scale-selective coupling at a single length scale in a static field.

Our conclusions are subject to six explicit limitations:

1. The best-fit scale and inferred free volume characterize the algebraic approximation evaluated on SPARC's mass models.
Solving the full disk field equation degrades the fit relative to the algebraic relation and, on the model's own field, trails MOND's unhardened simple function.
2. When galaxy distances are held fixed at their catalog values, the empirical RAR function fits better than Eq. (8).
3. At one fitted parameter apiece, SPARC rotation curves do not statistically separate the shape of Eq. (8) from either MOND's simple function or the empirical function, nor do they constrain the free volume independently of $`a_0`$.
4. The short-distance screen leaves the ultra-faint dwarfs Segue 1 and Reticulum II with large velocity dispersions supplied by neither source; if confirmed as equilibrium dynamical dispersions, they falsify the screen or the uniformity of the content beyond bodies.
5. Equality between gravitational lensing and dynamical masses holds at linear order in static fields via the identification of a shared material conductivity law for $`\Phi`$ and $`\Psi`$, whereas moving lenses rely on Paper II.
Cosmological background perturbations remain open.
6. Nothing is derived here for structure beyond bodies, and both the readout and the scale relation rest on seven unproved material identifications.

### 5.2 The relativistic status and the fabric's frame

Our boost law governs both the lapse and the spatial curvature of the static, weak-field metric at linear order.
Because the conductivity tensor in Eq. (6) reweights the differential operator identically for both potentials, nonrelativistic orbits and null light rays respond to the exact same boosted potential, $`\Psi = \Phi`$.
This reproduces the agreement between weak lensing and rotation curves observed around isolated galaxies [32, 64].

This optical equality distinguishes our conductivity readout from alternative metric modifications that fail in either the orbital or the lensing channel.
For example, the earlier Inherent Structure Hypothesis [20] attributed galactic anomalies to an intrinsic spatial curvature independent of local matter.
At the order-unity strain required to explain flat rotation curves, pure spatial curvature would leave slow orbits unboosted at leading order while deflecting passing starlight through a radian or more.
Modifying the conductivity of the lapse potential $`\Phi`$ alone while leaving $`\Psi`$ Newtonian fails in the opposite direction.
Photons would experience only half the anomalous acceleration felt by stars, causing weak lensing to underestimate dynamical masses by 0.20 dex at $`g_{\text{bar}} = 10^{-11}\ \mathrm{m\ s^{-2}}`$ and by 0.29 dex at $`10^{-13}\ \mathrm{m\ s^{-2}}`$.
Translated into effective General Relativity stress-energy, a lapse-only modification corresponds to a stress without energy density.
Its radial pressure approaches $`c^2`$ times the apparent dark-matter density in the deep regime, whereas our microscopic crimp introduces neither macroscopic displacement nor elastic stress.
Likewise, counting path length directly along the microscopic folds rather than counting flux across layers would either inflate lensing masses to $`\nu^2`$ times the baryonic mass or stretch radial ruler distances by factors of order unity.
Only flux conductivity acting symmetrically on both potentials reproduces an effective dark-matter mass simultaneously for orbits and for light.

Extending this static readout to moving bodies requires adding a time-derivative flux term to the gravitational momentum constraint, analogous to Maxwell's displacement current in electromagnetism, an extension developed in Paper II.
The way the expanding cosmological background enters both constraint equations, governing linear structure growth and the integrated Sachs–Wolfe effect, remains an open problem, as does nonlinear order, and none of this paper's fits or tests depends on it.

By the first microstructure requirement of §3.1, transverse shear waves in the shear continuum propagate at $`c`$ [19, Sec. 3.2], saturating the relativistic causal bound [19, 77], with the fabric's pressure neglected at the order of the residual strain.
Electromagnetic waves and radiative gravitational waves, however, propagate on the discrete lattice of cells; they match speed $`c`$ only if the cell lattice's elastic stiffness and inertia keep its wave speeds synchronized with the shear continuum.
Note that in the separate-lattice architecture, gravitational waves travel as shape deformations of the cells, whereas in Tenev and Horstemeyer [18] they were modeled directly as continuum shear waves.
Meanwhile, static gravitational potentials are transmitted along the folded material path of the orientational sector, leaving the exact connection between the zero-frequency limit of lattice waves and the static conductivity of Eq. (6) open.
Paper II states, without deriving them, the synchronization condition on the cells alongside the symmetry conditions under which the composite architecture keeps light and gravity propagation isotropic.
Experimentally, modern rotating-resonator tests bound light-speed anisotropy below roughly $`10^{-18}`$ [78]; provided the cell synchronization condition holds, the fabric model also satisfies the $`\sim 10^{-15}`$ multimessenger wave-speed bound from GW170817 / GRB 170817A [35].

In deriving Lorentz transformations for the fabric, Tenev and Horstemeyer [24] adopted physical rod contraction from the Michelson–Morley null result, making optical isotropy [78] an empirical input.
Subsequently, Tenev et al. [19, Sec. 8.1] showed that open-system accretion preserves the kinematic Lorentz invariance because the precursor reservoir exerts traction only on divergence-free slip, whereas uniform cosmic expansion and static potential gradients generate curl-free slip.
The material postulates hide the fabric's rest frame from local rods, clocks, and light rays.
The preferred-frame parameters $`\alpha_1`$ and $`\alpha_2`$ of the parameterized post-Newtonian (PPN) formalism, however, test whether orbital dynamics around a moving gravitating body expose that frame [79], a case that the open-system model does not yet derive [19, Sec. 10].
Lunar laser ranging bounds $`\lvert\alpha_1\rvert \lesssim 10^{-4}`$ [79], while pulsars bound the strong-field counterparts at 95 percent confidence to $`\hat\alpha_1 = -0.4^{+3.7}_{-3.1}\times10^{-5}`$ [80] and $`\lvert\hat\alpha_2\rvert < 1.6\times10^{-9}`$ [81], evaluated relative to the cosmic microwave background rest frame in which the Solar System moves at 369 km/s [80].
Following Tenev and Horstemeyer [24], we place the fabric's rest frame in the microwave background frame; whether the model satisfies these stringent PPN bounds remains open.

Crucially, under scale-selective coupling, threshold renewal introduces no additional preferred-frame anomalies into Solar System or pulsar tests, because those compact systems lie orders of magnitude below the catchment length and retain unmodified Newtonian internal fields.
Moreover, because the steady-state boost law represents a local event-by-event balance free of velocity terms, the fabric's rest frame enters only into time-dependent relaxation kinetics.
Because the trajectory clause denies inertia to the fabric's embedding position, newly solidified material is laid down at rest in the ambient cosmological frame.
A galaxy moving relative to the microwave background therefore continually sweeps across its own induced stretch pattern; Paper II treats the kinetics of such moving systems.

In our framework, the boost mechanism is kinetic, and having a finite threshold probability density at zero strain contrast sets $`p = 1`$ in the asymptotic exponent $`-p/(1+p) = -1/2`$.
Positing a nonvanishing density at zero threshold plays a logical role analogous to selecting a fractional 3/2 kinetic power in superfluid dark matter, though here it is met by any threshold law with a finite density at zero.

Among our theoretical inputs, the functional form of the threshold distribution and its mean value are the two choices that might appear tailored to recover MOND, yet each governs a distinct aspect of the phenomenology.
The detailed shape of the interpolation curve across galaxies is largely insensitive to the specific threshold distribution, as §3.5 shows, and among positive distributions of fixed mean, the exponential law introduces the minimum information, or maximum entropy.
While the exponential form specifically dictates the high-acceleration hardening and the logarithmic form of the scale relation in Eq. (10), current rotation-curve data test both features only weakly.
Meanwhile, equating the mean threshold to the per-axis share of the set point, an identification, sets the discrete counting factor to $`\kappa = 1`$ out of a four-value geometric menu that rotation curves alone leave undecided, as §4.2 shows.

Notably, condensed-matter precedents warn that a finite threshold density at zero load cannot be taken for granted.
In driven amorphous elastoplastic models, the steady-state distribution of local stress distances to yield vanishes as a power law near zero [56].
If renewal thresholds behaved similarly, the deep-MOND exponent would be steeper than $`-1/2`$.
Our postulate avoids that depletion gap because each renewal event draws a fresh threshold independently for a single comparison, mirroring how soft glassy rheology draws a fresh trap depth after each hop [57], preventing marginal depletion between events.
Finite density at zero contrast is therefore a substantive physical assertion of the postulate: any future microscopic model of a renewal event whose threshold density vanished at zero would fail to recover the baryonic Tully–Fisher slope of 4.

Three specific theoretical features distinguish this work from earlier proposals:

1. The kinetic balance yields a specific one-parameter interpolation family, MOND's simple function hardened exponentially at high acceleration at an onset determined by the trapped free volume $`\delta_{\text{fv}}`$, reducing smoothly to the simple function as $`\delta_{\text{fv}} \to 0`$.
2. The acceleration scale equals the de Sitter floor acceleration multiplied by a dimensionless material factor, $`a_0 = cH_{\rm dS}\ln(1+\delta_{\text{fv}})`$, because the unknown residual strain set point cancels out between the telegraph catchment length and the mean threshold.
By contrast, Verlinde's [9] parameter-free relation $`a_M = cH_0/6`$ sits 1.6σ to 2.7σ above our constrained best-fit scales, $`(0.973 \pm 0.074)`$ and $`(0.981 \pm 0.075)\times10^{-10}\ \mathrm{m\ s^{-2}}`$ at $`H_0`$ = 67.4 and 73 km/s/Mpc.
It sits 0.5σ to 1.6σ above the $`1.05\times10^{-10}\ \mathrm{m\ s^{-2}}`$ scale fitted under identical priors with the empirical RAR function.
3. Nonlocal strain averaging across the 30 to 200 pc catchment length screens internal dynamics in compact systems, a short-distance cutoff absent from standard AQUAL [14] and the quasi-linear formulation [15].
This yields an unambiguous prediction of Newtonian behavior in Solar neighborhood wide binaries.

Section 5.5 states how empirical tests could falsify the first two, though no criterion yet tests the hardening's onset, and Paper II states the tests of the screen.

Within the broader MOND paradigm, our model realizes the external-field effect literally, since local renewal senses the total strain gradient regardless of source.
On the aligned state of §3.6 our static field equation reduces to exact AQUAL, so the model inherits AQUAL's established successes and challenges across galactic environments [6].
Physically, whereas Blanchet [10] models the AQUAL function $`\mu`$ as the gravitational analog of a dielectric permittivity in a polarizable medium, our readout obtains $`\mu = 1/\bar\ell'`$ as the axial series conductivity of folded material layers in Eq. (6).

In standard cosmology, by contrast, the radial-acceleration relation must emerge statistically from dissipational galaxy formation and baryonic feedback inside cold dark matter halos.
While modern hydrodynamical simulations broadly reproduce the locus of the relation [82, 83], matching its tiny intrinsic scatter and universal acceleration scale across diverse galaxy histories remains a central challenge [4, 6].

Finally, two broader theoretical classifications separate competing accounts.
First is redshift evolution of $`a_0`$: models tying $`a_0`$ dynamically to the instantaneous Hubble rate predict $`a_0(z) \propto H(z)`$ [84], whereas models anchoring $`a_0`$ to a constant de Sitter horizon or material floor, ours included, predict a strictly redshift-independent $`a_0`$.
Kinematic observations at earlier epochs remain unsettled, and the offsets reported thus far, significant in one study [85], lean toward higher $`a_0`$ in the past [85, 86].
Second is the origin of the deep-MOND $`-1/2`$ exponent: Milgrom [87] demonstrated that any gravity modification becoming scale-invariant in the weak-field limit automatically inherits that exponent by symmetry alone without specifying a physical mechanism.
Our kinetic balance supplies the specific features that scale invariance leaves undetermined: a one-parameter transition shape and, via two material identifications, a crossover scale tied directly to $`cH_{\rm dS}`$.

Thus, although our galaxy fits share the single-parameter economy and weak-field form of MOND's simple function, two testable physical features separate this framework from a phenomenological curve fit.
These are the catchment-length screen, which makes system size a second discriminating axis and requires Newtonian wide binaries, and the exponential high-acceleration hardening linked to $`a_0`$ via a material free volume.

## 6. Conclusion

In the open-system Cosmic Fabric Model, a growing elastic space generates the galaxy-scale dark-matter effect as a steady-state pattern maintained by continuous material renewal.
We have derived the renewal growth boost law from four postulates, introducing the Threshold Renewal postulate, from structural requirements on a three-sector fabric, and from nine condensed-matter identifications.
Because renewal events align proportionally to the total field at weak accelerations, the boost law naturally recovers the deep-MOND exponent of $`-1/2`$.
Its resulting interpolation curve is MOND's simple function modified by an exponential high-acceleration hardening whose onset is set by the trapped free volume $`\delta_{\text{fv}}`$.
Through two material identifications, the crossover acceleration scale $`a_0`$ is tied directly to the cosmic de Sitter expansion floor $`cH_{\rm dS}`$, with the unknown residual strain set point canceling out.
That set point instead governs the spatial catchment length of 30 to 200 pc, below which nonlocal strain averaging dilutes a compact body's own field, keeping the Solar System and wide binary stars Newtonian.
Because the accumulated material excess enters the Poisson operator symmetrically as a flux conductivity for both metric potentials rather than stretching ruler distances, gravitational lensing matches orbital dynamics at linear order.

Imposing the theoretical scale relation between $`\delta_{\text{fv}}`$ and $`a_0`$ leaves the boost law with a single globally fitted parameter.
Tested across 153 SPARC disk galaxies using the algebraic relation with profiled distances and inclinations, the law leads MOND's simple function by 1.6σ and matches the empirical RAR function within 0.5σ.
The fitted scale of $`a_0 = (0.97 \pm 0.07)\times10^{-10}\ \mathrm{m\ s^{-2}}`$ implies a material free volume near 0.19, close to the 0.20 of vitreous silica over quartz, though rotation curves alone do not constrain $`\delta_{\text{fv}}`$ independently of $`a_0`$.

Important tensions and open tasks nonetheless remain.
Solving the full axisymmetric disk field equation fits worse than the algebraic relation and trails MOND's unhardened simple function on the model's own field; and holding catalog distances fixed favors the empirical RAR function over Eq. (8).
Beyond these empirical tensions, the condensed-matter identifications underlying the flux readout and scale cancellation await derivation.
Independent stellar mass-to-light calibrations and high-redshift measurements testing whether $`a_0`$ remains frozen in cosmic time will provide the next decisive tests of the law.
