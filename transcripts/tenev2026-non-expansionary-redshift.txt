Abstract.

This paper presents a non-expansionary mechanism for galactic redshift from a young-earth creationist perspective. This working hypothesis is developed through three progressively complex conceptual models that have mathematical backing: the Small Young Luminous Shell, the Large Young Luminous Shell, and the Large Young Luminous Ball. All models are based on a recently created static universe and incorporate the finite speed of gravity. The core idea is that in a newly created, static universe of finite size, not expanding from a singularity, the gravitational field has not yet reached its stable, long-term state. This temporary imbalance in the gravitational field causes a redshift that mimics accelerated expansion. We derive expressions for the redshift associated with each of the three models considered herein, and show that the Large Young Luminous Shell and Large Young Luminous Ball redshift-versus-distance relationships bear similarity with observations. In the process, we also derive a formula for the gravitational acceleration due to a spherical shell cap along its central axis.

Background.

Hubble observed that distant astronomical objects show a redshift, where light spectra are shifted towards the red end, roughly proportional to their distance. He interpreted this as the Doppler effect of stellar recession with redshift z given by z approximately equal to v over c, where v is galactic velocity and c is the speed of light. This led to the Hubble relation, equation one: v equals H-naught times x, or z is approximately equal to H-naught over c, times x, which equals x over R-sub-H, where x is distance, H-naught is the Hubble constant, and R-sub-H is the Hubble radius, the Hubble constant interpreted as distance.

Modern mainstream cosmology interprets the observed redshift as the effect of the expansion of the universe, which lengthens light waves in transit. However, recent observations of Type One-A supernovae show that the relation in equation one is not linear but has a slight downturn with distance, which is commonly understood to imply an accelerated expansion of the universe due to a hypothetical substance called dark energy. While the gravity of massive objects may also cause a nonlinear redshift, such effects cannot be used to explain Hubble's observation, because they do not apply on cosmological scales.

In this paper, we propose a novel cause of redshift that arises from the ex-nihilo creation of finite size matter, as opposed to a singularity, and the finite speed of gravity. Specifically, we show that in a recently created, inhomogeneous universe where the Distant Starlight Paradox has a solution of a certain type, one would observe a redshift even without expansion. To this end, we build on the Distant Starlight Paradox solution presented by Tenev and colleagues to show that redshift is a natural outcome of the setup described there. We offer this proposal as an additional tool for developing a young-earth creationist cosmology. While it does not necessarily contradict the concept of an expanding universe, it can be utilized alongside it to better explain astronomical observations without requiring the invocation of accelerated expansion or inflation.

Humphreys recently proposed a non-expansionary redshift mechanism due to light's electromagnetic interaction with the luminiferous ether. However, the gravity-based mechanism presented here avoids the light scattering issue, a notable drawback in Humphreys' proposal.

Method.

Just like matter, we treat space as a created thing. We do this for convenience, because it lets us ascribe cosmological time tau, equal to tau of X, at every point X in space representing the elapsed time measured by a stationary clock at X since the creation event. Nevertheless, we expect that the ideas presented herein can also be formulated from a Machian perspective.

For simplicity, we assume an approximately flat space under the conditions of weak gravity. This simplification is largely justified by the Planck 2018 results which, observing the acoustic peaks of the cosmic microwave background, concluded that at very large scales the spatial curvature of the universe is roughly flat. This simplification allows us to remain within the domain of Special Relativity and make use of equations from Newtonian gravity.

We will consider several spherically symmetric setups of increasing complexity: Small Young Luminous Shell, Large Young Luminous Shell, and a Large Young Luminous Ball. They all share one feature: center-bound light emitted from any point X at time tau since the creation event at X must reach the center point O no later than time tau plus one day since the creation event at O. In other words, the Distant Starlight Paradox has a solution within each setup, and such a solution is similar to the one described by Tenev and colleagues, or by Lisle.

The terms shell and ball refer to the shape of gravitating matter: for a shell, matter is distributed equidistantly from the center and has surface density sigma, while for a ball, matter is distributed evenly within a sphere and has volume density rho. The term luminous means that we consider light emitted from any point within the setup. This includes light emitted from the shell and from within its interior. We also construct the ball such that it is not opaque, but light from any distance can reach the center unobstructed. This is an idealization of a universe filled with point-like stars. Likewise, we allow for light to pass through the shell unobstructed.

Discussion.

We believe that the Large Young Luminous Shell and Large Young Luminous Ball models introduced here can be used as components of a larger young-earth creationist cosmological model. The redshift plots for each model, Figure four and Figure five, both show a monotonic increase of redshift with distance. Moreover, over the majority of the distance the plots appear near linear with a slight downward slope. This suggests that either model or some combination thereof might be useful in explaining the observed Hubble relation without necessarily invoking recessionary motion or universal expansion, as mainstream cosmology does. For example, while the Large Young Luminous Ball can be used to model the matter within a young universe, the Large Young Luminous Shell can be used to model the effects of the waters above, Psalm one hundred forty-eight, verse four, as Humphreys has speculated that these are literal waters forming a shell around the expanse of space. In this case, the cosmic microwave background may turn out to be the redshifted thermal radiation of such an outer shell.

We acknowledge that for a homogeneous, isotropic universe, Einstein's field equations forbid a simultaneously static, flat, and stable solution. However, a static, flat solution is possible with the appropriate cosmological constant. Although this solution is unstable over deep time, with matter perturbations causing expansion or contraction, the universe can remain essentially static within the young-earth creationist timeframe. Our aim is not to reject universal expansion but to propose an additional, young-earth-creationist-specific mechanism for the observed galactic redshift.

A possible objection to the inward-moving star-creation front model concerns the gravitational potential difference, which could be misinterpreted by a static analysis as causing a blueshift. However, as detailed in Appendix C, this argument is invalid because the model fundamentally depends on time-dependent gravity.

The vertical intercept z-naught of the Large Young Luminous Ball redshift curve in Figure five a, and similarly on the Large Young Luminous Shell curve, indicates that the Large Young Luminous Shell and the Large Young Luminous Ball alone are likely insufficient to fully explain the observed Hubble relationship, and that one would need an additional model component. For example, one might speculate that Earth is located in a gravitational well, which slows timelapse at Earth compared to timelapse in the distant cosmos. All else being equal, an observer within such a gravitational well will see that distant stars are blueshifted, but in the context of a Large Young Luminous Ball universe, such a blueshift will cancel out with the sharp redshift rise represented by the intercept z-naught in Figure five a. The value of z-naught equals one point five is equivalent to a time dilation factor of two point five times near Earth compared to the distant cosmos. The notion that time in the distant cosmos elapses several times faster than near Earth has also been suggested by Humphreys.

The applications of the Large Young Luminous Shell and Large Young Luminous Ball models along the lines described above might be developed in future work. Such work should involve the fitting of data from the Type One-A supernovae, which are used as standard candles to precisely measure redshift versus distance. The parameters of the models that can be adjusted to fit the data would be: the age parameter epsilon, the radius of the universe R, which can be larger than that of the observable universe, and the densities rho or sigma. For the model sketches above, we had chosen rho to be the density of the interstellar medium, which might be too high as an average density of the universe. In future work, we might consider adjusting this density down while increasing the age parameter epsilon to account for the near-Earth time dilation. Additionally, future work should consider incorporating manifold geometry from General Relativity for improved accuracy to account for additional effects of universal expansion and spatial curvature.

Model prediction: the dependence of redshift on the age parameter epsilon is an interesting feature of the Large Young Luminous Shell and Large Young Luminous Ball models that is unique to the young-earth creationist presupposition. If these models were correct, we would expect that the Hubble parameter H-naught would increase over time by measurable amounts.

Appendix C. Gravitational Potential Evolution and Photon Energy Loss.

A potential objection to the inward-moving star-creation front model relates to the gravitational potential difference, which a static analysis might suggest causes a blueshift. This blueshift reasoning is based on treating gravitational potential as a state function: as mass accumulates, the potential at the center, at reception, becomes much lower, more negative, than at the perimeter, at emission. In a static field, a photon moving from higher to lower potential gains energy, implying it should arrive blueshifted.

The static conclusion described above is invalid because the gravitational potential in this model is time-dependent, with the partial derivative of capital fai with respect to t not equal to zero. The critical factor determining the frequency shift is not the scalar difference between endpoints, but the total work done by the photon against the field during its transit.

The photon loses energy, is redshifted, by the time it reaches the center, despite the final potential at the center being lower than at x-sub-one.

In a dynamic system, the energy change is derived by integrating the potential gradient along the photon's worldline. While the absolute potential at the center decreases as mass accumulates, the photon itself travels in tandem with the collapsing front, see Figure C-one. Throughout its trajectory, the photon is located slightly inside the deepening potential well of the creation front. Consequently, the photon constantly encounters a positive potential gradient, effectively climbing out of the local depression created by the front, even as that depression moves inward. The integral of this gradient along the path yields a positive value, signifying that the photon expends energy to maintain its trajectory against the evolving geometry. This energy expenditure results in a redshift.

To intuitively visualize the distinction between the static and dynamic interpretations, consider the mechanics of a stair climber exercise machine, Figure C-two. A user on such a machine may maintain a constant geometric elevation relative to the ground, analogous to the gravitational potential at the photon's position. However, because the steps are dynamically moving downward, analogous to the potential well moving inward, the user must constantly expend energy to climb.
