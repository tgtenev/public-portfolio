Abstract.

We extend the descriptive power of the Cosmic Fabric model of space developed by Tenev and Horstemeyer to include moving observers, by demonstrating that all reference frames are phenomenologically equivalent with one another and transform between each other via the Lorentz transformations. Our approach is similar to that of Lorentz, which was used to explain the negative outcome of the Michelson-Morley aether detection experiment, except that we deduce the notions of length contraction and time dilation from the postulates of the Cosmic Fabric model. Our result is valid for the continuum length scale, at which, by definition, the cosmic fabric can be described mathematically as a continuum. Herein, we also discuss the length-scale dependent nature of the Cosmic Fabric model as a possible way to relate gravitational and quantum theories.

Keywords: modified gravity, aether, and cosmic fabric.

The Physics and Astronomy Classification Scheme numbers are zero four point fifty, K D, for modified theories of gravity, and forty-six point ninety, plus s, for other topics in continuum mechanics of solids.

Section 1. Introduction.

The Cosmic Fabric model proposed by Tenev and Horstemeyer is a formal analogy interpreting space as an elastic solid body, a cosmic fabric, with a Poisson ratio of unity, so that only signals in the form of shear waves, such as light and gravitational waves, are admitted. Matter-energy densities behave as inclusions, which are free to move unimpeded within the fabric. These inclusions induce mechanical strain, causing the bending of the fabric and the slowing down of clock rates within it, effects from which one can derive the field equations of General Relativity.

Because of the underlying material analogy, the Cosmic Fabric model is, in a sense, a descendant from earlier material models of space, commonly known as aether theories, which have played an indispensable role in our understanding of the nature of space. The notion of an aether has been thought about from different perspectives over time, probably initially by Aristotle, who considered it to be the fifth element comprising the heavenly spheres and bodies. For some time after Aristotle, the aether was viewed as a fluid. For example, although Isaac Newton described the aether as, quote, capable of contraction and dilatation, strongly elastic, end quote, which makes one think that he was alluding to a solid aether, he also discussed a universal fluid aether filling the cosmos at the largest length scale, but a dynamic short-range interaction of aether and matter at the smallest length scales. It was the aether whose multiple functions admitted transmitted forces to produce the phenomena in the universe that we see, including gravitation. Later, Laplace furthered the thought that gravity propagated through a liquid aether that had a lower length-scale basis from molecules. Fresnel proposed that the aether was partially entrained by matter, but Stokes argued that such entrainment was complete. Kelvin and Tait described the aether at the smallest length scales as vortex atoms comprising a frictionless, elastic material, with, quote, the hypothesis that space is continuously occupied by an incompressible frictionless liquid acted on by no force, and that material phenomena of every kind depend solely on motions created in this liquid, end quote. They argued that electromagnetism had to be based on a mechanical notion like that of the aether. Maxwell used the idea of an aether to build the theory of electromagnetic phenomena. Lodge presented mechanical Lagrangian models to illustrate the aether's phenomenological effects.

The Lorentz aether theory was the culmination of earlier material models of space. Based on Lodge's work, Lorentz and Michelson and colleagues developed an electron-aether theory, where matter, that is electrons, and the aether were different entities, in which the aether was completely motionless. This stationary configuration would not be in motion close to matter. By contrast to earlier electron models, the electromagnetic field of the aether appears as a mediator between the electrons, so a signal cannot propagate faster than the speed of light. The basic concept of Lorentz's theory was the, quote, theorem of corresponding states, end quote, in which an observer moving relative to the aether makes equivalent observations as a stationary observer. Lorentz changed the space-time variables between one reference frame and another, and introduced concepts like a physical length contraction and a local time to explain the Michelson and Morley work, which had shown that the stationary reference frame of the aether was undetectable. Lorentz and Larmor discussed that the notion of a local time is accompanied by a time dilation of matter moving in the aether. In other words, there is an elastic aether strain that arises when electrons, that is matter, are present. Larmor tried to view the aether in the context of different length scales: electrons, atoms, molecules, and the cosmos. Lorentz later noted that he considered a clock stationary in the aether to give the, quote, true, end quote, time, while local time was thought of as a working hypothesis with a dynamical mathematical backing. Therefore, Lorentz's theorem is viewed by modern authors as being a mathematical transformation from a, quote, real, end quote, stationary reference frame in the aether into a dynamic, quote, fictitious, end quote, configuration.

Initially, the Theory of Special Relativity appeared to have supplanted the material view of space, not because the latter was flawed, but because it seemed no longer needed. However, the more complete understanding came later, when General Relativity reinstated the relevance of attributing material-like nature to space. Poincare had already introduced the Principle of Relativity in the context of Lorentz's work, and declared it as a general law of nature, including gravitation, as he corrected some of Lorentz's mistakes and proved the Lorentz covariance of the electromagnetic equations. He included the aether as an undetectable medium that distinguished between apparent and real time. Shortly thereafter, Einstein proposed the Special Theory of Relativity, in which the aether was not necessary, and Dirac claimed that the aether was, quote, abandoned, end quote, because of Einstein. However, Einstein later came back and discussed the necessity of an aether in the context of the General Theory of Relativity. Lorentz wrote a letter to Einstein in which he speculated that within General Relativity the aether was reintroduced. In his response, Einstein wrote that one can in fact speak about a new stationary aether, but not a dynamic aether. In a lecture which Einstein was invited to give at Lorentz's university in Leiden, Einstein sought to reconcile the theory of relativity with the Lorentzian aether. In this lecture, Einstein stressed that special relativity does not rule out the aether, saying, quote, to deny the aether is ultimately to assume that empty space has no physical qualities whatever. The fundamental facts of mechanics do not harmonize with this view. For the mechanical behavior of a corporeal system hovering freely in empty space depends not only on relative positions, that is distances, and relative velocities, but also on its state of rotation, which physically may be taken as a characteristic not appertaining to the system in itself. In order to be able to look upon the rotation of the system, at least formally, as something real, Newton's objective space. Since he classes his absolute space together with real things, for him rotation relative to an absolute space is also something real. Newton might no less well have called his absolute space Aether; what is essential is merely that besides observable objects, another thing, which is not perceptible, must be looked upon as real, to enable acceleration or rotation to be looked upon as something real, end quote.

Although Einstein did eventually affirm the validity of the material view of space, Special Relativity remained dominant by a wide margin, in part because its generalization, that is General Relativity, explained gravitational phenomena better than the Lorentz aether theory, which was the state-of-the-art aether theory at the time. In addition, the Principle of Relativity, which is the cornerstone of Special Relativity and General Relativity, brought about the unification of space and time into a mathematically elegant spacetime continuum. Nevertheless, the need to separate space from time, such as in the Arnowitt-Deser-Misner formalism of General Relativity, remained indispensable for General Relativity's practical applications. Although the Arnowitt-Deser-Misner formalism is not a material model as such, the need for such a formulation points to the continual relevance of a material model of gravity. Such a model, as is the Lorentz aether theory or the recently introduced Cosmic Fabric model, necessarily separates space from time, because space is viewed as a material object progressing in time. Tenev and Horstemeyer laid the groundwork for a modern material model of space, and discussed the benefits of such a view, which seeks to leverage a century of advancement in Solid Mechanics since the time of the Lorentz aether theory.

Whereas in our earlier paper we showed that the Cosmic Fabric model is a valid analogy to General Relativity for nearly static observers, herein we extend the analogy to include moving observers as well. Although the Cosmic Fabric model ostensibly defines a hyperplane of absolute simultaneity, and therefore a preferred reference frame, we demonstrate that, to both moving and stationary observers alike, such a preferred reference frame remains undetectable at continuum length scales. In the discussion section, Section 4, we mention briefly how the Cosmic Fabric model is conducive to generalizations outside of the continuum length scale. The essential contribution of this paper is to expand the descriptive power of the Cosmic Fabric model to include moving observers, and to show that all reference frames are phenomenologically equivalent with one another, which is to say that the Principle of Relativity applies within the Cosmic Fabric model. Consequently, we conclude that the model has at least the descriptive power of Special Relativity and the Lorentz aether theory, and also, based on our earlier paper, the descriptive power of General Relativity for the case of weak gravity. Therefore, the work herein contributes toward a material model of space that generalizes the Lorentz aether theory, similarly to how General Relativity generalized Special Relativity.

Our paper is organized as follows. In Section 2, we show that the speed of signal propagation within the fabric, which we had previously identified with the speed of light in free space, is invariant for all observers within all reference frames in the fabric. This result recovers the Second Postulate of Special Relativity, which states that the speed of light is invariant for all inertial reference frames. In Section 3, we derive the transformations between the coordinates of a stationary and a moving observer, which we recognize as the Lorentz transformations. Because these apply in like manner between any two inertial reference frames, and not only between a stationary and a moving reference frame, it follows that no reference frame can be singled out as special by an observer within the fabric. Consequently, we recover the First Postulate of Special Relativity, which states that all physical laws are the same in all inertial reference frames. In Section 4, we discuss how the Cosmic Fabric model compares with Special Relativity and the Lorentz aether theory, and we conclude in Section 5.

Section 2. Invariance of the speed of signals.

In this section, we show that the speed of signal propagation is invariant with respect to any inertial reference frame for any observer within the fabric. First, we demonstrate the invariance for stationary observers at different locations. Next, we show that the invariance also applies for a moving and a stationary observer at a given location. The combination of these two results leads to the desired conclusion about the invariance of the speed of a signal.

Section 2.1. Stationary observers at different locations.

The formulation of the Cosmic Fabric model postulates that the fabric mediates all matter-matter interactions via signals that travel as mechanical disturbances within it. It is convenient to consider the fabric as immersed within a four-dimensional reference hyperspace, not necessarily physical, which also has its own time coordinate; this is illustrated in Figure 1, described below. Such a reference space is somewhat similar to Dicke's, quote, Newtonian coordinate system, end quote, except in the context of four spatial dimensions. From the perspective of this reference space, the rate of matter-matter interactions is proportional to the speed of signal propagation. Consequently, the rate of clock ticks, that is the time-lapse rate, is also proportional to the speed of signal propagation.

Although signals need not propagate with uniform speed when measured with respect to the enclosing reference space, nevertheless their speed will appear constant from the perspective of observers within the fabric, for the following reason. A clock placed where fabric signals propagate relatively slowly, in relation to the enclosing reference space, will also tick slowly. Therefore, to an observer within the fabric, the signal speed would appear to have remained unchanged, because the slowdown of the clock used to measure the signal speed would exactly compensate for the reduction of that speed. Consequently, the signal speed within the fabric appears to be invariant for any stationary observer. In the description of the Cosmic Fabric model, we had identified the speed of signal propagation with the speed of light in free space, c. Below, we provide an algebraic derivation of this result.

Let v-sub-a and v-sub-b be, respectively, the speeds of signal propagation at locations A and B of the fabric, measured in relation to the enclosing reference space. By definition, v-sub-a equals the rate of l-sub-a with respect to t, where l-sub-a is a distance element at location A, and t is the travel time, reckoned with respect to the enclosing reference space. According to the Time Lapse postulate of the Cosmic Fabric model, the rate of tau-sub-a with respect to t equals one over v-naught, times v-sub-a, where one over v-naught is a constant of proportionality. Therefore, the speed of signal propagation, c-sub-a, measured at location A within the fabric, is as follows.

C-sub-a, the rate of l-sub-a with respect to tau-sub-a, equals the rate of l-sub-a with respect to t, times the rate of t with respect to tau-sub-a. This equals v-sub-a times the quantity v-naught over v-sub-a, which equals v-naught.

In the same way, we can show that the speed of signal propagation, c-sub-b, at location B, is c-sub-b equals v-naught. Thus, we conclude that the speed of signal propagation at both locations A and B is one and the same with respect to stationary observers in the fabric. The magnitude of this speed can be identified with the speed of light in free space, c, as follows.

C-sub-a equals c-sub-b equals v-naught equals c.

Since locations A and B were arbitrary, it follows that the speed of signal propagation is invariant for all stationary observers within the fabric.

Section 2.2. Moving observer.

We now consider the situation of an observer at a given location moving with velocity v with respect to the fabric; this is illustrated in Figure 2, described below. Let tau-prime and tau represent the time measured by the moving observer and a stationary observer at the same location, respectively. As illustrated in Figure 2, it follows from the Pythagorean theorem that the effective signal speed, in any orientation transverse to the motion of the observer, is the square root of the quantity c squared minus v squared. Therefore, by the Time Lapse postulate of the Cosmic Fabric model, we conclude that the moving observer's clocks must tick slower by a factor of the square root of the quantity c squared minus v squared, divided by c, which equals the square root of the quantity one minus beta squared, where beta is defined as v over c. In other words,

the rate of tau-prime with respect to tau equals the square root of the quantity one minus beta squared, where beta is defined as v over c.

We considered the signal speed in a transverse orientation to avoid any direction-specific effects, such as length contraction.

Next, we consider how lengths are affected along the orientation of motion. We measure lengths by the round-trip time of signals; this is illustrated in Figure 3, described below. In all of our thought experiments, we use round-trip times to avoid complications due to clock synchronization. Let d-l be the rest length of a rod oriented along the direction of motion. From the perspective of the stationary observer, the total time, d-tau, that it takes for the signal to travel from one end of the rod and back, is as follows.

D-tau equals d-l over the quantity c plus v, plus d-l over the quantity c minus v. This equals two times d-l over c, times one over the quantity one minus beta squared.

From the viewpoint of the moving observer, per the time-dilation equation above, the round-trip travel time, d-tau-prime, is as follows.

D-tau-prime equals d-t times the square root of the quantity one minus beta squared, which equals two times d-l over c, times one over the square root of the quantity one minus beta squared.

The above equation can be interpreted in one of two ways: either the moving observer perceives the round-trip signal speed to differ in the direction of motion compared to any transverse direction, or the rod's length changed, such that the new length, d-l-prime, is d-l-prime equals d-l over the square root of the quantity one minus beta squared. The former possibility implies that the signal speed, and therefore the speed of light, would be anisotropic. The Michelson-Morley experiment was designed to measure such anisotropy, and its negative outcome rules out the former possibility. Therefore, one must conclude that measuring rods oriented along the motion of the reference frame experience a length change according to the following.

D-l-prime equals d-l over the square root of the quantity one minus beta squared.

the rate of l with respect to tau equals the rate of l-prime with respect to tau-prime, which equals c.

The above result is the same as what Lorentz concluded in constructing his aether theory, except that he considered only the speed of light, as opposed to a more general speed of signals, as in the Cosmic Fabric model. In the Cosmic Fabric model, the speed of signal propagation is more fundamental than the speed of light, because it controls all matter-matter interactions, and not just those pertaining to electromagnetic phenomena. This is why the variation of the speed of signal propagation affects not only the rate of clock ticks, but also the length of measuring rods.

Section 3. Lorentz transformations.

Next, we recover the Lorentz transformations from the speed-of-light invariance and from basic considerations of spatial symmetry. The method is detailed below.

Figure 4, described below, shows the spacetime coordinates of an unprimed and a primed observer, where the primed observer travels with velocity v with respect to the unprimed one. Let beta, defined as v over c, be the scaled relative speed. The origin represents an event where x equals x-prime equals zero, and c-tau equals c-tau-prime equals zero. Because the time coordinates are scaled by c, a photon emitted at the origin in the positive x direction will traverse a straight-line trajectory that bisects the angle between the unprimed axes. A particle stationary at the primed origin traverses a straight-line trajectory of slope one over beta with respect to the unprimed coordinates. Therefore, the primed time axis must have a slope of one over beta with respect to the unprimed coordinates. Because of the speed-of-light invariance, the trajectory of the aforementioned photon must also bisect the angle between the primed axes, and consequently, the primed space axis must have a slope of beta with respect to the unprimed coordinates.

Consider a sufficiently small region around the origin where the cosmic fabric is translationally symmetric in space and time. Such translational symmetry is only applicable at the continuum length scale, at which discrete substructure can be ignored or homogeneously included into the fabric's continuous smoothness. The translational symmetry implies that the coordinate transformations we seek must be linear, so let the transformation be written as follows.

The column vector consisting of x-prime and c-tau-prime equals a two-by-two matrix, with entries a-one-one and a-one-two in the top row, and a-two-one and a-two-two in the bottom row, multiplied by the column vector consisting of x and c-tau.

Here the coefficients a-sub-myu-nyu, that is, the matrix entries indexed by myu and nyu, depend only on the relative velocity v. Events along the primed time axis, where x-prime equals zero, must have unprimed coordinates such that c-tau equals x over beta. Therefore, a-one-two equals minus beta times a-one-one. Likewise, events along the unprimed time axis, where x equals zero, must have primed coordinates such that x-prime equals minus beta times c-tau-prime, and hence a-one-two equals minus beta times a-two-two. Finally, events along the primed spatial axis, where c-tau-prime equals zero, must have unprimed coordinates such that c-tau equals beta times x, from which we conclude that a-two-one equals minus beta times a-two-two. Consequently, letting gamma equal a-one-one, which equals a-two-two, the transformation above becomes the following.

The column vector consisting of x-prime and c-tau-prime equals gamma times a two-by-two matrix, with first row one and minus beta, and second row minus beta and one, multiplied by the column vector consisting of x and c-tau.

The inverse of the transformation above should also have the same form, and correspond to equal and opposite velocity, which leads to the following requirement.

The two-by-two identity matrix, with first row one and zero, and second row zero and one, equals gamma squared times the matrix with first row one and minus beta, and second row minus beta and one, multiplied by the matrix with first row one and beta, and second row beta and one. This equals gamma squared times the matrix with first row consisting of the quantity one minus beta squared, and zero, and second row consisting of zero, and the quantity one minus beta squared.

Therefore, we deduce that the coefficients a-sub-myu-nyu must be as follows.

A-one-one equals a-two-two equals gamma; a-one-two equals a-two-one equals minus beta, which equals minus v over c. And gamma equals one over the square root of the quantity one minus beta squared, which equals one over the square root of the quantity one minus v squared over c squared.

Which are, in fact, the coefficients of the well-known Lorentz transformations.

So far, we have derived the coordinate transformations between a stationary and a moving reference frame. It is straightforward to verify that the composition of two Lorentz transformations, corresponding to scaled velocities beta-sub-one and beta-sub-two, is also a Lorentz transformation, corresponding to the scaled velocity beta equals the quantity beta-sub-one plus beta-sub-two, divided by the quantity one plus beta-sub-one times beta-sub-two. Since a transformation between any two arbitrary reference frames can be treated as a transformation from the one to the special rest frame, and from the rest frame to the other, it follows that the coordinate transformation between any two arbitrary reference frames is also a Lorentz transformation. Because all reference frames within the fabric transform between each other in like manner, it will be impossible to distinguish which one is the special rest reference frame. Thus, we have shown that the Principle of Relativity, which is the first postulate of the Theory of Special Relativity, can be deduced from the postulates of the Cosmic Fabric model.

Section 4. Discussion.

As pointed out in the introduction, the Lorentz aether theory and Einstein's Special Relativity are mathematically equivalent. In a sense, the Lorentz aether theory describes reality from the perspective of the fabric's enclosing hyperspace, while Special Relativity describes the same reality from the perspective of an observer within the fabric. The phenomenological equivalence of the Lorentz aether theory and Special Relativity with the Cosmic Fabric model is limited to the continuum length scale, and to the absence of gravity. Indeed, both the Lorentz aether theory and Special Relativity are continuum theories, which means that they treat physical space as smooth and governed by local laws, that is, laws unaffected by any global attributes of space. Furthermore, both view physical space as flat, that is Euclidean, and spacetime also as flat, that is Minkowskian. The latter limitation is removed by the Theory of General Relativity, which extends Special Relativity to curved spacetime. In this sense, one can view the Cosmic Fabric model as extending the Lorentz aether theory to account for aether curvature and time dilation, in a way similar to how General Relativity extends Special Relativity. Nevertheless, like Special Relativity, General Relativity remains strictly a continuum theory, whereas the Cosmic Fabric model includes parameters, such as its thickness, texture, and an inherent, undeformed, shape, that capture information about the structure of physical space at length scales below and above the continuum scale.

The derivation of the Lorentz transformations in Section 3 depended on the translational symmetry of the fabric, which holds true only at a continuum length scale. At lower length scales, the substructure of the fabric, namely its weave-like composition and its thickness, become significant and break the translational symmetry. The weave-like composition of the fabric, which was inferred from its Poisson ratio being unity, has been discussed in our earlier paper. Examples of materials with a Poisson ratio of unity include those studied by Rodney and colleagues, and by Baughman and Fonseca, all of which have a complex fibrous substructure. Symmetry can also be broken by structure at length scales above the continuum, such as at the length scale of the visible universe. Whenever symmetry is broken, the special rest reference frame of the cosmic fabric becomes physically detectable.

For example, red-shift observations of the cosmic microwave background can be used to define a kind of universal rest frame. For any point in space, there is exactly one inertial reference frame within which the cosmic microwave background redshift appears isotropic. Since the redshift is associated with the expansion of cosmic space, this special reference frame represents an observer that is, quote, attached, end quote, to space, and moves along with it as it expands. Except for this special reference frame, within all other reference frames the cosmic microwave background redshift will appear to have a directional dipole, indicating the direction and relative velocity of the observer. For example, the dipole we observe from Earth suggests that the Solar System is moving at about six hundred kilometers per second in the direction of the constellation Centaurus. The existence of the special cosmic-microwave-background rest reference frame means that, while local physical observations remain independent of the observer's velocity and position, at the same time cosmic-scale observations will differ according to the observer's relative motion in relation to the cosmic microwave background.

Just as a special rest frame can be identified at cosmological length scales, we speculate that, in a similar way, a special rest frame could be identified at sub-continuum length scales. For example, if space itself has a complex topological structure, as Misner and colleagues suggest, then at sufficiently small length scales, such as quantum length scales, it can no longer be modeled as a continuum. That is why a quantum theory of gravity will need to offer a way of bridging between the continuum and sub-continuum length scales. As discussed by Horstemeyer, the field of Solid Mechanics has developed techniques for bridging between length scales within a material. The Cosmic Fabric model, which treats space as a solid material body, provides a way to leverage such existing techniques in the effort to develop a quantum theory of gravity.

Section 5. Conclusion.

The work presented herein is an extension of the Cosmic Fabric model by Tenev and Horstemeyer. Our earlier paper laid the beginnings of a modern material analogy of space, but its argument was limited to stationary observers. The present work removes that limitation by showing that the analogy also applies to observers moving with respect to the cosmic medium, that is, the cosmic fabric. In the context of the continuum length scale, the two postulates of Special Relativity were deduced from the postulates of the Cosmic Fabric model, and all reference frames, whether stationary or moving, were shown to transform between each other using the Lorentz transformations. Therefore, at the continuum length scale, the special rest reference frame remains indistinguishable from all other reference frames, and the Principle of Relativity is recovered.

Herein, we identified the relativistic speed limit with the speed of signals within the cosmic fabric, that is, the speed of shear mechanical vibrations within it. In Section 3.3 of our earlier paper, we had already computed the speed of the fabric's shear waves to be the speed of light in vacuum, c. Therefore, we now conclude from the Cosmic Fabric model that the relativistic speed limit is, in fact, c.

At the continuum length scale, the Cosmic Fabric model is to the Lorentz aether theory what General Relativity is to Special Relativity. At other length scales, the Cosmic Fabric model predicts that there is a physically detectable special rest reference frame. This prediction is consistent with observations of the cosmic microwave background, and is also likely pertinent to the development of a quantum gravity theory. The combination of this work with our previously published article completes a material analogy of space, which is useful for addressing various cosmological problems. The analogy also helps apply the computational tools of solid mechanics to the solving of such problems. For example, in future work we explore the implications of physical space itself having non-trivial structure at length scales below and above the continuum scale.
