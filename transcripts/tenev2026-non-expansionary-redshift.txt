Abstract. This paper presents a non-expansionary mechanism for galactic redshift from a young-earth creationist perspective. This working hypothesis is developed through three progressively complex conceptual models that have mathematical backing: the Small Young Luminous Shell, the Large Young Luminous Shell, and the Large Young Luminous Ball. All models are based on a recently created static universe and incorporate the finite speed of gravity. The core idea is that in a newly created, static universe of finite size, not expanding from a singularity, the gravitational field has not yet reached its stable, long-term state. This temporary imbalance in the gravitational field causes a redshift that mimics accelerated expansion. We derive expressions for the redshift associated with each of the three models considered herein, and show that the Large Young Luminous Shell and Large Young Luminous Ball redshift-versus-distance relationships bear similarity with observations. In the process, we also derive a formula for the gravitational acceleration due to a spherical shell cap along its central axis.

Background. Hubble observed that distant astronomical objects show a redshift, where light spectra are shifted towards the red end, roughly proportional to their distance. He interpreted this as the Doppler effect of stellar recession, with redshift z given by z approximately equal to v over c, where v is galactic velocity and c is the speed of light. This led to the Hubble relation: v equals H-naught times x, or equivalently, z is approximately equal to H-naught over c times x, which equals x over R-sub-H. We will call this the Hubble-relation equation. Here x is distance, H-naught is the Hubble constant, and R-sub-H is the Hubble radius, the Hubble constant interpreted as a distance.

Modern mainstream cosmology interprets the observed redshift as the effect of the expansion of the universe, which lengthens light waves in transit. However, recent observations of Type One-A supernovae show that the relation in the Hubble-relation equation is not linear but has a slight downturn with distance, which is commonly understood to imply an accelerated expansion of the universe due to a hypothetical substance called dark energy. While the gravity of massive objects may also cause a nonlinear redshift, such effects cannot be used to explain Hubble's observation, because they do not apply on cosmological scales.

In this paper, we propose a novel cause of redshift that arises from the ex-nihilo creation of finite size matter, as opposed to a singularity, and the finite speed of gravity. Specifically, we show that in a recently created, inhomogeneous universe where the Distant Starlight Paradox has a solution of a certain type, one would observe a redshift even without expansion. To this end, we build on the Distant Starlight Paradox solution presented by Tenev and colleagues to show that redshift is a natural outcome of the setup described there. We offer this proposal as an additional tool for developing a young-earth creationist cosmology. While it does not necessarily contradict the concept of an expanding universe, it can be utilized alongside it to better explain astronomical observations without requiring the invocation of accelerated expansion or inflation.

Humphreys recently proposed a non-expansionary redshift mechanism due to light's electromagnetic interaction with the luminiferous ether. However, the gravity-based mechanism presented here avoids the light scattering issue, a notable drawback in Humphreys' proposal.

Method. Just like matter, we treat space as a created thing. We do this for convenience, because it lets us ascribe cosmological time tau, a function of X, at every point X in space, representing the elapsed time measured by a stationary clock at X since the creation event. For simplicity, we assume an approximately flat space under the conditions of weak gravity. This simplification is largely justified by the Planck 2018 results, which, observing the acoustic peaks of the cosmic microwave background, concluded that at very large scales the spatial curvature of the universe is roughly flat. This simplification allows us to remain within the domain of Special Relativity and make use of equations from Newtonian gravity.

We will consider several spherically symmetric setups of increasing complexity: the Small Young Luminous Shell, the Large Young Luminous Shell, and the Large Young Luminous Ball. The terms shell and ball refer to the shape of gravitating matter: for a shell, matter is distributed equidistantly from the center and has surface density sigma, while for a ball, matter is distributed evenly within a sphere and has volume density rho. The term luminous means that we consider light emitted from any point within the setup. This includes light emitted from the shell and from within its interior. We also construct the ball such that it is not opaque, but light from any distance can reach the center unobstructed. This is an idealization of a universe filled with point-like stars. Likewise, we allow for light to pass through the shell unobstructed.

Appendix C: gravitational potential evolution and photon energy loss. A potential objection to the inward-moving star-creation front model relates to the gravitational potential difference, which a static analysis might suggest causes a blueshift. This blueshift reasoning is based on treating gravitational potential as a state function: as mass accumulates, the potential at the center, reception, becomes much lower, more negative, than at the perimeter, emission. In a static field, a photon moving from higher to lower potential gains energy, implying it should arrive blueshifted.

The static conclusion described above is invalid because the gravitational potential in this model is time-dependent, that is, the partial derivative of capital fai with respect to time t is not equal to zero. The critical factor determining the frequency shift is not the scalar difference between endpoints, but the total work done by the photon against the field during its transit.

In a dynamic system, the energy change is derived by integrating the potential gradient along the photon's worldline. This energy expenditure results in a redshift.
