Abstract. The Cosmic Fabric model of gravity that was previously introduced by Tenev and Horstemeyer is further developed to provide a method for deriving the spacetime metric of the fabric's deformation due to a spherically symmetric gravitating body. We show that the derived metric is equivalent to the Schwarzschild metric up to a rescaling factor. From analyzing the membrane portion of the elastic energy due to an inclusion, we conclude that matter cannot be continuous at arbitrary sizes but must comprise discrete particles. Therefore, we show that the Cosmic Fabric model leads to results consistent with accepted theories at both continuum as well as sub-continuum length scales.

Keywords. Modified gravity, cosmic fabric, Schwarzschild metric.

Section 1. Introduction.

The Cosmic Fabric model proposed by Tenev and Horstemeyer is a formal analogy interpreting space as a solid fabric with appropriately chosen material properties, and the field equations of General Relativity as its bending equations. According to this model, gravitating bodies behave as inclusions in the fabric. These inclusions are sources of volumetric strains that cause the fabric to expand laterally into the three ordinary spatial dimensions, and consequently, to bend transversely into an additional fourth spatial dimension, as shown in Figure 1. Along this extra dimension, the fabric possesses a small thickness which is on the order of Planck's length and by which the fabric can resist bending.

The bending illustrates that for the plate's geometry to accommodate the prescribed strain, it must curve into a transverse dimension out of its original flat plane. For the geometry of the plate to accommodate the prescribed strain, the plate must bend into the transverse dimension. The figure has been reproduced from Tenev and Horstemeyer.

Whereas in our earlier paper we considered the behavior of the cosmic fabric in the exterior of matter inclusions, herein we focus on the interaction between the cosmic fabric, physical space, and matter. As in that earlier paper we assume nearly static conditions and weak gravity, small strains, in order to keep the math tractable. These conditions correspond to our ordinary experience of gravity and speeds on Earth and within our Solar System. The limitations of spherical symmetry, small strains, and nearly static bodies can be obviated in the future by solving numerically the pertinent differential equations.

How does the Cosmic Fabric Model of gravity relate to General Relativity? Previously, we had shown the equivalence between these two for the case of weak gravity with nearly static fields in the exterior of mass densities, in our earlier paper, and herein we add the case of mass density interior. We expect that in the future, the equivalence can be further extended to strong gravity and non-static fields. Rigorously demonstrating so involves incorporating additional constitutive parameters of the cosmic fabric, such as those controlling strain rate, nonlinear elasticity, and plasticity. By adjusting these, the Cosmic Fabric Model can be calibrated to provide equivalent solutions with General Relativity. Currently, the Cosmic Fabric Model does not account for electromagnetic fields, but we are considering future extensions of the Cosmic Fabric Model which will be able to incorporate electromagnetic phenomena. At the same time, we expect that there may be situations where the Cosmic Fabric Model and General Relativity diverge in their descriptions of physical phenomena. For example, one such situation is describing the interior of a black hole, for which the Cosmic Fabric Model may need additional postulates, but which General Relativity already handles. At the same time, the Cosmic Fabric Model can naturally account for space having inherent structure, which is beyond the scope of classical General Relativity. This is the notion that physical space has structure, such as intrinsic curvature that is uncaused by matter, which we have explored in a separate publication.

The contribution of this paper is twofold. First, we extend the descriptive power of the Cosmic Fabric model to include the behavior of the cosmic fabric, physical space, in the interior of matter inclusions. Consequently, we show that the Schwarzschild metric, which is a solution of the Einstein Field Equations, can also be deduced from the governing equations of the cosmic fabric. To this end, we work with spherically symmetric inclusions to provide a simple illustration of the method, but the ideas described here are not fundamentally limited to spherical symmetry. Herein, the terms quote Schwarzschild solution end quote or quote Schwarzschild metric end quote refer generally to the solution of the Einstein Field Equations for any non-rotating finite spherical body of neutral charge and not just to a delta-like mass distribution or a black hole. Second, we illustrate and validate the length-scale dependent nature of the Cosmic Fabric model as we demonstrate that it predicts the discrete nature of matter. In particular, the model shows that matter above a certain small size cannot be continuous but must be made of discrete parts. Subsequently, we validate this prediction from considering the size of common subatomic particles that make up matter.

Accomplishing the above two goals demonstrates that the Cosmic Fabric model leads to results consistent with accepted theories at both continuum as well as sub-continuum length scales. At the continuum length scale, the model yields results consistent with General Relativity, while at the sub-continuum length scale the model relates to notions from particle physics. The term quote continuum length scale end quote refers to characteristic lengths at which the physical quantities under consideration, such as the fabric strain and the relativistic energy density of inclusions, can be treated as continuous and differentiable fields. By contrast, the term quote sub-continuum length scale end quote refers to much shorter characteristic lengths at which continuity is no longer a good approximation, but the discrete nature of the underlying structure becomes significant.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews concepts from solid mechanics and the Cosmic Fabric model that are used throughout this paper. Section 3 introduces the mathematical tools used for handling the geometry of spherically symmetric hypersurfaces. In Section 4, we formulate the governing equations for the bending of the fabric and derive its spacetime metric, that is, the metric of its world volume, and in Section 5 we address the length-scale dependent nature of the membrane energy. The Discussion section, Section 6, compares the fabric's spacetime metric derived herein to the Schwarzschild metric; it also compares the shape of the fabric's deformation to Flamm's paraboloid. Finally, we summarize and conclude in Section 7.

Section 2. Preliminaries.

Below, we introduce the concepts from Solid Mechanics and the Cosmic Fabric model that will be used through the remainder of this paper. The Cosmic Fabric model identifies physical space with the mid-hypersurface of a thin four-dimensional fabric, which bends within an enclosing hyperspace. The prefix quote hyper end quote refers to an additional spatial dimension, so hyperspace is a four-dimensional, not necessarily physical, space, while hypersurface is a three-dimensional surface-like object immersed within this hyperspace.

In the following subsections we describe the specific way we assign coordinates to the material points of the fabric's mid-hypersurface, the fundamental deformation equations that use these coordinates, and the relevant postulates of the Cosmic Fabric Model.

Section 2.1. Coordinate Assignments.

We adopt the coordinate conventions as in our earlier paper, which we summarize here. The cosmic fabric, physical space, is immersed in a four-dimensional hyperspace within which it can deform. The enclosing hyperspace is flat and has been assigned Cartesian coordinates, y-superscript-K, for K running from one to four. Another set of coordinates, x-superscript-i, for i running from one to three, is painted on the fabric in the following manner: prior to deformation the x-superscript-i coordinates are painted such that they coincide with the y-superscript-i coordinates. As the fabric deforms, shown in Figure 2, the x-superscript-i coordinates remain attached to each material point and displace along with it. We call y-superscript-K hyperspatial coordinates since they pertain to the enclosing hyperspace. By contrast, we call x-superscript-i material coordinates, because they label material points on the fabric. These hyperspatial and material coordinates are analogous, respectively, to the concepts of spatial and material coordinates from solid mechanics. Note that what we consider quote material coordinates end quote from the viewpoint of Solid Mechanics are also the coordinates of physical three-dimensional space from the viewpoint of General Relativity.

Section 2.2. Deformation Basics.

The metric tensor g-sub-i-j of the fabric's mid-hypersurface defines how coordinate differences relate to distances. The three-dimensional distance d l between two nearby material points is given by, d l squared equals g-sub-i-j times d x-superscript-i times d x-superscript-j, where per Einstein's convention, summation over repeated indexes is implied. Prior to deformation, the distance between the same two material points is the following: d l-bar squared equals g-bar-sub-i-j times d x-superscript-i times d x-superscript-j.

In that equation, and for the remainder of the paper, a bar over the variable name of a quantity indicates that the quantity pertains to the undeformed configuration. For Cartesian coordinates, as adopted here, g-bar-sub-i-j equals delta-sub-i-j, where delta-sub-i-j equals delta-superscript-i-sub-j equals delta-superscript-i-j, and each equals one when i equals j, and zero when i does not equal j.

In principle, there are multiple ways to assign coordinates. The specific coordinate assignment defined here attributes physical meaning to the metric tensor g-sub-i-j as the Cauchy-Green deformation tensor from solid mechanics.

The small strain tensor epsilon-sub-i-j quantifies the amount of relative length change during deformation, and is given by the following equation: two times epsilon-sub-i-j times d x-superscript-i times d x-superscript-j equals d l squared minus d l-bar squared, which equals the quantity g-sub-i-j minus delta-sub-i-j, times d x-superscript-i times d x-superscript-j. Therefore, epsilon-sub-i-j equals one half times the quantity g-sub-i-j minus delta-sub-i-j.

The three-D volumetric strain, epsilon-3D, is defined as follows. Epsilon-3D is defined to equal epsilon-sub-i-j times g-bar-superscript-i-j, which equals epsilon-sub-i-j times delta-superscript-i-j. Therefore, epsilon-3D equals one half times the quantity g-sub-one-one plus g-sub-two-two plus g-sub-three-three, minus three.

The volumetric strain is a scalar field that represents the fractional increase of the fabric's volume, its hypersurface area. In other words, the ratio of the deformed volume element d-V-3D to the undeformed volume element d-V-bar-3D equals one plus epsilon-3D, where d-V-3D and d-V-bar-3D are, respectively, the deformed and undeformed volume elements. The definition of strain given above only applies when epsilon-sub-i-j is much less than one, which is why the term quote small strain end quote is used, and only when g-bar-sub-i-j equals delta-sub-i-j. The superscript 3D indicates that the superscripted quantity pertains to the ordinary three spatial dimensions.

Hooke's Law relates the strain epsilon-sub-i-j in a material to the stress sigma-superscript-i-k according to the following general formula: sigma-superscript-k-l equals Y over the quantity one plus nyu, times the quantity, nyu over the quantity one minus two nyu, times g-bar-superscript-i-j times g-bar-superscript-k-l, plus g-bar-superscript-i-k times g-bar-superscript-j-l, all multiplied by epsilon-sub-i-j; where Y and nyu are the Young's elastic modulus and Poisson's ratio, respectively.

According to the Cosmic Fabric model, the Poisson ratio is unity, nyu equals one, and since we assume flat unstrained geometry, g-bar-superscript-i-j equals delta-superscript-i-j, so that equation reduces to the following simpler form: sigma-superscript-k-l equals Y over two, times the quantity epsilon-superscript-k-l minus epsilon-3D times delta-superscript-k-l.

Section 2.3. The Cosmic Fabric Model.

The Cosmic Fabric model is based on the following postulates. First, physical space is identified with the mid-hypersurface of a four-dimensional hyperplate, which is thin in a fourth spatial dimension. Second, matter induces volumetric strain epsilon-3D per, the Laplacian of epsilon-3D equals minus one half times c squared times kappa times rho, where kappa is defined to equal eight pi G over c to the fourth power, and c and G are the speed of light and gravitational constant, respectively, rho is the matter-energy density, and kappa is known as the Einstein constant. In our earlier paper this postulate was stated more generally as the Laplacian of epsilon-3D being proportional to kappa times rho, while here we specify the exact constant of proportionality. Third, time lapse slows down in proportion to the volumetric expansion, so that the time-time component of the spacetime metric tensor becomes the following, see equations 2.7 and 2.10 in our earlier paper: g-sub-zero-zero equals minus the quantity one plus epsilon-3D, raised to the power minus two, which equals minus the quantity d-V-3D over d-V-bar-3D, raised to the power minus two, which equals minus one over the determinant of g-sub-i-j.

The last equality uses the fact that g-sub-i-j is the fabric's Cauchy-Green deformation tensor, and therefore its determinant is the square of the volumetric expansion. Furthermore, since we assume a nearly static condition, velocities much less than c, the time-space components of the spacetime metric tensor are negligible so that the determinant of the spacetime metric can be approximated as follows: the determinant of g-sub-myu-nyu is approximately equal to g-sub-zero-zero times the determinant of g-sub-i-j, which equals minus one.

The following are some key results of the Cosmic Fabric model, which are important to this study. First, the Poisson ratio nyu of the fabric and its Young's modulus of elasticity Y are the following: nyu equals one; Y equals twenty-four over the quantity kappa times L squared, which equals three c to the fourth power over the quantity pi G L squared, where L and kappa represent the thickness of the fabric, and Einstein's constant, respectively. Second, the bending energy U-sub-B only depends on the intrinsic spatial curvature scalar R-3D and is given by, U-sub-B equals minus one over two kappa, times R-3D.

Although the cosmic fabric is a thin hyperplate, for the purposes of this paper, it can be approximated with its mid-hypersurface, which is a three-dimensional object. Such an approximation is admissible, because the thickness of the fabric, which resists bending, is already accounted for in the computation of the bending elastic energy density U-sub-B in terms of the fabric's intrinsic curvature R-3D per the equation above.

Section 3.1. Material, Spatial, Metric Tensor.

The spatial metric g-sub-i-j can be computed from the inner product of the three surface tangent vectors, the partial derivative of y-superscript-L with respect to x-superscript-i, as follows: g-sub-i-j equals the partial derivative of y-superscript-L with respect to x-superscript-i, times the partial derivative of y-superscript-L with respect to x-superscript-j.

So, we begin by computing the first derivatives of the hyperspatial coordinates y-superscript-L. The second derivatives of y-superscript-L are also useful for the computation of other geometric quantities, such as extrinsic and intrinsic curvature, which is why we calculate both types of derivatives below. Our strategy is to first determine the general expressions, and next, evaluate these at conveniently chosen coordinates to take advantage of the spherical symmetry.

Let y-superscript-K, for K running from one to four, be the hyperspatial coordinates of a material point after deformation, and let x-superscript-i, for i running from one to three, be its material coordinates. Then: y-superscript-four equals w, by construction. The partial derivative of y-superscript-four with respect to x-superscript-i equals x-superscript-i over R-bar, times w-prime times r-prime. The second partial derivative of y-superscript-four with respect to x-superscript-i and x-superscript-j equals, x-superscript-i times x-superscript-j over R-bar squared, times the quantity w-double-prime times r-prime squared plus w-prime times r-double-prime, plus one over R-bar, times w-prime times r-prime, times the quantity delta-superscript-i-sub-j minus x-superscript-i times x-superscript-j over R-bar squared. Next, y-superscript-k equals x-superscript-k times the quantity one plus e, by construction. The partial derivative of y-superscript-k with respect to x-superscript-i equals delta-superscript-k-sub-i times the quantity one plus e, plus x-superscript-i times x-superscript-k over R-bar, times e-prime. The second partial derivative of y-superscript-k with respect to x-superscript-i and x-superscript-j equals, x-superscript-i times x-superscript-j times x-superscript-k over R-bar squared, times e-double-prime, plus the quantity delta-superscript-k-sub-i times x-superscript-j over R-bar, plus delta-superscript-k-sub-j times x-superscript-i over R-bar, plus delta-sub-i-j times x-superscript-k over R-bar, minus x-superscript-i times x-superscript-j times x-superscript-k over R-bar cubed, all times e-prime.

Section 3.2. Intrinsic Curvature.

The intrinsic curvature scalar R-3D can be computed from the extrinsic curvature tensor b-superscript-i-sub-j according to the following identity from differential geometry: R-3D equals the square of b-superscript-i-sub-i, minus b-superscript-j-sub-k times b-superscript-k-sub-j.

Furthermore, the extrinsic curvature can be computed from the derivatives of the tangent vectors, the partial derivative of y-superscript-L with respect to x-superscript-i, as follows: b-sub-i-j equals minus the second partial derivative of y-superscript-K with respect to x-superscript-i and x-superscript-j, times n-hat-superscript-K, where n-hat-superscript-K is the surface unit normal vector, and the indexes have been raised using the deformed spatial metric g-sub-i-j. Because of the spherical symmetry, we only need to work out the values of b-sub-i-j and n-hat-superscript-K for the chosen coordinates given earlier.

The normal vector n-hat is given by the following equation: n-hat-superscript-I equals one over the magnitude of n-superscript-I, times n-superscript-I, where n-superscript-I equals epsilon-superscript-I-sub-J-K-L, times the partial derivative of y-superscript-J with respect to x-superscript-one, times the partial derivative of y-superscript-K with respect to x-superscript-two, times the partial derivative of y-superscript-L with respect to x-superscript-three, and epsilon-superscript-I-sub-J-K-L is the permutation operator. Substituting the earlier first-derivative result into this equation produces the following result, the normal vector expressed as a four-component column: n-hat-superscript-one equals w-prime, over the square root of the quantity one plus w-prime squared; n-hat-superscript-two equals zero; n-hat-superscript-three equals zero; n-hat-superscript-four equals minus one, over the square root of the quantity one plus w-prime squared.

Evaluating the extrinsic-curvature definition for the chosen coordinates yields the extrinsic curvature b-sub-i-j as follows. b-sub-one-one equals w-double-prime times r-prime squared, over the square root of the quantity one plus w-prime squared. b-sub-two-two equals b-sub-three-three, which equal the quantity one plus e, squared, times w-prime, over r times the square root of the quantity one plus w-prime squared. Therefore, the components b-superscript-i-sub-k, obtained by raising the second index of b-sub-i-j with g-superscript-j-k, are as follows. b-superscript-one-sub-one equals b-sub-one-one over g-sub-one-one, which equals w-double-prime over the quantity one plus w-prime squared, raised to the power three-halves. b-superscript-two-sub-two equals b-sub-two-two over g-sub-two-two, which equals w-prime over r, times the quantity one plus w-prime squared, raised to the power one-half. b-superscript-three-sub-three equals b-sub-three-three over g-sub-three-three, which equals the same expression, w-prime over r, times the quantity one plus w-prime squared, raised to the power one-half. And b-superscript-i-sub-j equals zero whenever i does not equal j.

The following variable substitution helps simplify the algebra in the remaining equations. Let u be defined as follows: u is defined to equal w-prime squared, over two times the quantity one plus w-prime squared.

As we shall see later, u also happens to represent the portion of the volumetric stretch that is due to transverse displacement into the fourth spatial dimension. In terms of u, the displacement w and its derivatives are as follows. W-prime squared equals two u, times the quantity one minus two u, raised to the power minus one. W-prime squared plus one equals the quantity one minus two u, raised to the power minus one. W-prime equals the square root of two u, times the quantity one minus two u, raised to the power minus one-half. W-double-prime equals the quantity two u, raised to the power minus one-half, times the quantity one minus two u, raised to the power minus three-halves.

When the above identities are substituted into the earlier result for b-superscript-i-sub-j, we obtain the following result for the extrinsic curvature, again a diagonal array. The one-one entry equals u-prime, over the square root of two u; the two-two entry equals the square root of two u, over r; the three-three entry equals the same, the square root of two u, over r; and off-diagonal entries are zero.

Substituting this result into the identity for R-3D, and taking into consideration the identity that r times u-prime, plus u, equals the derivative with respect to r of the product r times u, produces the following result for the intrinsic curvature scalar: R-3D equals four times the derivative with respect to r of the quantity r times u, all divided by r squared, where u is defined as before, w-prime squared over two times the quantity one plus w-prime squared.

Notice that R-3D only depends on the transverse displacement w and not on the horizontal stretch e. This is expected, because according to continuum mechanics theory, compatible deformations do not affect the intrinsic curvature. A quote compatible end quote deformation is one that does not introduce overlaps or tears in the material. Therefore, any purely three-D deformation will not contribute to the three-D intrinsic curvature.

Section 4. Spacetime Metric due to Spherically Symmetric Inclusion.

In this section we formulate the equations that govern the interaction between a spherically symmetric inclusion and the fabric, and we calculate the resulting spacetime metric. We work in the limit of nearly static bodies and small strains to determine the fabric deformation caused by an inclusion. The result, expressed in terms of the metric tensor that characterizes the deformation, represents the spatial components of the fabric's spacetime metric. Section 6.2. Flamm's Paraboloid.

The paraboloid is generated by revolving a parabola around its directrix within the same four-dimensional space. Section 6.3. Generalization Using Numerical Methods.

The method for calculating the cosmic fabric's metric can be readily generalized to non-flat initial geometry and asymmetric configuration. In such a case, the governing equations reasoned in Section 4.1 do not necessarily have closed form solutions but must be solved numerically. If the initial geometry of the fabric and its inclusion are spherically symmetric, then the method still reduces to a single first order ordinary differential equation, which is straightforward to solve using well known numerical techniques. If the configuration is not spherically symmetric, however, it may still be possible to approximate it with linear superposition of a finite number of spherically symmetric sub-configurations. These can be solved individually, and their solutions superimposed. Such a decomposition into spherically symmetric configurations and the subsequent linear superposition is valid in the regime of small strains, weak gravity.

Another potentially straightforward generalization of our method is for the spherically symmetric case of large strains, strong gravity. The majority of the steps used in earlier sections that derive the various kinematic and energy quantities of the fabric are admissible for large strains. The calculations for the metric tensor, extrinsic, and intrinsic curvature are such examples. The small strain assumption is mainly a convenience to avoid complicated radicals in the closed form solution, but that is not an issue when using numerical techniques.

Section 6.4. Bridging between Length Scales.

As we examine matter-fabric interaction at the continuum length scale, we are led to quote peek end quote at the substructure, sub-continuum, length scale, and eventually conclude per Section 5.2 that matter must be made of discrete particles. The notion of quote bridging between length scales end quote refers to the description of how phenomena at a lower length scale are aggregated to manifest at the length scale above, and conversely, what constraints are imposed by observations at a higher length scale onto the lower length scale models. Theories that bridge between length scales include a length parameter that controls the transition between scales. The fabric's thickness L plays this role for the Cosmic Fabric Model.

One might ask whether using the fabric's thickness L as a length scale parameter could lead to violating the Lorentz invariance. Ostensibly, there is a preferred reference frame wherein the fabric's thickness has the value of L, but in a boosted frame the thickness will be different due to the Lorentz contraction. Since a Lorentz contraction occurs in the direction of motion, which must be within the three ordinary spatial dimensions, the fabric's thickness will not be directly affected by it. Nevertheless, Lorentz contraction is still a factor since L is used as a length scale against which the lengths of moving objects are compared. For example, consider a body in a boosted reference frame, whose length shortens from being much larger than L at rest to being comparable to or less than L; such a body would have transitioned from one length scale regime to another as it was boosted and will experience physical laws differently from one reference frame to the other. Such an outcome, which is indeed a consequence of the Cosmic Fabric Model, would constitute violation of the Lorentz invariance. However, achieving such a length scale transition requires boost speeds that are too close to the speed of light c to be experimentally attainable. For example, the proton diameter is about ten to the twentieth power times larger than Planck's length, and so it is about that much larger than L. For the proton to achieve length contraction of such order requires a boost speed that approaches c to within a fraction of ten to the minus fortieth power. By comparison, CERN's Large Hadron Collider accelerates protons to only within ten to the minus tenth power of the speed of light. For larger bodies, the boost speed required to cross between length scale regimes will have to be even greater than what is required for the proton. So, while the Cosmic Fabric Model does suggest the theoretical possibility for violating the Lorentz invariance, doing so requires extreme conditions that are beyond practical observation.

The thickness of the cosmic fabric along a fourth spatial dimension is ostensibly reminiscent of models of large extra dimensions that have been proposed to explain why gravity is much weaker than the weak force, which is known as the quote hierarchy problem end quote. As with these models, the fabric's thickness L only affects gravitational flux at length scales comparable to L, but for much larger length scales the fabric behaves as an essentially three-dimensional object. This, however, is the extent of the similarity. The models of large extra dimensions must rely on a large number of additional dimensions to quote hide end quote the gravitational flux at short range. By contrast, the Cosmic Fabric Model introduces only one extra dimension beyond the ordinary three, and for a different reason: the fabric's thickness creates the needed resistance to bending, so that the Einstein Field Equations can be interpreted as the bending equations of a thin plate, as discussed in our earlier paper.

Other theories which have attempted to bridge the continuum and substructure length scales of physical space include the theory of Causal Sets, Loop Quantum Gravity, and Causal Dynamic Triangulation. Causal Sets treats spacetime as a collection of discrete events connected through causal relationships; in Loop Quantum Gravity, space has extremely small substructure consisting of interwoven loops on the order of Planck's length; Causal Dynamic Triangulation is a complementary idea to Loop Quantum Gravity, treating four-dimensional spacetime as arising from a two-dimensional spacetime near the Planck scale. Unlike the Cosmic Fabric Model discussed here, all three of the aforementioned theories are formulated at the substructure length scale, whereas the Cosmic Fabric Model's formulation is at the continuum length scale and offers, for now, limited perspective on the length scales below. In this context, the Cosmic Fabric Model may be complementary with Causal Sets, Causal Dynamic Triangulation, and especially with Loop Quantum Gravity, because it is consistent with these theories in the following significant ways. They all seek to explain physical phenomena at multiple length scales, they all allow for space to have discontinuous substructure, and both the Cosmic Fabric Model and Loop Quantum Gravity point to the notion that space and matter are fundamentally similar to each other.

Section 7. Summary and Conclusion.

In this paper, we advanced the Cosmic Fabric model beyond what Tenev and Horstemeyer accomplished in earlier work, by investigating the details of how mass-energy inclusions interact with the fabric and cause it to bend. This investigation complemented the initial work reported in our earlier paper, which had focused on how the cosmic fabric bends outside of inclusions. In the process, we developed a method for calculating the spacetime metric of the cosmic fabric, which we applied for the case of initially flat fabric and a spherically symmetric inclusion. The derived metric was equivalent to Schwarzschild's up to a rescaling factor that accounted for in-plane stretch. Furthermore, we showed that the fabric bends into the shape known as Flamm's Paraboloid.

We also demonstrated the scale-dependent nature of the Cosmic Fabric model, which is linked to its thickness. From an investigation of the total elastic energy due to an inclusion, we determined an upper limit for the mass of the largest possible continuous inclusion. We showed that the total membrane energy W-sub-M varies disproportionately with the size of the inclusion and is therefore length-scale dependent, where the fabric's thickness L serves as the length-scale parameter. To avoid disproportionately large membrane energy, we reasoned that there must be a limit on the largest possible continuous material body, which we expressed in Sections 5.1. Consequently, we concluded that matter must have discontinuous substructure, as illustrated in Figure 6. This result is consistent with the known constitution of matter at atomic and subatomic length scales.

In conclusion, the work presented herein further validates the Cosmic Fabric model of gravity by showing that it leads to equivalent results as linearized General Relativity. Unlike General Relativity, however, the Cosmic Fabric model includes a built-in length-scale parameter, which leads to interesting predictions such as the discrete nature of matter. By contrast, General Relativity is strictly a continuum theory and therefore has no means of modeling substructure length scales. The length-scale dependence that is part of the Cosmic Fabric model but lacks in General Relativity is a necessary and useful aspect of the model that helps bridge between a theory of gravity and other physical theories, such as fundamental theories of matter.
