Section 1. Introduction.

De Swart and colleagues present a good review of the historical analysis of dark matter. The notion of dark matter was introduced in the 1920s and 1930s by Kapteyn, Oort, Zwicky, Holmberg, and Smith. Later, in the 1970s and 1980s, the notion was popularized by Rubin and colleagues as a way to explain anomalous rotational curves of galaxies. In addition, dark matter was also invoked to explain gravitational lensing, which was discovered by Lynds and Petrosian. Herein we will use the term dark-matter effect to describe such observations of anomalous gravity. The development of the Standard Cosmological Model, also known as the Lambda-CDM model, where the letters C D M stand for Cold Dark Matter, appealed to dark matter as a means to balance the contents of the universe and provide a mechanism for structure formation during the early epoch since its inception. In this context, the Lambda-CDM model predicted that dark matter must be non-baryonic and has to make up a certain fraction of the total contents of the cosmos, namely about twenty-seven percent, which is more than five times the ordinary, that is baryonic, matter which, according to the Lambda-CDM model, is supposed to comprise only about five percent of the cosmic content. Whereas the dark-matter effect provides an observational support for dark matter, by contrast, the idea that dark matter must make up twenty-seven percent of the contents of the universe is model dependent.

Despite the overwhelming evidence for the dark-matter effect, there has been no direct confirmation for the existence of dark matter, such as would be, for example, the discovery of the particle responsible for it. The lack of direct evidence for dark matter has prompted the development of other models to explain the dark-matter effect, such as the Modified Newtonian Dynamics theory, or MOND, introduced in the 1980s by Milgrom. According to MOND, Newton's Second Law of motion must be modified so that in the case of very weak acceleration a, such that a is much less than a-naught, where a-naught is a universal acceleration scale parameter, the force F associated with a is no longer linear with respect to a but is proportional to its square. A more narrow formulation of MOND is one where only Newton's Gravitational Law needs to be modified, as follows. Equation 6.1: m times a equals F, which equals G times M times m, divided by the quantity myu of a over a-naught, times l-bar squared; with a-naught equal to one point two times ten to the minus ten meters per second squared. Here M and m are, respectively, the mass of a gravitating body and that of a test particle situated a distance l-bar from each other and attracted to each other with force F. Also, myu of x is an interpolation function such that myu of x tends to x for x much less than zero, and myu of x tends to one otherwise. Using this simple modification to the Gravity Law, MOND has been successful in explaining the dark-matter effect for a great majority of observations. However, there have also been notable outliers, such as galaxies appearing to have too little dark matter to fit into MOND's simple one-parameter model. Randriamampandry and Carignan show that among a sample of fifteen galaxies, six do not fit well MOND if a-naught were treated as a universal constant but prefer larger or smaller values for it compared to the one given in Equation 6.1. Problems such as these, and also MOND's empirical nature, that is the lack of satisfactory explanation from first principles, have been continual sources of criticism. Most recently, Boran and colleagues have argued that the detection of gravitational waves known as the event G W one seven zero eight one seven has falsified MOND and other so-called Dark Matter Emulator theories, as they call them, because these would have predicted, contrary to observations, that photons and gravitational waves move along different geodesics.

Herein we propose an alternative explanation to the dark-matter effect, which we call the Inherent Structure Hypothesis. The Inherent Structure Hypothesis is the idea that physical space has inherent structure, such as inherent curvature, that exists apart from matter and leads to modified gravity effects. While the Inherent Structure Hypothesis refers to inherent structure in general, herein we focus specifically on inherent curvature being one of its quantifiable attributes.

The Cosmic Fabric Model analogy of General Relativity proposed in Chapters three through five helps motivate and analyze the effect of the Inherent Structure Hypothesis. In the context of the material model of space proposed in this dissertation work, if solid matter can have structure, and space is like a solid object, then it is reasonable to suppose that space, too, has structure. The work presented here fits within the current limitations of the Cosmic Fabric Model, namely weak gravity and nearly static fields, because the dark-matter effect is observed at such conditions.

The inherent curvature figures as the additional script-L-bar term in Equation 1.1 from Chapter one. The extra Lagrangian term represents a modification to the equations of General Relativity, Equation 1.3, because it implies that in the absence of any matter-energy fields, that is, with T-sub-myu-nyu equal to zero, the intrinsic curvature does not vanish, that is, R-sub-myu-nyu is not equal to zero, but equals the inherent curvature of space. Since the Cosmic Fabric Model is an analogy of General Relativity, any results derived through it should also be derivable from conventional General Relativity once its field equations have been modified to account for background spatial curvature. Because the Inherent Structure Hypothesis does not invoke new physics, but only a new initial configuration, namely an initially curved physical space, it therefore avoids the flaw that Boran and colleagues point out regarding other dark-matter-emulator theories.

The idea that the dark-matter effect has a geometrical explanation is not new, but has received relatively little attention so far. For example, Bohmer and colleagues, and later Usman, propose an, quote, f of R modified theory of gravity, end quote, to explain the dark-matter effect, where the Ricci scalar R, which figures in the Einstein-Hilbert action, is replaced with some more general expression, f of R. In a limited sense, our approach can be viewed as a special case of an f-of-R theory, provided that the inherent curvature of space were constant and can be incorporated as a parameter into f of R. However, per the Inherent Structure Hypothesis proposed here, the inherent curvature must be a field, and so the Inherent Structure Hypothesis is not the same as an f-of-R theory. Dolginov does consider the inherent geometry of space as the cause for the dark-matter effect and offers several arguments against the conventional dark-matter explanation, such as the absence of dense dark-matter clouds. He states that such problems do not exist if, quote, the dark matter effect is a result of local non-flat geometry of the empty space, end quote. However, Dolginov goes only as far as to raise the possibility for the role that inherent structure plays, but comes short of quantifying the effect, and does not compare it to existing dark-matter models as we have done here.

This chapter presents the case for the Inherent Structure Hypothesis, namely that the dark-matter effect is the manifestation of the inherent structure of cosmic space above continuum length scale. We show that the inherent curvature of space amplifies the gravity of ordinary matter that resides in it, and we quantify the effect, demonstrating that it can feasibly be one and the same as the dark-matter effect. By inherent curvature we mean the curvature of space that is uncaused by any matter inclusions. In the context of the Cosmic Fabric analogy of physical space, the inherent curvature corresponds to the neutral shape of the cosmic medium prior to it being tensed or compressed. As part of our presentation, we analyze the range of observations for which the Inherent Structure Hypothesis produces equivalent results to other models, such as the dark-matter or MOND explanations, and we propose ways in which the Inherent Structure Hypothesis can be experimentally distinguished from these models. In order that we can work with closed-form expressions, our calculations are for a spherically symmetric configuration and nearly static conditions, but such limitations are not fundamental to the ideas presented here. Furthermore, although we develop the Inherent Structure Hypothesis in the context of the Cosmic Fabric Model, its validity does not depend on said model, and the same conclusions can be reached by considering solutions to the General Relativity equations that have been modified to account for inherent intrinsic curvature.

In the remainder of the chapter, we introduce the mathematical tools used for handling spherically symmetric inherent curvature, in Section two, after which we derive the expressions for how said curvature affects gravity of ordinary matter, in Section three, and how much of it is required to reproduce the effect of a given hypothetical dark-matter distribution. In the discussion, Section four, we analyze the conditions under which the Inherent Structure Hypothesis proposed here is observationally equivalent with dark matter, and we offer ways to distinguish between the two explanations; we also compare the Inherent Structure Hypothesis with MOND in the context of sample galactic data, and discuss implication to cosmological models and future work. Finally, we summarize and conclude in Section five.

Section 4. Discussion.

Below we compare the Inherent Structure Hypothesis to other explanations of the dark-matter effect, and propose how these can be experimentally distinguished from one another. We also discuss the implication of the Inherent Structure Hypothesis to cosmological models.

Section 4.1. Conditions for observational equivalence and falsifiability.

We analyze below under what conditions the Inherent Structure Hypothesis is observationally equivalent to the dark-matter explanation of the dark-matter effect. For this purpose, we consider two questions. First, can the effect of any given dark-matter distribution be equivalently explained by a geometrically consistent inherent curvature? And second, can the effect of any given inherent curvature be equivalently explained by a physically admissible dark-matter distribution? By geometrically consistent we mean that the fabric's material does not intersect itself and has no kinks. Also, for the dark-matter distribution to be physically admissible, we require that it has non-negative and finite density. Below, we answer the above questions in the context of spherical symmetry, but we expect that the responses also apply more generally.

To answer the first question, consider the radial functions, M-sub-dark-matter, a function of r-bar, and M, also a function of r-bar, representing, respectively, the hypothetical dark-matter mass and ordinary mass enclosed within some coordinate radius r-bar that corresponds to proper distance from the center, l-bar, itself a function of r-bar. According to the Dark Matter Hypothesis, one would expect that the proper acceleration is a equals minus G times the quantity M plus M-sub-dark-matter, over l-bar squared, which, in view of Equation 6.23, implies Equation 6.24: l-bar equals r-bar times the square root of the quantity one plus M-sub-dark-matter over M. From this follows Equation 6.25: l-bar-prime equals the square root of the quantity one plus M-sub-dark-matter over M, plus r-bar times the derivative of the ratio M-sub-dark-matter over M, divided by two times that same square root.

Equation 6.25 fully specifies the equivalent inherent curvature, because once l-bar-prime is known, the displacement function w-bar can be computed from Equation 6.9 up to a rigid translation. For the resulting curvature to be geometrically consistent, we require that l-bar-prime lie between one and infinity, not including infinity itself. From Equation 6.25, it is clear that except in the complete absence of visible matter, that is when M equals zero, it must be the case that l-bar-prime is finite. Furthermore, as long as the ratio between dark and visible matter increases with distance from the center, then the derivative of that ratio is non-negative, and so l-bar-prime is at least one. Both of these are consistent with known observations, because the dark-matter effect is always observed along with visible matter. Also, dark matter is supposed to dominate the exterior of the galaxies, so that the ratio of the dark to visible matter content enclosed within a given radius increases in the outward direction. Therefore, any known distribution of dark matter can be modeled as inherent curvature.

To answer the second question, we now consider how an inherent-curvature profile specified by the proper-distance function l-bar, as a function of r-bar, is interpreted as dark-matter content, M-sub-dark-matter, also as a function of r-bar. For simplicity, we will focus on distances sufficiently far away from the center of symmetry beyond which the enclosed visible mass does not increase appreciably, so that M of r-bar is approximately constant. This simplification is consistent with the structure of galaxies and dark-matter models, where most of the visible mass is concentrated within the galactic center, while the dark-matter halo is supposed to extend well beyond the visible mass of the galaxy. By rearranging Equation 6.24 we obtain Equation 6.26: M-sub-dark-matter equals M times the quantity l-bar squared over r-bar squared, minus one; and its derivative, M-sub-dark-matter primed, equals two times M times l over r-bar squared, times the quantity l-bar-prime minus l over r-bar. Here l, unbarred, again denotes the deformed proper distance, as distinguished from the inherent, or undeformed, distance l-bar.

For M-sub-dark-matter to be physically admissible, we require that its derivative be non-negative, since the reverse implies negative dark-matter density. Therefore, we require, as Equation 6.27 states, that l-bar-prime be greater than or equal to l over r-bar. Beyond the boundary of the hypothetical dark-matter halo, the derivative of M-sub-dark-matter is zero, so l-bar-prime equals l-bar over r-bar, implying that l-bar-prime is constant there. Therefore, within the dark-matter halo, where that derivative is positive, we would expect that l-bar-prime is monotonically increasing. So, in general, l-bar-prime has to be non-decreasing for the dark-matter effect due to inherent curvature to be explainable by actual dark matter. In other words, we conclude with Equation 6.28: l-bar-double-prime is greater than or equal to zero.

Given the relationship between w-bar and l-bar, where w-bar-prime squared equals l-bar-prime squared minus one, Equation 6.28 means that the radial function w-bar of r-bar should not change concavity for the equivalent dark matter to be physically admissible as such.

Unless the inherent curvature of space can be maintained globally for the entire cosmos, it will necessarily be the case that beyond a certain critical radius, r-sub-c, the condition stated in Equation 6.28 no longer applies. As Figures two and three illustrate, beyond r-sub-c, the concavity of w-bar of r-bar must reverse for the local inherent curvature to return to flat, or to some lesser curvature that can be maintained globally. Therefore, beyond such r-sub-c the Inherent Structure and the Dark Matter hypotheses are no longer observationally equivalent.

Beyond the critical radius r-sub-c, the expected observational differences between the Inherent Structure and the Dark Matter explanations provide a way to verify one and falsify the other. In particular, per the Inherent Structure Hypothesis: first, at sufficiently large distances from the center of the hypothetical dark-matter halo, the dark-matter effect will reverse and eventually disappear as if the halo were not present — in other words, a test particle orbiting a galaxy well beyond the critical distance r-sub-c will behave as if no dark matter were enclosed within its orbit; second, gravitational systems for which the dark-matter effect is observed will exhibit a relatively more pronounced edge at approximately the critical distance r-sub-c, compared to gravitational systems that do not exhibit the dark-matter effect; and third, on the cosmic length scale, the hypothetical dark matter will have no net contributions to the contents of the universe — note that the currently estimated twenty-seven percent dark-matter content is a model-dependent result and therefore does not necessarily falsify the Inherent Structure Hypothesis. Confirming or falsifying any of the above predictions will either confirm or falsify the Inherent Structure Hypothesis.

Section 4.3. Implication to cosmological models.

Like MOND and other dark-matter alternative models, the Inherent Structure Hypothesis is incompatible with the Lambda-CDM model, which critically depends on the existence of non-baryonic dark matter. Even more fundamentally, the Lambda-CDM model depends on the presupposition known as the Cosmological Principle, that at the cosmic length scale, greater than one hundred megaparsecs, or three times ten to the twenty-fourth meters, the universe is homogeneous and isotropic, which in essence is a presupposition about the absence of structure at that scale.

By contrast, the Inherent Structure Hypothesis is based on the presupposition that structure is a fundamental property of nature, and as such, it is an essential element in cosmological models that adopt the same view. Indeed, common experience shows that every sufficiently complex functioning system exhibits structure at its greatest length scale, and so the cosmos should be no exception. For this reason, it is quite likely that new cosmological data will soon conclusively repudiate the Cosmological Principle, triggering the revision of the Standard Cosmological Model to account for structure at every length scale. The Inherent Structure Hypothesis will be well fitted for such a revised cosmological model.

What would a cosmological model based on the presupposition of structure look like? According to the Inherent Structure Hypothesis, the inherent curvature of space is uncaused by matter, and yet, the galaxy data in Table 1 demonstrates a correlation between inherent curvature and matter. Therefore, one must conclude that there is a causal relationship after all, but in reverse: the inherent structure of space is what causes matter to form galaxies and galactic clusters in the first place. This idea may also explain the so-called Large Scale Structure of the universe, consisting of walls and filaments made up of galaxies and galactic clusters that appear organized into definite forms, but are not gravitationally bound together. New cosmological models may appeal to the inherent structure of space as the seed needed for matter-structure formation, similarly to how the Lambda-CDM model appeals to dark matter for the same purpose.

Section 5. Summary and conclusion.

In this chapter we showed that the inherent curvature of physical space, that is, curvature uncaused by matter, amplifies the gravitational effects of ordinary matter and produces the kind of gravitational anomalies that are currently attributed to the presence of dark matter. We proposed the Inherent Structure Hypothesis, stating that the so-called dark-matter effect is the manifestation of the inherent structure of space at galactic length scales, and not the result of invisible mass.

We validated the Inherent Structure Hypothesis by demonstrating that any dark-matter effect, which can be explained by the Modified Newtonian Dynamics theory or by the presence of a dark-matter halo, can be equally well explained by the Inherent Structure Hypothesis. At the same time, we showed that the Inherent Structure Hypothesis allows for dark-matter effects that cannot be explained by MOND or by the dark-matter halos. The inherent-structure explanation is equivalent to the dark-matter explanation to within some distance from the center of a gravitating system, but beyond that distance, the Inherent Structure Hypothesis predicts that the gravitational impact of the hypothetical dark matter begins to be reversed and is nearly completely eliminated at sufficiently far distances. This is a verifiable prediction that would distinguish our model from other explanations of the dark-matter effect.

In the comparison between the Inherent Structure Hypothesis and MOND we noted an interesting relationship between the size of a gravitational system and its Schwarzschild radius through the MOND parameter a-naught. Such relationship hinted at the structural underpinnings of the dark-matter effect.

The Inherent Structure Hypothesis stems from the principle that structure is a fundamental aspect of matter, space, and nature in general, and as such can be incorporated into cosmological models that subscribe to the same principle.
