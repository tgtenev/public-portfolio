Chapter One. Introduction.

The dissertation investigates a two-part proposition. First, physical space exhibits material-like properties. This idea is developed into the Cosmic Fabric Model of gravity, a formal analogy that treats the vacuum of three-dimensional space as a solid body, a thin elastic plate, and the field equations of General Relativity as the bending equations of that body. Second, having material nature, space has inherent structure at multiple length scales. This Inherent Structure Hypothesis offers a new approach to open problems in cosmology, in particular explaining the gravitational anomalies currently attributed to dark matter.

In the model, space is identified with a solid hyperplate called the cosmic fabric: broad in the three ordinary spatial dimensions and very thin along a fourth spatial dimension. Matter behaves as inclusions in this fabric, think of grains embedded in a sheet, that force the surrounding material to strain, causing the fabric to bend into the fourth dimension. The fabric's intrinsic curvature corresponds to the curvature of space, and its volumetric strain, the fractional change in volume, corresponds to the slowing of clocks, which, for weak gravity, is the same as the gravitational potential.

Why take a material view of space seriously. Material models of space, historically aether theories, go back to Aristotle, and were developed by Newton, Laplace, Kelvin, and Maxwell, who used mechanical pictures of space to build electromagnetism. The line culminated in the Lorentz aether theory: Lorentz explained the null result of the Michelson-Morley experiment, which found no motion of Earth through the aether, by showing that moving clocks slow down and moving rods contract in exactly the way needed to make the aether's rest frame undetectable.

Einstein's Special Relativity, from 1905, seemed to make the aether unnecessary, not wrong, just superfluous. But after developing General Relativity, Einstein himself revisited the question, arguing in his 1920 Leiden lecture that, quote, to deny the aether is ultimately to assume that empty space has no physical qualities whatever, end quote, which conflicts with the fundamental facts of mechanics, since rotation, for instance, is physically detectable even in otherwise empty space. Moreover, practical applications of General Relativity often require separating space from time: the Arnowitt, Deser, and Misner formalism, known by its initials A D M, used in numerical relativity does exactly that. A material model of space, which views space as an object progressing in time, does this separation naturally.

The clue: Hooke's Law resembles Einstein's equation. The specific motivation for the model is a structural similarity between the two fields' central laws. Hooke's Law for an isotropic linear elastic material relates the stress tensor, sigma-superscript-k-l, internal force per unit area, to the strain tensor, epsilon-sub-i-j, dimensionless deformation:

sigma-superscript-k-l equals the quantity Y over the quantity one plus nyu, times the quantity, nyu over the quantity one minus two nyu, times g-superscript-i-j times g-superscript-k-l, plus g-superscript-i-k times g-superscript-j-l, end inner quantity, times epsilon-sub-i-j.

Here Y is Young's modulus, or stiffness, and nyu is Poisson's ratio, how much a stretched material narrows sideways. Einstein's field equations of General Relativity read:

T-sub-myu-nyu equals one over kappa, times the quantity R-sub-myu-nyu minus one half times R times g-sub-myu-nyu.

Here T-sub-myu-nyu is the stress-energy tensor, energy and momentum content; R-sub-myu-nyu and R measure spacetime curvature; g-sub-myu-nyu is the spacetime metric; and kappa, defined as eight pi G over c to the fourth power, is the Einstein constant. Both laws relate stress, on the left side, to deformation, on the right side, a constitutive relation, the kind of material-specific law that distinguishes steel from rubber. The apparent mismatch is dimensional: strain is dimensionless while curvature has dimensions of one over length squared. The resolution, developed in Chapter Three, is to compare Einstein's equation not to stretching but to plate bending, where stress is proportional to the second spatial derivative of strain.

A century of related work has connected mechanics and relativity from various directions: relativistic generalizations of elasticity, entropy-based derivations treating spacetime as an elastic solid, and fluid analogies of gravity, which fall short because fluids carry no shear waves, while gravitational waves are transverse. Notably, Hehl and Kiefer independently arrived at elastic constants for a cosmic medium equivalent to Poisson's ratio nyu equals one, the same value Chapter Three derives. What distinguishes this dissertation is the insight that Einstein's equation corresponds to the bending of a plate rather than to simple stretch or shear; without it, the detailed correspondence between space and a material medium remains obscured.

Length scales and inherent structure. A length scale is a range of distances over which certain physical laws and parameters dominate. The dissertation considers four: substructure, from ten to the minus thirty-six meters up to ten to the minus ten meters, the discrete make-up of the fabric itself; continuum, from ten to the minus ten meters up to ten to the fourteen meters, where space is smooth and General Relativity applies; structure, from ten to the fourteen meters up to three times ten to the twenty-four meters, galactic scales where dark-matter anomalies appear; and cosmic, above three times ten to the twenty-four meters, the global geometry of the universe.

The analogy: at scales under a kilometer the Earth's surface is well approximated as flat; between one and one hundred kilometers local terrain matters; beyond one hundred kilometers the globe's curvature dominates. Those boundaries are not fundamental constants, they reflect how the Earth happens to be structured. Likewise, the Inherent Structure Hypothesis proposes that space has structure at galactic and cosmic scales reflecting the organization of the universe. Formally, this enters the Lagrangian, or action, formulation of gravity as an extra term, script-L-bar:

script-S equals the integral over omega of the quantity script-L minus script-L-bar plus script-L-sub-M, d-omega.

Here script-L is the Lagrangian density from spacetime curvature, script-L-sub-M accounts for matter-energy fields, and script-L-bar, a term not previously considered, is the correction due to the inherent, that is undeformed, curvature of space. Without script-L-bar, the Cosmic Fabric model reproduces conventional General Relativity, the subject of Chapters Three through Five; with it, the model modifies General Relativity at galactic scales and above, the subject of Chapter Six.

Dark matter and MOND. Galaxies rotate too fast: stars far from the galactic center orbit at speeds that the visible mass cannot gravitationally sustain. Similar anomalies appear in the motions and gas pressure of galaxy clusters. The dissertation calls these observations the dark matter effect and distinguishes them from the dark matter hypothesis, the proposal that invisible mass halos cause them. Despite decades of searches, no dark matter particle or compact object has been directly detected.

The leading alternative, Milgrom's Modified Newtonian Dynamics, called MOND, postulates that for accelerations below a universal parameter a-naught, approximately one point two times ten to the minus ten meters per second squared, Newton's law is modified. MOND fits many galactic rotation curves remarkably well with a single parameter, but it remains empirical, has notable outlier galaxies, and lacks a settled theoretical foundation. Intriguingly, two pi times a-naught is approximately equal to c times H-naught, where H-naught is Hubble's parameter, hinting that a-naught may be related to the inherent geometry of the cosmos rather than being a new constant of nature. Chapter Six returns to this.

Assumptions. Throughout, two simplifying assumptions apply; they keep the math tractable and are not fundamental to the model. The first is weak gravity: the gravitational potential satisfies the condition that the absolute value of the quantity capital fai over c squared is much less than one, true nearly everywhere — at Earth's surface, the Sun contributes about ten to the minus eight. The second is nearly static fields: gravitating masses move much slower than light, so time derivatives are negligible.

Chapter Three. The Cosmic Fabric Model of gravity.

This chapter constructs the analogy in detail: it specifies a material body whose behavior, derived solely from Hooke's Law, is demonstrably analogous to spacetime's behavior per General Relativity.

The picture. Physical space is identified with the mid-surface of the cosmic fabric, a four-dimensional plate, thin along the fourth spatial dimension x-superscript-four, immersed in a flat four-dimensional reference space. Matter-energy acts as inclusions that prescribe strain in the fabric. Because the prescribed strain is uneven, larger near the inclusion, the fabric must bend into the fourth dimension to accommodate it geometrically.

The figure shows a plate in two panels: panel a shows it in flat geometry, and panel b shows it bent into curved geometry, the bending caused by an inclusion that prescribes an uneven strain field, drawn with dashed lines and diverging arrows showing larger strain near the center that tapers off with distance. The caption reads: A plate bending from flat geometry, panel a, into a curved geometry, panel b, because of an inclusion that prescribes an uneven strain field, dashed lines, diverging arrows: larger strain near the center, tapering off with distance. To accommodate the prescribed strain, the plate must bend into the transverse dimension.

This is the mechanical counterpart of matter curves spacetime. Two coordinate systems track the deformation: material coordinates, x-superscript-I, painted onto the fabric, which move with it as it deforms, and reference coordinates, y-superscript-I, fixed in the enclosing hyperspace. An observer inside the fabric can only ever measure with rulers that are themselves part of the fabric, so stretching of the fabric is perceived from within as expansion of space, and differential stretching as intrinsic curvature of space. The reference space is a mathematical scaffold, not necessarily a physical entity.

The caption reads: Material coordinates x-superscript-I versus reference coordinates y-superscript-I, before, panel a, and after, panel b, deformation of a body script-B. Material coordinates are carried along with the material as it deforms; reference coordinates remain fixed.

Three postulates. The first postulate: elastic thin hyperplate. The fabric is a stack of three-dimensional elastic layers, each isotropic and obeying Hooke's Law as stated above. Its small thickness, L, comparable to Planck's length, l-sub-p, approximately one point six times ten to the minus thirty-five meters, creates resistance to bending, while leaving the fabric an essentially three-dimensional object at ordinary scales.

The second postulate: inclusions. Matter is a source of volumetric strain, epsilon, the fractional volume change of the fabric:

the Laplacian of epsilon equals minus one half times c squared times kappa times rho.

Here rho is the matter-energy density. Equivalently, the Schwarzschild-radius density of matter, its mass converted to a geometric length, is what sources strain. How pointlike particles move freely through a stiff fabric is answered by their wave nature, since waves propagate readily through stiff media; the model abstracts those details and takes the prescribed strain field as its input.

The third postulate: lapse rate. All matter-matter interactions are mediated by signals propagating in the fabric as shear waves. Where those waves propagate slower, relative to the reference space, all physical processes, hence all clocks, run proportionally slower, regardless of the clock's design. Quantitatively, the shear wave speed, v-sub-s, and the rate of proper time tau, clock time, relative to coordinate time t, both scale as:

v-sub-s equals c over the quantity one plus epsilon; and the rate of tau with respect to t equals one over the quantity one plus epsilon.

An observer inside the fabric always measures the same signal speed, because the clock slowdown exactly compensates the wave slowdown, the mechanical origin of the invariance of the speed of light.

From Hooke's Law to the Einstein-Hilbert action. In physics, a system's dynamics are fully encoded in its action, script-S, an integral whose extremization yields the equations of motion. Two theories with the same action have the same physics. General Relativity follows from the Einstein-Hilbert action; so if the fabric's elastic energy, computed purely from Hooke's Law, produces an action of the same form, the analogy is established.

The derivation proceeds in three steps. First, in the weak-field regime the spacetime metric is written g-sub-myu-nyu equals eta-sub-myu-nyu plus two times epsilon-sub-myu-nyu, flat spacetime plus small deviations, and the deviations are identified physically: the spatial components are the fabric's strain, and the time-time component equals the volumetric strain, epsilon-naught-naught equals epsilon, from the lapse-rate postulate. Second, the elastic energy per unit volume is averaged across the plate's thickness and split into a bending part, U-sub-B, strains that reverse sign across the mid-surface, and a membrane part, U-sub-M, uniform in-plane stretching. Using thin-plate theory, the bending energy comes out proportional to the intrinsic curvature, R-three-D, of the mid-surface:

U-sub-B equals minus Y L squared over forty-eight, times R-three-D.

This holds provided Poisson's ratio is fixed at nyu equals one, the unique choice that removes an unphysical dependence on how the fabric sits in the reference space, its extrinsic curvature. Third, the membrane energy is shown to vanish for nearly static deformations. The fabric's Lagrangian density is then minus U-sub-B times the square root of the absolute value of g, which, using the linearized relation between spatial curvature R-three-D and the four-dimensional Ricci scalar R, takes exactly the Einstein-Hilbert form:

script-S-sub-F equals Y L squared over forty-eight, times the integral of R times the square root of the absolute value of g, d to the four x. Compare this with script-S-sub-E-H, equal to one over two kappa, times that same integral, the integral of R times the square root of the absolute value of g, d to the four x.

Identifying the two coefficients, Y L squared over forty-eight equals one over two kappa, calibrates the model: the fabric's stiffness is fixed by Newton's constant.

What the analogy buys: physical interpretations. Gravitational potential is volumetric strain. Combining the linearized-gravity relation between capital fai and g-naught-naught with the lapse-rate postulate gives capital fai over c squared equals minus epsilon: where space is stretched, potential is negative and clocks run slow.

The cosmic fabric is literally a fabric. Known materials with nyu equals one have fibrous, woven substructure, recently discovered compressive dilatant and stretch densifying materials are made of entangled wires and twisted threads. The model thus hints that space has a weave-like substructure.

Only transverse waves, and why light speed is the maximum. With nyu equals one, the shear modulus is myu equals Y over four, but the pressure-wave modulus is exactly zero: the fabric supports no longitudinal, that is pressure, waves, only transverse, that is shear, waves. In ordinary materials pressure waves outrun shear waves; here they are absent altogether, making the shear speed, the speed of light, the fastest possible signal. This matches observation: all waves known to propagate in vacuum, light and gravitational waves, are transverse.

Gravitational waves are shear waves. Assuming the Lagrangian extends to fast-changing fields, shear disturbances in the fabric obey the wave equation, the second partial derivative of epsilon-sub-i-j with respect to time equals c squared times the Laplacian of epsilon-sub-i-j, identical in form to the solid-mechanics shear-wave equation with c squared equal to myu over rho-sub-F. Working out which strain components can oscillate independently leaves exactly two, the two polarizations of gravitational waves, here derived by solid-mechanics reasoning.

Stiffness and density of free space. Taking L comparable to l-sub-p gives an enormous elastic modulus, Y comparable to four point four times ten to the one hundred thirteen newtons per square meter, and a fabric density, rho-sub-F equal to myu over c squared, comparable to one point three times ten to the ninety-six kilograms per cubic meter, agreeing to order of magnitude with Quantum Field Theory's estimate of the zero-point energy density of the vacuum.

Chapter Four. Recovering relativity.

The fabric ostensibly defines a hyperplane of absolute simultaneity, a preferred rest frame. Doesn't that contradict relativity? This chapter shows that it does not: at the continuum length scale, the preferred frame is undetectable, and both postulates of Special Relativity, first, that physical laws are the same in all inertial frames, and second, that the speed of light is invariant, are deduced from the fabric's postulates rather than assumed.

Invariance of the speed of signals. Stationary observers at different locations: signals need not propagate at uniform speed relative to the reference space. But by the lapse-rate postulate, a clock located where signals travel slower ticks proportionally slower. Measuring the local signal speed with the local clock, the two effects cancel exactly: every stationary observer measures the same value, identified with the speed of light, c.

A moving observer. Consider an observer moving with velocity v through the fabric, timing a light signal's round trip across a fixed distance transverse to the motion. From the fabric frame, the signal traverses a diagonal path, by the Pythagorean theorem, so its effective transverse speed is the square root of the quantity c squared minus v squared. By the lapse-rate postulate, the moving observer's clocks slow by:

the rate of tau-prime with respect to tau equals the square root of the quantity one minus beta squared, where beta is defined as v over c.

This is time dilation, derived mechanically.

Lengths along the motion. Timing a round-trip signal along a rod carried with the motion, with travel times d-l over the quantity c plus v, and d-l over the quantity c minus v, and applying the time-dilation result forces a choice: either the moving observer finds light speed different along the motion than across it, an anisotropy that the Michelson-Morley experiment ruled out, or rods aligned with the motion change length, with the comoving observer's measured length, d-l-prime, related to the fabric-frame length by d-l-prime equals d-l over the square root of the quantity one minus beta squared. This is the familiar length contraction, seen from the fabric's side: a moving rod occupies less fabric-frame length than its rest measurement. The combined effects of time dilation and length change make the moving observer measure exactly c in every direction. Lorentz reached the same conclusion for light; here it holds for all signals, because the fabric mediates all matter-matter interactions, which is why not just electromagnetic clocks but every physical process is affected.

The Lorentz transformations, geometrically. With invariant signal speed established, the coordinate transformations between a stationary and a moving observer follow from symmetry alone. On a spacetime diagram with time scaled by c, a photon's path bisects the stationary observer's axes; by invariance it must also bisect the moving observer's axes, which tilt inward symmetrically, with slopes beta and one over beta. Translational symmetry of the fabric, valid at the continuum scale, forces the transformation to be linear, and requiring that the inverse transformation has the same form with v replaced by minus v pins down the coefficients: x-prime equals gamma times the quantity x minus beta times c-tau; and c-tau-prime equals gamma times the quantity c-tau minus beta times x; where gamma equals one over the square root of the quantity one minus beta squared. These are the Lorentz transformations.

Composing two Lorentz transformations yields another, with the relativistic velocity-addition law, so any two inertial frames, not just moving versus rest, transform between each other identically. No experiment at the continuum scale can single out the fabric's rest frame: the Principle of Relativity is recovered.

When the rest frame becomes detectable. The derivation relied on translational symmetry, which holds only at the continuum scale. Where symmetry breaks, the rest frame becomes physical. At the cosmic scale it already has: for any point in space there is exactly one frame in which the redshift of the cosmic microwave background is isotropic. From Earth, the cosmic microwave background shows a dipole indicating the Solar System moves at about six hundred kilometers per second toward Centaurus. Local physics stays frame-independent, but cosmic-scale observations do distinguish states of motion. At sub-continuum scales, the fabric's woven substructure and thickness break the symmetry too, relevant, the dissertation argues, to any future quantum theory of gravity, where solid mechanics' techniques for bridging length scales within materials could be leveraged.

In summary, at the continuum scale the Cosmic Fabric model stands to Lorentz aether theory as General Relativity stands to Special Relativity, a generalization to curved, deforming space, while additionally carrying length-scale parameters that the purely continuum theories lack.

Chapter Five. The spacetime metric of a spherical mass.

Chapters Three and Four treated the fabric outside matter. This chapter works out the matter-fabric interaction for the simplest case, a static, spherically symmetric body, a ball of matter, and derives the resulting spacetime geometry from mechanics alone. Two results follow: the metric matches the Schwarzschild solution of General Relativity, and, unexpectedly, the model requires matter to be made of discrete particles.

Setting up the deformation. A metric is the rule for computing real distances, and times, from coordinate differences; deriving it is equivalent to specifying the deformation completely. By symmetry, the deformation has two ingredients: an in-plane radial stretch, r equals the quantity one plus e, times r-bar, meaning material initially at radius r-bar moves to radius r, and a transverse displacement, w as a function of r, into the fourth dimension.

The figure shows the profile of a spherically symmetric deformation as a radial displacement, r as a function of r-bar, an in-plane stretching, followed by a transverse displacement, w as a function of r, into the fourth spatial dimension. The caption reads: The deformation is a radial displacement, r as a function of r-bar, in-plane stretching, followed by a transverse displacement, w as a function of r, into the fourth spatial dimension. Here r-bar is distance from the origin prior to deformation.

The governing equations come from the Principle of Least Action applied to the total energy, bending energy U-sub-B, membrane energy U-sub-M, and the body's relativistic energy density, c squared times rho. First, the bending energy must balance the matter energy, U-sub-B plus c squared rho equals zero, which in geometric terms fixes the spatial curvature inside the body, R-three-D equals six over r-naught squared. Second, the inclusion postulate gives the Laplacian of epsilon equals minus three-halves over r-naught squared. Third, the total membrane energy must vanish, taken up below. Here mass is described geometrically: r-sub-s, defined as two G M over c squared, is the body's Schwarzschild radius, its mass expressed as a length; r-sub-g is its actual radius; and r-naught, defined as the square root of r-sub-g cubed over r-sub-s, encodes its density.

Solving these ordinary differential equations, with regularity at the center and continuity at the surface, yields closed forms for w, epsilon, and e. Outside the body, the transverse displacement is w-sub-ext equals two times the square root of r-sub-s times the quantity r minus r-sub-s, and the strains fall off as epsilon-sub-ext equals r-sub-s over two r-bar.

Recovering the Schwarzschild metric. Assembling the strains into the line element, the squared spacetime distance d-s-squared, gives, outside the body:

d-s-squared-sub-ext equals minus the quantity one minus r-sub-s over r-bar, times c squared d-t squared, plus the quantity one plus r-sub-s over r-bar minus two r-sub-s r-sub-g squared over five r-bar cubed, times d-r-bar squared, plus the quantity one plus r-sub-s r-sub-g squared over five r-bar cubed, times r-bar squared, times d-omega squared.

For comparison, the linearized Schwarzschild metric of General Relativity is:

d-s-squared-sub-ext equals minus the quantity one minus r-sub-s over r, times c squared d-t squared, plus the quantity one plus r-sub-s over r, times d-r squared, plus r squared d-omega squared.

The time coefficients are identical, and both metrics produce the same intrinsic spatial curvature everywhere. The spatial coefficients differ only because the fabric solution tracks the in-plane stretch, e as a function of r-bar, which General Relativity regards as physically meaningless coordinate relabeling. Substituting r equals the quantity one plus e, times r-bar, into the fabric's exterior line element above recovers the linearized Schwarzschild metric above exactly: the two metrics are equivalent up to a coordinate rescaling. This validates the model's postulates. The fabric version carries extra physical content, though: it is simultaneously the deformation tensor of a solid, and it shows that the fractional volumetric stretch of space always equals the fractional slowdown of time.

The shape the fabric bends into is also recognizable. In 1916 Ludwig Flamm showed that the spatial part of the Schwarzschild solution can be visualized as a paraboloid of revolution, exterior, capped by a spherical dome, interior, in four-dimensional space, long used as a teaching picture. Here, Flamm's paraboloid appears as an actual material body: the computed w as a function of r is exactly Flamm's profile.

Why matter must be discrete. The remaining constraint, vanishing membrane energy, has a surprising consequence. Computing the total membrane energy, W-sub-M, for a perfectly continuous spherical body gives a negative quantity whose magnitude saturates at:

the maximum of the absolute value of W-sub-M equals twenty-seven c to the fourth power, times r-sub-s squared, times r-sub-g, over thirty-five G L squared.

This grows as the square of the mass, since it scales as r-sub-s squared, while the body's own relativistic energy grows only linearly, proportional to r-sub-s. For the fabric's response not to overwhelm the energy of the body that caused it, one must require r-sub-s times r-sub-g to be at most on the order of thirty-five over fifty-four, times L squared, an upper bound on how massive a continuous body can be, set by the fabric's thickness, L. Proposing L equal to the square root of two h G over c cubed, where h is Planck's constant, which comes to five point seven times ten to the minus thirty-five meters, the energy scale where a body's Schwarzschild radius equals its de Broglie wavelength, the maximum continuous mass works out to about two point five times ten to the minus eight kilograms, a Planck-scale speck. Anything more massive must be composed of discrete particles.

Checking real particles against the bound, fifty-four r-sub-s r-sub-g over thirty-five L squared, at most about one: the electron scores about zero point zero zero two, comfortably within; the proton about one point zero; and the neutron about one point three, essentially saturating it. That the proton and neutron slightly exceed the bound is consistent with the fact that they, unlike the electron, have substructure, namely quarks. Moreover, if individual particles prescribe asymmetric strains, longitudinal or oblate rather than perfectly spherical, plausible given their wave nature, the membrane energy vanishes identically, while the body still appears continuous and spherically symmetric at large scales.

The significance: General Relativity, a strictly continuum theory, cannot make statements about the granularity of matter. The Cosmic Fabric model, carrying a built-in length-scale parameter, L, predicts discreteness, and the prediction agrees with the known constitution of matter.

Chapter Six. The dark matter effect.

This chapter presents the dissertation's proposed alternative to dark matter: the Inherent Structure Hypothesis, the gravitational anomalies attributed to dark matter are manifestations of the inherent curvature of space, curvature that exists apart from matter. In fabric terms: the relaxed, unstrained shape of the cosmic fabric need not be flat. In General Relativity terms: the field equations are modified so that where the stress-energy tensor vanishes, curvature does not vanish but equals the inherent curvature. Because the Inherent Structure Hypothesis invokes no new dynamics, only a different initial geometry, it escapes the criticism, based on the gravitational-wave event designated G W one seven zero eight one seven, that felled several dark-matter-emulator theories: photons and gravitational waves follow the same geodesics here.

How inherent curvature amplifies gravity. Equivalence with dark matter halos, and where it breaks. Can inherent curvature mimic any dark matter distribution? Yes: given enclosed visible mass, M as a function of r-bar, and hypothetical dark mass, M-sub-dark-matter as a function of r-bar, the equivalent curvature profile is l-bar equals r-bar times the square root of the quantity one plus M-sub-dark-matter over M, which is geometrically consistent for all realistic distributions, since dark matter is always observed alongside visible matter, with the dark fraction growing outward.

The converse fails, and that is where the hypothesis becomes falsifiable. For a curvature profile to be interpretable as physically admissible dark matter, that is, non-negative density, the profile must satisfy the condition that the second derivative of l-bar with respect to r-bar is greater than or equal to zero, the slope must keep growing. But unless the inherent curvature extends over the whole cosmos, the profile must eventually flatten out and rejoin the global geometry: beyond some critical radius, r-sub-c, the concavity reverses, and no dark matter distribution can reproduce the effect. This yields three testable predictions distinguishing the Inherent Structure Hypothesis from dark matter. First, far beyond r-sub-c, the dark matter effect reverses and disappears, a distant orbiting test particle behaves as if no halo were enclosed. Second, gravitational systems exhibiting the effect will show a relatively pronounced edge near r-sub-c. Third, at the cosmic scale, dark matter contributes nothing net to the universe's contents, the famous twenty-seven percent figure is model-dependent, not a direct observation.

Implications for cosmology. Like MOND, the Inherent Structure Hypothesis is incompatible with the standard Lambda cold-dark-matter model, which requires non-baryonic dark matter and presupposes the Cosmological Principle, that there is no structure above one hundred megaparsecs. The Inherent Structure Hypothesis takes the opposite premise, that structure is fundamental at every length scale. The galaxy data show inherent curvature correlated with matter, yet inherent curvature is by definition uncaused by matter; the suggested resolution is reversed causality: the inherent structure of space is what caused matter to organize into galaxies and clusters in the first place, potentially also explaining the universe's large-scale walls and filaments, which are too extended to be gravitationally bound. Inherent structure could serve future cosmological models as the seed for structure formation, the role the Lambda cold-dark-matter model assigns to cold dark matter.
